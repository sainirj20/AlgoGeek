SYSTEM DESIGN

Designing a Hyperlocal Weather App (Dark-Sky-Style Nowcasting)

A complete, interview-ready design walkthrough for a hyperlocal, minute-by-minute precipitation-nowcasting product in the mold of the original Dark Sky: requirements and capacity estimation, the getNowcast / subscribeToRainAlerts API and grid-cell data model, radar/satellite ingestion and transformer-based nowcasting architecture, hyperlocal grid serving, push-notification fan-out via a cell-to-subscriber reverse index, batch-training vs. real-time-serving trade-offs, partitioning, replication, and a bank of follow-up interview questions.

Problem Statement

Design a hyperlocal weather app in the mold of the real, historically notable Dark Sky product. Dark Sky was a predictive weather app and API, acquired by Apple in March 2020 and shut down as a standalone product in January 2023, built around a proprietary machine-learning model that could tell a user, with uncanny accuracy, when rain would start or stop — often down to the exact minute — at their precise location. That is a fundamentally different problem from a generic weather app: instead of a city-level forecast refreshed a handful of times a day from sparse, widely-spaced weather-station readings, the goal here is a per-minute precipitation timeline for the next one to two hours, computed for the user's exact latitude/longitude from live radar reflectivity and satellite imagery rather than station interpolation.

Apple absorbed Dark Sky's approach into its own Weather app and WeatherKit API, which today offers hyperlocal forecasts down to roughly 0.001 degrees of latitude/longitude (block-level precision), including a "Next Hour" precipitation forecast at about 15-minute steps, high-resolution radar, and proactive rain notifications. Former Dark Sky team members have since built spiritual successors (e.g. Acme Weather) that lean on transformer-based neural network architectures — the same broad model family behind large language models — applied to sequences of radar/satellite frames to generate these short-horizon nowcasts. This design walkthrough builds that class of product: minute-level nowcasting as the headline feature, with a traditional multi-day forecast as a secondary, coarser-grained add-on.

The engineering philosophy behind this problem

Dark Sky's founding insight was that a small, focused team could out-execute much larger, generalized weather platforms specifically by concentrating all engineering effort on one narrow, hard problem — hyperlocal, minute-level precipitation timing — rather than spreading across the dozens of features a full weather platform typically ships. That focus is exactly what an interviewer is probing for here: it's tempting to sketch "a generic weather API with a cache in front of it," but the interesting, defensible design decisions in this problem are all about how you get a per-minute, per-exact-location precipitation answer to be fast, fresh, and cheap to serve to millions of overlapping users at once — not about caching a slow-changing city forecast.

Clarifying Questions to Ask the Interviewer

This problem hinges entirely on data availability and freshness requirements that a generic "weather API" design would never need to ask about. Spend the first few minutes narrowing scope before drawing any boxes.

  1. What raw data sources are available — ground radar network, geostationary satellite imagery, or both — and at what native refresh cadence (e.g. every 2 minutes, every 5–10 minutes)?
  2. How far out does the "nowcast" need to stay accurate — strictly the next 60–120 minutes, or do we also need to blend into a longer hourly/multi-day forecast?
  3. What push-notification latency is acceptable between "the model detects an upcoming rain onset" and "the user's phone buzzes"?
  4. Do we need global coverage from day one, or can we assume a rollout that's radar-dense in some regions (e.g. US, Western Europe) and sparse or absent elsewhere?
  5. What's the expected concurrent user base, and how peaky is it — do we expect correlated spikes (an entire metro checking the app right before a storm)?
  6. Do users need multiple saved locations (home, work, a trip destination), or just their current GPS position?
  7. Is a live radar map overlay in scope, or only the numeric per-minute precipitation timeline?
  8. What's the acceptable staleness bound before we fall back from a hyperlocal nowcast to a coarser traditional forecast?
  9. Do we need historical "how much did it rain here yesterday" lookups, or is this purely forward-looking?
  10. Is there a public/third-party API surface (like Dark Sky's own developer API), or is this strictly a first-party consumer app?
Interview tip
State reasonable defaults out loud and keep moving: "I'll assume both radar and satellite feeds refresh every ~2 minutes, the nowcast horizon is 1–2 hours at minute granularity, coverage is dense in some regions and sparse in others, and tens of millions of daily active users are polling their forecast every few minutes while the app is open." This shows judgment, not indecision, and it's exactly the kind of assumption Dark Sky itself had to make about its own radar/satellite data providers.

Requirements and Goals

Functional Requirements

  1. Given a user's exact latitude/longitude, return a minute-level precipitation nowcast covering the next 1–2 hours.
  2. Provide a secondary, coarser-grained hourly and multi-day forecast for the same location, for users planning further ahead.
  3. Proactively push a notification to a user when precipitation is about to start or stop near a location they've subscribed to.
  4. (Extended) Let a user view a live radar/satellite map overlay in addition to the numeric nowcast.
  5. (Extended) Support multiple saved locations per user (home, work, a trip destination).
  6. (Extended) Support a historical lookback ("how much did it rain here in the last 24 hours") for the user's location.

Non-Functional Requirements

  1. Very low-latency reads: the nowcast is checked constantly — on app open, on pull-to-refresh, and passively via widgets — so getNowcast must return in well under 100–200 ms.
  2. Freshness is critical: a nowcast is worthless if it's stale by more than a few minutes — unlike a city-level forecast that's tolerably stale for hours, a "rain starts in 8 minutes" claim that's based on 20-minute-old radar is actively misleading.
  3. Massive read fan-out efficiency: millions of users can be querying overlapping map regions simultaneously (an entire metro checking the app before the same storm), so the system must serve that fan-out from shared, precomputed state rather than redoing work per request.
  4. High availability on the read path — users check weather precisely when conditions are volatile, which is the worst possible time for the service to be down.
  5. Independent horizontal scalability of the ingestion/inference pipeline versus the read-serving tier, since the two have very different load profiles and failure modes.
  6. Graceful degradation: a region with no fresh radar-based nowcast should fall back to a coarser traditional forecast rather than returning an error.

Extended / Out-of-Scope

This design goes deep on radar/satellite ingestion, the nowcasting model's serving path, hyperlocal grid-cell caching, and alert fan-out. We explicitly call out — but don't build — long-range multi-week forecasting and the full internals of numerical-weather-prediction (NWP) climate modeling; those are noted as an external, upstream dependency (feeding the coarse secondary forecast) rather than designed here.

Capacity Estimation and Constraints

This system has an unusual shape compared to most read-heavy services: the read side is enormous but each read is a tiny lookup, while the write/ingest side is comparatively low in request-rate but each write is a large binary imagery frame. Getting this asymmetry right is what drives the entire caching/precompute strategy below.

Read Traffic — Nowcast Lookups

Assumption 50 million daily active users (tens of millions, per scope), each opening the app ≈3×/day
Each app open triggers ≈2 nowcast reads (initial load + one live refresh while watching the "rain in N minutes" countdown) → 6 reads/user/day
Reads/day = 50,000,000 × 6 = 300,000,000
Average read QPS = 300,000,000 ÷ 86,400 ≈ 3,472 → round to ~3,500 reads/sec
A widespread severe-weather event drives correlated checking across an entire metro → design for ~6× peak ≈ 21,000 peak read QPS

Write / Ingest Traffic — Raw Radar & Satellite Frames

Assumption ≈400 covered radar/satellite regions worldwide, each producing a new raw imagery frame every ~2 minutes (typical operational scan cadence)
Ingest events/sec = 400 ÷ 120s ≈ 3.3 frames/sec — a very low request rate compared to reads
Each raw frame (reflectivity + IR channels) ≈ 8 MB → ingest bandwidth ≈ 3.3 × 8 MB ≈ ~26 MB/sec sustained, bursty at scan boundaries

The read path is ~1,000× higher in request rate but each request is a few hundred bytes of JSON; the write path is comparatively rare but each event is tens of thousands of times larger. That asymmetry is exactly why this can't be one shared write-through pipeline: raw imagery needs a high-throughput binary ingestion/object-store path, while the read side needs a low-latency small-object cache that can absorb a massive, overlapping fan-out of tiny lookups — which in turn is only possible if the expensive part (model inference) has already happened before any user request arrives.

Grid-Cell Forecast Cache Sizing

Derivation At ~0.001° resolution (block-level): 1 cell ≈ 111m (lat) × 85m (long) ≈ 9,400 m² ≈ 0.0036 sq mi
For one radar-covered metro region of ~19,300 sq mi: cells/region ≈ 19,300 ÷ 0.0036 ≈ 5.3 million raw cells
Across ~400 covered regions: raw grid ≈ 400 × 5.3M ≈ 2.1 billion candidate cells
Sparsity caveat — only materialize what's populated or in-coverage
Just as a ride-hailing app's geo-tile scheme doesn't allocate a physical shard for every ocean tile on Earth, most of that 2.1-billion-cell raw grid is unpopulated land, ocean, or simply outside any radar's effective range and needs no materialized forecast at all. Restricting precomputation to cells that are both populated and inside active radar/satellite coverage brings the real number down to roughly ~150 million materialized cells. At ≈300 bytes/cell (a 120-entry per-minute timeline plus metadata), that's ≈45 GB — comfortably held in a sharded in-memory key-value cluster with room for replicas.

High-Level Estimate Summary

MetricValue
Daily active users~50 million

The implied strategy: never run model inference synchronously inside a user's request. Run inference once per cycle per covered region, write the results into a hyperlocal grid-cell cache, and serve every one of the ~3,500–21,000 reads/sec entirely out of that cache. The read tier's job becomes "map a coordinate to a cell_id and do a KV lookup" — nothing more.

System / API Design

Two APIs anchor this design: one for pulling a hyperlocal nowcast on demand, and one for subscribing a location to proactive rain alerts.

getNowcast(lat, long)

GET /api/v1/nowcast?lat={lat}&long={long}

Response 200 OK:
{
  "location": { "lat": 37.7749, "long": -122.4194 },
  "cell_id": "wthr_37774_-122419",
  "generated_at": "2026-07-19T14:32:00Z",
  "minute_forecast": [
    { "minute_offset": 0,  "precip_probability": 0.05, "precip_type": "none" },
    { "minute_offset": 1,  "precip_probability": 0.07, "precip_type": "none" },

    { "minute_offset": 9,  "precip_probability": 0.84, "precip_type": "rain" }
    /* ... up to minute_offset 119 ... */
  ],
  "hourly_forecast": [ /* coarser, secondary; sourced from the traditional NWP feed, see Feature-Specific Logic */ ],
  "source": "nowcasting_model"

Errors: 400 invalid coordinates · 404 no coverage for this location · 429 rate-limited

subscribeToRainAlerts(user_id, lat, long)

POST /api/v1/alerts/subscribe

Request body:
{
  "user_id": string,               // required
  "lat": double,                   // required
  "long": double,                  // required
  "notify_on": ["rain_start", "rain_stop"]  // optional, default both
}

Response 201 Created:
{ "subscription_id": "sub_88213", "cell_id": "wthr_37774_-122419", "status": "active" }

Errors: 400 invalid coordinates · 401 unauthenticated · 429 rate-limited
Interview tip
Notice that both APIs resolve a raw (lat, long) into a cell_id immediately. That single normalization step is what lets the read path avoid per-request model inference and lets the alert path avoid scanning every individual subscription — both of the system's hardest scaling problems collapse into "look something up by cell_id" once this mapping exists.

Data Model and Database Schema

Two records anchor the schema: the precomputed per-cell forecast (the output of the nowcasting model, read on every getNowcast call) and a user's alert subscription (read whenever the model detects a precipitation change in a cell).

grid_cell_forecast is written only by the Nowcasting Model Service (once per inference cycle) and read at very high QPS — a textbook fit for a sharded, replicated key-value store keyed by cell_id. alert_subscription is written rarely (on subscribe/unsubscribe) and must support an efficient "give me every subscription for cell X" query — which is exactly why cell_id carries a secondary index rather than being a free-text coordinate pair.

High-Level Design

Raw radar and satellite feeds are continuously pulled by a Radar/Satellite Ingestion Service, which normalizes frames and hands sequences of them to a Nowcasting Model Service. That model runs inference once per cycle and writes results into a Grid-Cell Forecast Cache — the only thing the client-facing Read Tier ever touches. A Push Notification Engine watches the cache for precipitation-onset/cessation events and fans out to subscribers via a cell-to-subscriber reverse index. A Traditional NWP Feed supplies the coarser secondary hourly/multi-day forecast merged into the same API response.

Figure 1. High-level architecture: radar/satellite sources feed the Ingestion Service, which hands frame sequences to the Nowcasting Model Service; inference output is written to the Grid-Cell Forecast Cache, the only thing the Read Tier touches on the request path. The Push Notification Engine watches the cache and fans out via the cell-to-subscriber reverse index, while the Traditional NWP Feed supplies the coarser secondary forecast.

Key Design Principles Reflected in the Diagram

  • Inference happens once per cycle, not once per request: the Nowcasting Model Service never sits on the getNowcast request path — it only ever writes to the cache.
  • The read tier is deliberately dumb: its only job is coordinate-to-cell resolution and a cache lookup, which is what lets it absorb tens of thousands of QPS cheaply.
  • Ingestion and serving scale independently — a burst of new radar frames during a storm never directly load-spikes the read tier, and a read traffic spike never slows down ingestion.
  • Everything not strictly needed to answer "what's the nowcast right now" (alerting, the coarse NWP blend) is a parallel branch off the cache, not a dependency of the core read path.

Low-Level Design

The core request flow deliberately contains no model inference at all — that's the entire point of precomputing forecasts per grid cell ahead of demand.

Core Nowcast Request Flow

Figure 2. Core nowcast request flow: the client's exact lat/long is mapped to a hyperlocal grid cell, the precomputed forecast for that cell is read straight from cache, and the response is returned — the Nowcasting Model Service sits entirely off this path, populating the cache asynchronously on its own inference cycle.
  1. The client requests a forecast for its exact latitude/longitude.
  2. The Grid-Cell Resolver maps that coordinate into the containing hyperlocal grid cell (a deterministic bucketing function on lat/long at ~0.001° resolution — no database round-trip needed).
  3. The precomputed forecast for that cell is read straight from the Grid-Cell Forecast Cache, with absolutely no per-request model inference.
  4. The response is a per-minute precipitation timeline for the next 1–2 hours, plus the coarser secondary hourly/multi-day forecast merged in from the traditional NWP feed.

The only way this request flow stays cheap at tens of thousands of QPS is that step 3 is a pure cache read. If the mapping in step 2 ever fell through to invoking the model directly, read latency and cost would scale with request volume instead of with covered area — exactly the trap a generic "weather API with caching" design falls into when it treats the model as just another slow backend to cache around, rather than decoupling generation from serving entirely.

Deep Dive — Nowcasting Model: Radar/Satellite Ingestion and Short-Horizon Precipitation Prediction

Raw radar reflectivity and satellite imagery are ingested on a continuous cadence (assumed ~2 minutes per covered region above). Each new frame is appended to a rolling sequence buffer per region; the Nowcasting Model Service consumes that sequence — not a single snapshot — because precipitation motion and evolution can only be inferred from how the imagery has changed over the last several frames.

Dark Sky's original model was a proprietary, purpose-built system; the broad successor approach used across the industry today (and in spiritual-successor products built by former Dark Sky engineers) treats this as a sequence-to-sequence prediction problem: a transformer-based neural network — the same broad architecture family behind large language models, here applied to sequences of radar/satellite frames instead of sequences of tokens — is trained to predict how precipitation fields will move and evolve over the next one to two hours. This is fundamentally more granular, in both space and time, than traditional numerical weather prediction (NWP), which solves physical fluid-dynamics equations on a coarser grid and a much coarser output cadence (hourly, at best).

Nowcasting model (this design)Traditional NWP
InputSequences of live radar/satellite imageryAtmospheric physics equations + sparse station/balloon/satellite observations
Output granularityPer-minute, per ~0.001° grid cellPer-hour (or coarser), per much larger grid cell
HorizonNext 1–2 hours (short-horizon "nowcast")Hours to days ahead
Compute patternFrequent, low-latency inference on live dataPeriodic, heavy batch simulation runs
Best use in this designHeadline hyperlocal nowcast featureSecondary coarse hourly/multi-day forecast (see Feature-Specific Logic)
Why not just use NWP everywhere?
NWP is excellent at multi-day forecasting but its coarse spatial/temporal grid and multi-hour compute cycle make it structurally unable to answer "will it rain on my block in the next 15 minutes" — that gap is precisely the product opportunity Dark Sky identified and built its entire company around.

Deep Dive — Hyperlocal Grid Resolution and Serving Architecture

The coverage area is divided into a very fine grid — real hyperlocal weather products resolve down to roughly 0.001 degrees of latitude/longitude, i.e. block-level, not just city-level. Forecasts are precomputed per grid cell on every model-inference cycle rather than computed per user request, so that millions of overlapping user queries in the same city all hit the exact same, already-computed cache entry instead of triggering redundant inference.

Figure 3. Hyperlocal grid serving: the highlighted cell shows four overlapping user queries all resolving to the exact same precomputed cache entry — one inference result serves an unbounded number of reads, which is what makes millions of simultaneous overlapping queries in one city cheap to serve.

This is the single most important serving-side decision in the whole design: precomputing per grid cell — rather than per user, per session, or per request — turns an otherwise unbounded read fan-out problem (millions of users, each theoretically needing "their own" forecast) into a bounded one (however many grid cells the coverage area contains, refreshed on a fixed cadence).

Deep Dive — Push Notification Engine for Precipitation Alerts

Users subscribe a lat/long to rain alerts via subscribeToRainAlerts. When the nowcasting model detects an upcoming precipitation onset or cessation for a grid cell, the system must notify every subscription whose coordinates fall in that cell — without scanning every individual subscription in the system one at a time on every model update.

Figure 4. Push notification fan-out: a detected precipitation change in a grid cell triggers a single reverse-index lookup (cell_id → subscriber list), followed by a parallel fan-out to every affected device — never a full scan of every subscription in the system.

The reverse index (cell_id → [subscription_id, ...]) is the key data structure: it's built by indexing the alert_subscription table on the same cell_id the forecast cache already uses, so a rain-onset event for a cell resolves directly to "which subscriptions care about this" in one lookup, similar in spirit to a geospatial index but deliberately keyed by the identical grid used for forecasting — so the two systems (forecasting and alerting) always agree on which physical area a given cell covers.

Deep Dive — Data Pipeline: Batch Model Training vs. Real-Time Inference Serving

The nowcasting model itself is trained offline in batch against historical radar/satellite imagery paired with ground-truth precipitation outcomes — a slow, resource-heavy process that may run for hours on accumulated historical data. The trained model is then served in a low-latency real-time inference path against live incoming imagery, on the ~2-minute cycle described above. These two paths must be fully decoupled: a slow retrain should never block or slow down live nowcast updates.

Batch TrainingReal-Time Inference Serving
CadencePeriodic (e.g. weekly/monthly retrains)Continuous, every ~2 minutes
InputLarge historical archive of radar/satellite/ground-truth dataThe latest live frame sequence per region
Compute profileHeavy, long-running, can tolerate hoursMust complete well within one inference cycle
OutputA new model version (weights)Fresh grid-cell forecasts written to the cache
Failure impactA slow/failed retrain delays only the next model versionA slow/failed inference cycle directly risks nowcast staleness

The freshness/staleness trade-off runs in both directions: retraining more often can improve the model's accuracy over time as it learns from more recent weather patterns, but retraining has no effect on how fresh any single nowcast is — that's governed entirely by how often inference re-runs against new imagery. Conversely, running inference more frequently than new imagery actually arrives buys nothing; the inference cadence should track the ingestion cadence, not the other way around. A new model version is rolled out to the serving path independently (e.g. shadow-scored against the current version before promotion), so a bad retrain never directly reaches users without a validation gate.

Data Partitioning and Sharding

Both raw-imagery ingestion and the grid-cell forecast cache are partitioned geographically, so a region's ingest/inference/serving load is handled by servers physically and logically close to that region's radar coverage.

  • Ingestion partitioning: each covered radar/satellite region is assigned to an ingestion worker pool located near that region's data source, minimizing cross-continent transfer of large raw imagery frames before any processing happens.
  • Cache partitioning: the grid-cell cache is sharded by cell_id prefix (which itself encodes a geographic bucket), so reads for a given metro area are served by a shard co-located with that metro's users and, ideally, its inference workers — keeping the whole pipeline for one region physically close together.
  • Consistent hashing across cache shards avoids a full reshard whenever coverage expands into a new region, so onboarding a newly radar-covered area doesn't require rebalancing existing shards.

Replication and Fault Tolerance

  • Grid-cell forecast caches are replicated per region so the loss of a single cache node doesn't blackout an entire metro area's nowcasts — a replica promotion takes over within the failure-detection window.
  • If fresh radar-based nowcast data isn't available for a region (ingestion outage, model service degraded, or the region simply has no radar coverage), the read tier gracefully degrades to the coarser traditional hourly/daily forecast rather than returning an error — the source field in the schema (nowcasting_model vs. traditional_nwp_fallback) makes this degradation explicit and observable.
  • The Nowcasting Model Service itself runs as a replicated pool of inference workers per region, so a single worker failure delays — but doesn't halt — that region's next inference cycle.

Feature-Specific Logic

The core "predict precipitation timing down to the minute at an exact point" feature is itself the headline feature-specific logic of this entire system — nearly every architectural decision above (grid-cell precomputation, the reverse index, the decoupled training/serving pipeline) exists in service of making that one feature fast, fresh, and cheap at scale.

The secondary, coarser multi-day forecast is handled with deliberately simpler logic: it's sourced from a traditional numerical-weather-prediction feed rather than the nowcasting model, since multi-day forecasts don't need minute-level precision and NWP is already well-suited to that longer horizon. This keeps the nowcasting model focused on the one hard, narrow problem it's actually good at, echoing Dark Sky's own founding philosophy of concentrating engineering effort on a single, well-scoped capability rather than spreading a single model thin across every weather-data feature.

Security, Privacy and Permissions

  • Location data handling: a user's precise lat/long (both for on-demand lookups and stored alert subscriptions) is sensitive personal data; store subscription coordinates only as precisely as needed (cell-level bucketing is often sufficient for alerting) and apply a clear retention policy — e.g. purge inactive subscriptions and raw request logs containing coordinates after a bounded window.
  • Rate limiting on the public API: without limits, a bad actor could enumerate getNowcast across the entire hyperlocal grid to scrape the full precomputed dataset; enforce a per-API-key or per-device token-bucket quota and return HTTP 429 when exceeded.
  • Alert subscription integrity: require authentication on subscribeToRainAlerts so an attacker can't silently subscribe (or unsubscribe) another user's device.
  • Transport security: TLS everywhere, including on the push-delivery path to device push gateways.

Load Balancing

Nowcast-read traffic is load-balanced separately from the heavier, more bursty radar-ingestion/inference workload, since the two have very different scaling profiles: reads are extremely high-QPS but each unit of work is trivial (a cache lookup), while ingestion/inference is comparatively low-QPS but each unit of work is compute- and bandwidth-heavy. Sharing one load-balancing tier between them would let a burst of incoming radar frames starve read-tier capacity, or vice versa. Start with simple round-robin across the read tier (cheap, drains a failed health-check node automatically); the ingestion/inference tier is better served by work assigned per covered region rather than generic round-robin, since a given inference worker needs the full recent frame history for its region.

Scaling to Multiple Regions

  • Each region runs its own ingestion/inference pipeline aligned to that region's own radar/satellite data sources, since coverage quality and refresh cadence genuinely differ by geography (dense radar networks in some countries, satellite-only or sparse coverage in others).
  • A region with sparse or no radar coverage gracefully falls back to the coarser traditional forecast (see Replication and Fault Tolerance) rather than being denied service entirely — the product is simply less differentiated there until better data sources become available.
  • The client-facing read tier and grid-cell cache are deployed per region (Geo-DNS or anycast routes a user to the nearest one), so nowcast latency stays low regardless of which region a user is physically in.

Monitoring, Logging and Analytics

  • Nowcast freshness/staleness per grid cell is the single most important health metric in this entire system — alert on any cell whose generated_at exceeds the expected inference cycle interval by more than a small margin, since a stale nowcast silently degrades the product's core value proposition without ever returning an error.
  • Alert-delivery latency from the moment the model detects a precipitation onset/cessation event to the moment the push notification is actually delivered to the device — this end-to-end number is what determines whether "rain alerts" feel proactive or feel late.
  • Model accuracy monitoring: continuously compare predicted precipitation timing/probability against observed ground-truth precipitation (from the same radar/satellite feeds, after the fact) to catch model drift before it erodes user trust.

Common Interview Questions

These are organized by theme — use them to rehearse, or expect a real interview to pick several as "and then what?" follow-ups.

Scaling and Performance

A major storm causes millions of users in the same metro to check the app within the same few minutes — walk through what happens.
Every one of those requests resolves to the same small set of grid cells covering that metro, and every one of them is served from the already-precomputed Grid-Cell Forecast Cache — the spike never reaches the Nowcasting Model Service at all, since inference already happened before any of these requests arrived.
How do you avoid running model inference separately for every single user request?
By precomputing a forecast per grid cell on a fixed inference cycle and having the read tier do nothing but a coordinate→cell_id mapping plus a cache lookup — the number of inference runs is bounded by covered area and cycle time, not by read volume.
How would you handle a sudden spike in radar/satellite ingest volume during an active severe-weather outbreak?
Ingestion is partitioned per region and scales independently of the read tier; a spike in one region's frame volume is absorbed by that region's ingestion workers without affecting other regions or read-serving capacity anywhere else.

Correctness and Freshness

How do you define "freshness" for a nowcast, and what happens when a region's data goes stale?
Freshness is measured as the age of a grid cell's generated_at timestamp relative to the expected inference cycle interval. Once a cell's data exceeds an acceptable staleness bound, the read tier marks that response's source as a fallback and serves the coarser traditional forecast instead of a misleadingly precise but outdated nowcast.
Two overlapping model inference cycles finish out of order — could a user ever see an older forecast overwrite a newer one?
Only if cache writes aren't guarded by the forecast's own generated_at/cycle timestamp; a correct implementation writes conditionally ("only overwrite if this result is newer than what's cached"), so an out-of-order write never regresses freshness.
How would you validate that the nowcasting model's predicted "rain starts in 8 minutes" is actually accurate?
Continuously compare each prediction against the same radar/satellite feed's own after-the-fact observed precipitation for that cell and time window, aggregating accuracy metrics (e.g. onset-timing error in minutes) per region and model version — this is the model accuracy monitoring described above.

Product and API Design

How would you support a user with multiple saved locations (home, work, etc.)?
Each saved location is simply its own independent getNowcast lookup and, optionally, its own subscribeToRainAlerts subscription — no change to the core design, since the system already treats every (lat, long) as an independent cell resolution.
How would you let the app show a live radar map overlay in addition to the numeric nowcast?
Serve the same underlying radar/satellite imagery frames the Ingestion Service already normalized — likely via a CDN-backed tile endpoint — as a separate read path from the numeric nowcast API, reusing the ingested data rather than re-deriving it.
Should getNowcast return a coarse fallback if a location has no radar-based nowcast available?
Yes — return the traditional NWP-derived forecast with source: "traditional_nwp_fallback" rather than a 404, so the app always has something reasonable to show, just at lower precision.

Security and Privacy

What privacy protections matter most for storing a user's precise lat/long for alerts?
Minimize retention (store only what's needed to resolve a cell_id and re-verify a subscription), avoid storing raw precise coordinates in long-lived logs, and give users a clear way to delete a subscription, which should also purge it from the cell-to-subscriber reverse index immediately.
How do you stop a competitor from scraping the entire hyperlocal grid via the public API?
Per-API-key/device token-bucket rate limiting on getNowcast, enforced at the gateway layer before requests reach the read tier, plus anomaly detection on unusually broad, systematic coordinate sweeps.
Should alert subscriptions store a raw device push token indefinitely?
No — push tokens rotate and go stale; store them with a freshness check on each notification attempt, and prune subscriptions whose token has been invalid beyond a reasonable grace period.

Reliability and Operations

A single grid-cell cache node fails in the middle of a storm — what's the customer impact?
Replica promotion takes over that shard's cells within the failure-detection window; briefly, affected users may see a slightly older cached forecast or a short-lived fallback to the coarse forecast, but the system doesn't go fully down.
The Nowcasting Model Service goes down entirely for 20 minutes — what happens to users during that window?
The cache still serves the last computed forecasts until they age past the staleness bound, at which point the read tier transitions those cells to the traditional NWP fallback — a strong answer quantifies exactly how many cycles of staleness the product tolerates before that switch happens.
How would you detect that a region's radar feed has silently gone stale (no failure, just no new frames)?
Monitor per-region ingestion frame-arrival cadence directly (not just service health/uptime) and alert when the time since the last received frame for a region exceeds its expected cadence — a "no news" gap is just as dangerous as an explicit error here.

Extensions

How would you extend this design to support historical "how much did it rain here yesterday" queries?
Archive completed grid-cell forecast records (or the raw observed radar data) into a separate historical store keyed by cell_id and time window, decoupled from the live cache that only ever needs to hold the current forecast per cell.
How would you support a completely new metro region that just got radar coverage for the first time?
Onboard it as a new geographic partition: assign ingestion workers near the new radar source, add its cell_id range to the consistent-hashed cache ring, and flip its source from traditional-fallback to nowcasting-model once inference output for that region is validated.
How would you retrain the model to improve accuracy without disrupting live nowcasts?
Train and shadow-score the new model version entirely offline against historical and recent live data without it ever touching the production cache, then promote it as an atomic swap once it's validated — the real-time serving path never blocks on, or is exposed to, an in-progress retrain.

Key Takeaways

Remember
This is not a generic "weather API with a cache" problem — it's a precompute-and-serve problem built entirely around one hard capability: turning continuous radar/satellite imagery into a per-minute, per-exact-location precipitation timeline, in the mold of the real, historically notable Dark Sky product. Every major design decision traces back to that: precompute forecasts per hyperlocal grid cell so millions of overlapping queries share one inference result; keep ingestion/inference decoupled from the read-serving tier since their scaling profiles are completely different; keep batch model training decoupled from real-time inference serving so a slow retrain never risks live freshness; and use the same grid-cell key for both forecasting and alert fan-out so "who cares about this cell" is always a single lookup, never a full scan. Freshness — not raw accuracy alone — is the metric to protect above all others, since a stale hyperlocal nowcast is worse than an honest, coarser fallback forecast.
