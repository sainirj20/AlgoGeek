SYSTEM DESIGN

Marketplace Feature for Facebook

A complete, interview-ready design walkthrough for a peer-to-peer local marketplace feature embedded inside a large existing social network: requirements and capacity estimation, API & data model, high- and low-level design, a deep dive into multimodal (image + text) listing understanding and automatic categorization — conceptually similar to Meta's real Lumos and DeepText systems — geospatial search/ranking, messaging and trust & safety, partitioning, replication, and a bank of follow-up interview questions.

Problem Statement

Design a peer-to-peer local marketplace feature embedded inside a large, existing social network (comparable to Facebook Marketplace). Users list items for sale by uploading photos and writing a title/description; buyers browse and search nearby listings by keyword, category, and location; a buyer can message a seller to ask questions or negotiate before completing the transaction off-platform. Because listings arrive as unstructured photos and free text at very high volume, the platform must automatically understand and categorize each listing at the moment it's posted rather than relying on the seller to manually tag it correctly.

Why does this problem matter in an interview?

It looks like "just another CRUD + search" problem, but the interesting part is what makes it distinctive: this isn't a purpose-built marketplace start-up bolting on a userbase — it's a marketplace feature riding on top of an already-massive social graph and app surface. That reframes almost every decision: search must be local by default (geo-bounded), listings must be understood automatically at a volume no human moderation team could keep up with, and the feature has to interoperate with an existing identity/social layer rather than build its own from scratch.

Why do people use a marketplace feature like this?

  • Sellers reach nearby buyers without leaving the social app they already use daily — no separate account, no separate app install.
  • Buyers get hyper-local, browsable inventory (furniture, cars, electronics, free items) filtered automatically by proximity.
  • Built-in messaging keeps the negotiation and coordination inside the same trusted identity system the rest of the social network already relies on.
  • Automatic categorization means a seller never has to correctly guess which of dozens of categories their item belongs in — the system figures it out from the photo and text.

Clarifying Questions to Ask the Interviewer

Always narrow scope before designing. A strong candidate spends the first few minutes pinning down assumptions rather than silently picking defaults.

  1. Is browsing strictly local/geo-bounded (e.g. within a configurable radius of the buyer), or should we also support broader/national search for some categories?
  2. How much of the automatic categorization and moderation pipeline is in scope to design in depth — just category classification, or also fraud/prohibited-item detection?
  3. Are payments and checkout in scope, or is this purely a listing/discovery/messaging surface with the actual transaction happening off-platform (cash, in person, or a separate payments flow)?
  4. Does messaging need to be real-time (chat-like, with typing indicators/read receipts), or is a simple async inbox with notifications acceptable?
  5. What listing volume are we designing for — tens of thousands, millions, or tens of millions of new listings per day?
  6. Can a seller edit a listing after posting (price drop, mark as sold), and does an edit re-trigger the categorization pipeline?
  7. Do we need to support multiple photos per listing, and does each photo need its own embedding, or just a representative one?
  8. Is there a fixed, small set of top-level categories set by the platform, or can categories evolve/be added over time?
  9. Do we need seller/buyer ratings and reputation, or is that explicitly out of scope for this design?
  10. Single region/country launch, or global from day one across many languages and currencies?
Interview tip
State a reasonable default out loud and move on: "I'll assume a geo-bounded browse radius, payments/shipping off-platform, async messaging with push notifications, tens of millions of new listings per day, and a fixed set of roughly two to three dozen top-level categories assigned automatically at post time." That shows judgment, not indecision — and it mirrors how the real system is actually scoped.

Requirements and Goals

Functional Requirements

  1. Sellers can publish, edit, and delete listings that include one or more photos plus a text title/description and a location.
  2. Buyers can search and browse listings by keyword, category, and location (proximity to the buyer).
  3. Buyers can send an inquiry/message to a seller about a specific listing; sellers are notified and can reply.
  4. Every new listing is automatically classified into a category at the moment it's posted — the seller is not required to manually pick one.
  5. (Extended) Sellers can mark a listing as sold/unavailable, and view basic engagement stats (views, inquiries) for their own listings.

Non-Functional Requirements

  1. Low-latency search/browse: despite a huge and constantly growing listing corpus, keyword + category + location queries must return in well under a second — this is the feature's home-screen experience.
  2. Inline classification without materially delaying publish: the automatic categorization/moderation pipeline runs at post time, on the critical path of "seller hits publish," and must add only a small, bounded amount of latency — not turn publishing into a multi-second wait.
  3. Massive scale: the feature must scale to the size of an existing, huge social-network user base from day one — there's no gradual bootstrapping period the way a standalone marketplace start-up would have.
  4. High availability for both the publish path and the browse/search path, since both are core, frequently used surfaces of the parent app.
  5. Durability of listings and buyer-seller messages — a lost listing or a lost inquiry is a directly visible product failure to a real user.

Extended / Out-of-Scope

This design goes deep on listing creation, multimodal understanding/categorization, geo-filtered search & ranking, and messaging. We explicitly call out — but don't design in depth — in-app payments/escrow and shipping/logistics integration. Both are large sub-systems of their own; in the real product, the transaction itself typically happens off-platform (in person, cash, or a separate checkout flow), and this design treats that as a deliberate boundary rather than an omission.

Capacity Estimation and Constraints

We assume tens of millions of new listings created per day across a huge existing social-network user base, with search/browse read traffic vastly exceeding listing-creation write traffic — a similar read-heavy skew to other content-heavy platforms in this section (e.g. a social feed or a URL-redirect service), since every buyer scrolls and re-queries far more often than any single seller posts.

Listing-Creation Traffic

Assumption 50 million new listings created per day, across a huge existing social-network user base
Listings / second = 50,000,000 ÷ 86,400 ≈ 579 → round to ~580 writes/sec (average)
Peak traffic runs 2–3× average (evenings/weekends) → design for ~1,200–1,700 peak listing-creation QPS

Search / Browse Read Traffic

Assumption Assume a read:write skew on the order of 200:1 — higher than a typical 100:1 read-heavy service, since a single browsing session generates many search/scroll calls per listing ever created
Search QPS = 200 × 580 ≈ 116,000 average search QPS
Peak (2–3×) ≈ 250,000–350,000 search QPS

Inference-Throughput Requirement for the Categorization Pipeline

Every listing triggers inline inference at post time: an embedding pass per photo, one text-embedding pass, and one classification pass. Assume an average of 4 photos per listing.

Derivation Image-embedding inferences/sec = 580 listings/sec × 4 photos ≈ 2,320/sec
Text-embedding inferences/sec = 580/sec (one per listing: title + description)
Autosuggest classification inferences/sec = 580/sec (one per listing)
Total inline inference throughput ≈ 2,320 + 580 + 580 ≈ 3,480 inferences/sec sustained
At peak listing-creation load (2–3×) ≈ 7,000–10,000 inferences/sec of provisioned inference capacity

This inline inference requirement — not the search index size — is usually the more surprising capacity number in this design: it means the categorization pipeline is provisioned as its own independently-scaled, low-latency inference tier, not an afterthought batch job (see Deep Dive sections below).

System / API Design

Three core operations anchor the design: creating a listing, searching/browsing listings, and sending a buyer-to-seller inquiry.

// Seller publishes a new listing. Category is NOT supplied by the caller —
// it is assigned automatically by the autosuggest classification pipeline.
createListing(seller_id, title, description, images[], location)
  -> { listing_id, category, status: "PUBLISHED", created_at }

// Buyer searches/browses listings near them, optionally scoped by keyword and/or category.
searchListings(query, location, radius, category_filter)
  -> { results: [ { listing_id, title, price, distance_km, thumbnail_url, category }, ... ],
       next_page_token }

// Buyer sends a message to the seller about a specific listing.
sendInquiry(buyer_id, listing_id, message)
  -> { inquiry_id, status: "SENT", sent_at }
Interview tip
Notice createListing takes no category parameter at all — that's deliberate. Removing manual category selection from the seller's flow (by classifying automatically at post time) is one of the defining product decisions of this design, and it's worth calling out explicitly rather than letting the interviewer assume it's an oversight.

Two core records anchor the schema: the listing itself, and the buyer-seller inquiry/message thread attached to it.

The listing table's embedding_ref column is the seam between the transactional CRUD store and the multimodal search/ranking index: the listing row is the source of truth for ownership and metadata, while the (much larger, vector-shaped) embedding lives in a store purpose-built for similarity search and geo-filtering.

High-Level Design

Client apps talk to an API gateway, which routes writes to the Listing Service (create/edit/delete) and buyer-seller traffic to the Messaging Service. Every listing that passes through the Listing Service is fed into a Multimodal Understanding pipeline that produces image and text embeddings; the resulting unified representation, combined with the listing's geo-coordinates, is written into a Search/Ranking Index that serves all keyword, category, and location-based browse/search traffic back to clients.

Figure 1. High-level architecture: client apps → API gateway → Listing Service and Messaging Service. Every new listing flows through the Multimodal Understanding pipeline before landing in the Search/Ranking Index, which combines embeddings with geo-distance filtering and returns browse/search results back to clients (dashed feedback line).

Key Design Principles Reflected in the Diagram

  • The write path (Listing Service) and the read path (Search/Ranking Index) are decoupled by the Multimodal Understanding pipeline sitting between them, so each can be scaled independently.
  • Messaging is a fully separate service from listing CRUD — a buyer-seller conversation has nothing to do with how the listing was indexed or categorized.
  • Inline inference is on the write path, not a side job — a listing isn't considered "published" in a fully useful sense until it has a category and an embedding, since browse/search depend on both.

Low-Level Design

The core "Create Listing" flow is where the feature's most distinctive logic lives: the seller's raw upload (photos + text + location) is turned into a searchable, categorized listing in a single inline pipeline.

Figure 2. Core "Create Listing" flow: an image-understanding model and a text-understanding model each produce an embedding in parallel; the embeddings are concatenated and passed through an MLP to form one unified representation; an autosuggest classifier assigns a top-level category; the combined representation is written into the search/ranking index alongside geo-coordinates, and the listing becomes immediately browsable and searchable.

Deep Dive — Multimodal Listing Understanding: Image and Text Embeddings

This is the crux of the design, and it mirrors the real, documented approach Meta has publicly described for Facebook Marketplace. Each listing has two very different raw inputs — photos and free text — and the system needs a single representation that captures both.

Image Understanding

An image-understanding component — conceptually similar to Meta's real Lumos platform, Meta's general-purpose image/video understanding system reused across many products — encodes each listing photo into a dense embedding. Historically, this has been done via a 50-layer ResNet image-classification encoder pretrained on a large image-classification task, then repurposed as a feature extractor: the network's penultimate-layer activations become the photo's embedding vector, rather than using the network's original classification output directly.

Text Understanding

A text-understanding component — conceptually similar to Meta's real DeepText engine — maps words from the listing's title and description into word embeddings, which are fed through a neural text model to produce a single dense text embedding summarizing the listing's textual content.

Fusion: Concatenation + MLP

The image embedding and the text embedding are concatenated into one longer vector, which is passed into a multilayer perceptron (MLP). The MLP learns to combine the two modalities into one unified listing representation — used downstream for search, recommendation, and categorization alike, so all three consumers share a single, consistently-trained representation of "what this listing is."

Figure 3. Multimodal fusion: a 50-layer ResNet encoder produces an image embedding per photo (Lumos-style); a neural text model produces a text embedding from the title/description (DeepText-style); the two are concatenated and passed through an MLP to produce one unified listing representation, reused for search, recommendation, and categorization.

Deep Dive — Autosuggest and Automatic Categorization at Post Time

An autosuggest/classification model runs on every new listing at the exact moment it's posted, taking the unified multimodal representation from the fusion step above and classifying it into one of roughly two to three dozen top-level categories automatically. This removes the friction of manual category selection from the seller's posting flow entirely — the seller just writes a title, description, and uploads photos; they never have to browse a category tree and guess where their couch or bicycle belongs.

Just as importantly, automatic categorization gives the search/browse experience a consistent taxonomy to filter on immediately — every listing, from the first second it exists, has a category a buyer can filter by, rather than sitting in an "uncategorized" bucket until some later manual or batch process catches up. Because this runs inline rather than as an asynchronous batch job, the category is available in the exact same request that makes the listing browsable in the first place.

Interview tip
A common follow-up: "What if the classifier is wrong?" A strong answer covers both directions — letting a seller override the suggested category post-publish (feeding a correction signal back into future model training), and monitoring classifier accuracy against a human-reviewed sample set (see Monitoring below) so systemic misclassification patterns get caught and retrained against, not just fixed one listing at a time.

Deep Dive — Search, Ranking, and Geospatial Filtering

A buyer's search query combines a text/semantic match against the multimodal listing embeddings with a hard geo-distance filter — conceptually similar to the geospatial-indexing techniques used elsewhere in this section for places/people search (e.g. tile- or grid-based proximity indexing) — plus a ranking layer that blends relevance, recency, and listing-quality signals.

Why a hard geo filter, not just a ranking signal

Distance is treated as a filter, not merely one input among many to a relevance score: a buyer searching for "bike" within a 10-mile radius should never see a semantically perfect match 200 miles away ranked above a decent match 2 miles away. The geo-distance constraint narrows the candidate set first; only candidates surviving that filter are then scored and ranked.

Ranking signals

  • Relevance — semantic similarity between the query and the listing's unified embedding, plus classic keyword/text matching signals.
  • Recency — newer listings are generally favored, since stale, long-unsold listings are more likely to have already been claimed or no longer be available.
  • Listing quality — signals such as photo completeness, description length, seller responsiveness/history, and prior engagement (views, inquiries) on similar listings.
Figure 4. Geo-filtered search flow: a buyer query is matched semantically against listing embeddings and, in parallel, filtered to a hard geo-distance radius; only listings surviving both are candidates, which the ranking layer then orders by relevance, recency, and listing quality before returning results.

Deep Dive — Messaging, Inquiries, and Trust/Safety

A lightweight buyer-to-seller inquiry system lets a buyer ask a question or express interest in a listing; the seller receives a notification on any new message and can reply from within the same thread. This is intentionally simple — async messaging with notification delivery, not a full real-time chat protocol with presence/typing indicators — consistent with treating in-app negotiation as a lightweight bridge to an off-platform transaction rather than a product in its own right.

Trust and Safety, Running Inline

Automated fraud/scam-listing detection and content moderation for prohibited-item categories run inline, alongside the categorization pipeline, from the moment of posting — not as a separate after-the-fact review queue that a listing sits in before going live. This mirrors the same "understand at post time" philosophy as automatic categorization: a listing that is fraudulent or lists a prohibited item ideally never becomes visible to buyers in the first place, rather than being taken down minutes or hours after exposure.

  • The same unified listing representation (image + text embedding) that feeds categorization also feeds a fraud/prohibited-item classifier, reusing the expensive inference work rather than running a second, separate model pass from scratch.
  • Messages themselves are scanned for known scam patterns (e.g. requests to move payment off-platform to an unverifiable channel, phishing links) before delivery.
  • Flagged listings/messages can be routed to a human review queue as a backstop for cases the automated models are uncertain about, rather than fully blocking or fully allowing on every borderline case.

Data Partitioning and Sharding

  • Search/ranking index: partition geographically, mirroring the geosharding pattern used elsewhere in this section, since browse queries are inherently local — a shard serving Chicago-area listings never needs to answer a query scoped to Tokyo, so co-locating listings by region keeps the hot working set of any single query small.
  • Listing storage (transactional CRUD path): partition by listing_id (or by seller_id for locality of a seller's own listings) using consistent hashing, since create/edit/delete/view-by-id operations are simple key lookups independent of geography.
  • Keeping these two partitioning schemes independent is deliberate: the geo-sharded search index and the key-sharded transactional store serve very different access patterns (broad radius scans vs. exact-key lookups) and would be over-constrained by forcing one partitioning scheme to serve both.

Replication and Fault Tolerance

  • Search-index shards are replicated per geographic region for read availability — a replica failure in one region's shard set shouldn't take that region's browse experience down.
  • Listing storage and the inquiry/message log are durably replicated so that a node failure never loses a live seller's listing or a buyer's in-flight message — both are directly visible, unrecoverable losses to a real user if dropped.
  • The inline inference tier (image/text embedding + classification) is itself replicated and load-balanced independently (see Load Balancing below), so a single inference node failure degrades capacity rather than blocking publishing entirely.

Feature-Specific Logic

The single most distinctive piece of feature-specific logic in this design is the multimodal-embedding + autosuggest-categorization pipeline covered in the deep dives above. Restated end-to-end: a seller submits photos, text, and a location → an image-understanding model (Lumos-style, 50-layer ResNet encoder) and a text-understanding model (DeepText-style neural text model) each produce an embedding → the two embeddings are concatenated and passed through an MLP to form one unified listing representation → an autosuggest classifier uses that representation to assign a top-level category automatically, with no manual seller input → the same representation, together with geo-coordinates, is written into the search/ranking index → the listing is immediately browsable and searchable by keyword, category, and location. Every other subsystem in this design (search, ranking, moderation, recommendation) is, in effect, a consumer of this one pipeline's output.

Security, Privacy and Permissions

  • Location-radius controls: per-listing visibility settings ensure a seller's listing shows an approximate area (e.g. neighborhood-level) to buyers rather than broadcasting their exact home address; only a coarser location is exposed publicly, with precise coordinates used internally for ranking/distance computation only.
  • Content moderation: the inline classification pipeline (see Trust/Safety deep dive) screens for prohibited or unsafe listing categories (e.g. weapons, animals, recalled goods) before a listing becomes publicly visible.
  • Rate limiting on listing creation: per-account limits on how many listings can be created in a given window deter spam and large-scale scraping/farming of the marketplace surface.
  • Messaging privacy: inquiry threads are visible only to the buyer and seller involved; a seller cannot see a buyer's full profile/location beyond what's needed to respond to the inquiry.

Load Balancing

Three independently-scaled tiers sit behind load balancers:

  1. Listing-creation traffic (writes) — comparatively low volume, latency-sensitive because of the inline inference step, load balanced across Listing Service instances.
  2. Browse/search traffic (reads) — the much heavier of the two, load balanced separately across the search/ranking index's query-serving tier so a surge in search traffic never starves listing-creation capacity, and vice versa.
  3. Inference workload for the image/text embedding pipeline — scaled and load balanced as its own independent tier, since inference is GPU/accelerator-bound and has a fundamentally different capacity profile (and cost) than either the CRUD or the search-serving tiers.

Scaling to Multiple Regions

  • Regional search-index shards aligned to population density, mirroring the dynamic-grid-size idea used for proximity search elsewhere in this section (finer-grained shards for dense metros, coarser shards for sparsely populated regions) rather than one fixed global resolution.
  • Regional inference clusters for the embedding/classification pipeline keep post-time categorization latency low everywhere, avoiding a scenario where a seller in one region waits noticeably longer to publish than a seller near the primary inference cluster.
  • Listing and inquiry data can replicate across regions asynchronously; a listing is normally read back from the same region it was created in, so full cross-region propagation completing within a few seconds is not a correctness issue.

Monitoring, Logging and Analytics

  • Listing-to-first-view latency — how quickly a newly published listing becomes visible in at least one real search/browse result; a proxy for whether the inline pipeline is keeping up.
  • Categorization-accuracy monitoring against human-reviewed samples — tracked continuously so a drifting or degraded classifier is caught before it silently misfiles a large share of new listings.
  • Search-relevance and click-through metrics — do buyers click/engage with top-ranked results, segmented by category and region, to catch ranking regressions.
  • Fraud/scam-detection precision and recall — tracked separately from category accuracy, since a false negative here (a scam listing that stays live) and a false positive (a legitimate seller wrongly blocked) have very different costs.

Common Interview Questions

Beyond the core design, interviewers love to pressure-test one component at a time. These are organized by theme; expect a real interview to pick a handful as "and then what?" follow-ups.

Scaling and Performance

A viral sale event in one dense city spikes search traffic on a single geo-shard — walk through what happens.
The regional/city shard absorbs the spike first; if it's a recurring hotspot, the fix is finer-grained sharding for that region (smaller grid cells, more shards) rather than resharding the whole global index, plus autoscaling the query-serving tier in front of that shard.
How would you keep publish latency low even as the inline inference pipeline grows more sophisticated (bigger models)?
Scale the inference tier independently from the CRUD/search tiers, batch small photo/text inference calls where latency budgets allow, and consider a smaller/faster "good enough" model on the synchronous path with an asynchronous, more expensive re-classification pass that only overrides on high-confidence disagreement.
Search QPS is roughly 200× listing-creation QPS — how does that ratio shape the architecture?
It justifies aggressively caching/serving reads from a purpose-built search/ranking index (rather than querying transactional listing storage directly), and it means the read-serving tier needs far more horizontal capacity and independent scaling than the write tier.

Multimodal Understanding and ML

Why concatenate the image and text embeddings and pass them through an MLP, instead of just averaging or scoring them separately?
Concatenation + an MLP lets the model learn interactions between modalities (e.g. a photo that looks like a "chair" but the text says "chair frame, needs reupholstering") rather than treating image and text as independent signals that are only combined at the very end by simple averaging.
A listing has 8 photos — how do you get a single image embedding for the fusion step?
Common approaches: encode a designated primary/cover photo only, or encode all photos and pool (average/max) their embeddings into one vector before concatenation with the text embedding — a real system would pick based on measured relevance/categorization accuracy trade-offs.
How would you detect that the autosuggest classifier has started degrading after a model update?
Continuously score a held-out, human-reviewed sample of newly posted listings against the live classifier and alert on accuracy drift, rather than relying solely on downstream signals like search complaints, which surface the problem too late.

Search and Ranking

Should geo-distance be a hard filter or just a ranking signal?
Hard filter for the default browse radius — a buyer generally cannot act on a listing 200 miles away, so it shouldn't occupy a results slot regardless of how semantically relevant it is; distance beyond the filter boundary can still be a soft ranking signal within the radius (closer ranks slightly higher, all else equal).
How would you let a buyer widen their search radius when there aren't enough local results?
Progressive radius expansion: query the tightest radius first, and only if the candidate set is below a minimum threshold, re-query neighboring/wider geo-shards — the same boundary-expansion idea used in other geo-indexed proximity searches in this section.
A seller complains their listing never shows up in search even though it was published successfully — how do you debug it?
Check whether the listing actually made it through the inference pipeline into the search index (a stuck/failed inference job is the most likely culprit), then check ranking (it may be indexed but ranked very low due to low quality/recency signals), then check the geo-shard routing.

Data and Correctness

A seller edits their listing's photos after publishing — what has to happen?
The edit should re-trigger the multimodal understanding pipeline (new image embedding, possibly a new category suggestion) and re-index the updated representation; simply patching the listing row without recomputing the embedding would leave search/categorization based on stale photos.
How do you keep the transactional listing store and the search/ranking index consistent?
Treat the listing table as the source of truth and the search index as a derived, eventually-consistent projection updated by the inline pipeline; a reconciliation job can periodically detect and repair listings whose embedding_ref never successfully landed in the index.
What happens if the inference pipeline fails mid-publish (e.g. the text model times out)?
Retry with backoff for a bounded window; if still failing, either hold the listing in a "processing" state invisible to search rather than publishing an uncategorized/unindexed listing, or fall back to a lower-quality synchronous heuristic and asynchronously reprocess once the pipeline recovers — the right choice depends on how strict "no listing ever appears without a category" needs to be.

Security, Trust and Safety

How would you stop the marketplace from being used to list prohibited items (weapons, recalled goods, live animals)?
Run a prohibited-item classifier inline off the same unified embedding used for categorization, block or hold listings above a confidence threshold before they're publicly visible, and route uncertain cases to human review rather than fully auto-approving or auto-blocking every borderline case.
A scammer is mass-posting fake listings to harvest buyer contact info via inquiries — how do you catch this?
Rate limit listing creation per account, monitor for known scam-message patterns in inquiries (e.g. requests to move off-platform to an unverifiable payment channel), and correlate signals across many listings from the same account/device rather than evaluating each listing in isolation.
How do you protect a seller's exact home address while still supporting accurate distance-based search?
Store the precise coordinate for internal distance computation and ranking, but only ever surface a coarser, approximate location (e.g. neighborhood or a randomized offset within a small radius) in anything visible to buyers.

Product and Extensions

How would you extend this design to support in-app payments later?
Payments would be a separate service invoked from the messaging/inquiry flow once a buyer and seller agree on a price, with the listing/search/categorization pipeline entirely unaffected — the boundary was deliberately drawn so payments can be added without touching the core listing pipeline.
How would you support seller reputation/ratings if it were added to scope?
A separate ratings service keyed by seller_id, fed by post-transaction feedback (necessarily gathered off-platform or via a lightweight in-app prompt), with aggregate reputation surfaced as one more ranking-quality signal — without altering the core categorization/search pipeline.
A right-to-be-forgotten/account-deletion request arrives for a seller with hundreds of active listings — how do you honor it?
Delete/deactivate the seller's listings from the transactional store and issue removal from the search/ranking index and any downstream caches; a strong answer also flags the propagation gap between "removed at the source of truth" and "fully purged from every replicated search shard," bounded by index refresh intervals.

Key Takeaways

Remember
This design is defined by one core insight: it's a marketplace feature riding on an already-massive social network, not a marketplace start-up building its userbase from scratch — which is why geo-bounded browsing, inline multimodal understanding, and automatic categorization matter so much more here than in a generic e-commerce search problem. The multimodal-embedding + autosuggest-categorization pipeline (image embedding via a Lumos-style ResNet encoder, text embedding via a DeepText-style neural model, fused via concatenation + MLP, classified into roughly two to three dozen top-level categories) is the single most distinctive piece of logic in the whole design, and nearly every other subsystem — search, ranking, moderation, recommendation — consumes its output. Keep payments and shipping explicitly out of scope and say so out loud; state the inline-inference-latency trade-off explicitly (a little publish latency for much better search/categorization quality); and always tie capacity numbers (listings/sec, search QPS, inference throughput) back to concrete architectural decisions rather than leaving them as standalone math.
