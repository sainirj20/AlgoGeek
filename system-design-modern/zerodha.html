SYSTEM DESIGN

Designing Zerodha

A complete, interview-ready design walkthrough for a Zerodha/Kite-style discount stock-broking platform covering user onboarding and authentication, real-time market-data streaming, order placement/execution, portfolio and P&L tracking, and fund management: requirements and capacity estimation, high-level architecture, API and low-level design, database schema, sharding, feature-specific deep dives (the order-placement timing budget, the concurrent-margin-check correctness walkthrough, exchange reconciliation), replication and fault tolerance, caching, monitoring, security, and a bank of follow-up interview questions. The thread running through every section is that this system has two very different scaling problems living under one roof — a correctness-bound transactional core (orders, margin, funds) sharded by account_id, and a throughput-bound market-data fan-out sharded by symbol — plus one architectural fact that trips up most candidates: the broker risk-checks and routes orders, but never matches them. Matching happens at the exchange.

Problem Statement & Scope

Design a discount stock-broking platform, similar to Zerodha's Kite, that lets retail users:

  • Register and onboard with KYC (PAN, Aadhaar e-KYC, bank account and demat account linkage) and authenticate securely (password + mandatory 2FA/TOTP).
  • View real-time stock prices for thousands of listed instruments across NSE and BSE via a live-updating watchlist, quote panel, and market-depth (5-level order book) view.
  • Place, modify, and cancel buy/sell orders — market and limit, equity and F&O — that are risk-checked and routed to the exchange for matching.
  • Track a portfolio: delivery holdings (T+1) and open positions (intraday/carry-forward) with live, unrealized and realized P&L, plus a full order and trade history.
  • Add or withdraw funds, moving money exclusively between the trading ledger balance and the client's own registered bank account.
  • Give the business an admin/analytics dashboard over the same event stream for operational, revenue, and risk visibility.

In scope

The order-execution core: the risk (RMS) gate, the order-management state machine, exchange connectivity, the market-data fan-out, the funds ledger, portfolio/holdings computation, and reconciliation against the exchange's end-of-day files.

Out of scope (acknowledged, not designed in depth)

The exchange's own matching engine internals (NEAT/BOLT), full SEBI/exchange membership and clearing-corporation onboarding, margin-funding/BNPL products, options-strategy builders, charting internals, and the mobile client UI. We treat SEBI/exchange rules (RMS mandate, T+1 settlement, Algo-ID regime, client-fund segregation) as constraints the design must satisfy, not as features to build from scratch.

Key design tension
Two correctness-critical facts shape almost every later decision. First, the broker is a router, not a matcher: order-matching is the exchange's job (price-time priority at NSE/BSE), so our Order Management Service (OMS) is a state machine and FIX-style client, never an order book. Second, every order must clear a synchronous, SEBI-mandated Risk Management System (RMS) check against available margin before it ever leaves the broker — get this wrong (race two orders past the same margin) and you can let a client trade with money that isn't there, which is a regulatory incident, not just a bug.

Non-Functional Requirements

These are the constraints that actually shape the architecture.

  • High availability and low latency. Target 99.95%+ availability during market hours (9:15 AM–3:30 PM IST) — every minute of downtime is a minute a client cannot manage a live position. Order-acceptance latency (gateway→RMS→OMS→exchange ack) should be well under 200–300 ms P95, excluding the exchange's own matching time, which is outside the broker's control. Multi-AZ deployment (Zerodha's own real infrastructure runs its user-facing tier across 3 AWS availability zones in Mumbai) with auto-scaling absorbs both baseline load and daily spikes.
  • Scalability during market open/close peaks. Traffic is not diurnal in the generic web-traffic sense — it is a predictable, sharp, twice-daily spike at 9:15 AM open and around 3:00–3:30 PM close, plus event-driven spikes on high-volatility days. Unlike a general consumer app, the peak windows are known in advance, so scaling can be scheduled/predictive rather than purely reactive (see Capacity Estimation and Feature-Specific Logic).
  • Event-driven architecture for order processing. Order-lifecycle transitions (RMS-checked, routed, filled, rejected) and fund movements are published as events; portfolio/holdings recomputation, notifications, the admin analytics dashboard, and reconciliation are all async consumers off the same stream, never blocking the synchronous order-placement path.
  • Strong consistency for portfolio/account balance. Available margin, blocked margin, holdings quantity, and ledger balance must never be read stale on a path that decides whether an order can be placed. This is the same "no lost update" discipline as a payments ledger, applied to margin instead of wallet balance — see the RMS deep dive.
  • Secure fund transfers and compliance with SEBI regulations. Client funds are segregated and can only move to/from the client's own registered bank account (no P2P wallet). Every order carries an audit trail sufficient for SEBI/exchange inspection. Algorithmic order flow (via Kite Connect) must comply with the Algo-ID framework (see Security Considerations). RMS is a regulatory requirement, not just good engineering practice.

Capacity Estimation and Constraints

Given assumptions (illustrative, for this exercise — not a claim about Zerodha's actual current user base): ≈2 crore (20M) registered trading accounts; ≈30 lakh (3M) DAU; ≈15 lakh (1.5M) of those place at least one order on a given trading day ("active traders/day"); ≈1.5–2M concurrently logged in right as the market opens. That concurrent-login figure is not arbitrary — it is chosen to sit in the same order of magnitude as the 2M+ concurrent logins at market open Zerodha's own engineering blog (zerodha.tech) has publicly cited, and we treat that as grounding for a reasonable assumption, not as an assertion of today's exact numbers. Similarly, our order-volume assumptions below land in the same order of magnitude as the 7–9 million trades/day Zerodha has cited on its busiest historical days.

Order & trade volume

Assumption Active traders/day = 1.5M
Order events/trader/day (new + modify + cancel) ≈ 8 → Order events/day ≈ 1.5M × 8 = 12M/day
Assume ≈70% of order events resolve into a fill → Trades/day ≈ 12M × 0.7 ≈ 8.4M ≈ 8M/day
(8M/day lands inside the real, cited 7–9M historical range — used here only as a sanity check on the assumption, not as today's actual figure.)

Throughput (TPS) — and why the open matters more than the average

NSE/BSE equity cash session runs 9:15 AM–3:30 PM IST = 6h15m = 22,500 s.

Calculation Average order TPS = 12M ÷ 22,500 s ≈ 533 orders/sec
Peak-open concentration (assumption): ≈10% of the day's order volume arrives in the first 5 minutes (300 s) after 9:15
Orders in first 5 min = 12M × 10% = 1.2M → 1.2M ÷ 300 s = 4,000 orders/sec sustained
Instantaneous burst multiplier for the literal first 10–15 s (queued AMO releases + simultaneous manual clicks): ×1.5 → ≈6,000 orders/sec peak

So the system must be provisioned for ≈6,000 orders/sec at the open — roughly 11× the all-day average — even though the all-day average (≈533/sec) is unremarkable. This is a fundamentally different shape from a generic web app's peak: the spike is scheduled, recurring daily at 9:15:00 AM, and known in advance.

Storage growth (orders / trades / funds ledger)

Per order record ≈0.5 KB; per trade/execution row ≈0.6 KB (adds exchange trade id, fill price/qty); per funds_ledger double-entry leg ≈0.4 KB (assume ≈2 legs/trade for margin block+settlement).

Calculation Orders: 12M × 0.5 KB = 6 GB/day
Trades: 8M × 0.6 KB = 4.8 GB/day
Funds ledger legs: 8M × 2 × 0.4 KB = 6.4 GB/day
Raw total ≈ 17.2 GB/day → round to ≈18 GB/day
+ secondary indexes (≈50%) ≈ 27 GB/day
Trading days/year (NSE/BSE holidays + weekends) ≈ 250 → ≈27 GB × 250 ≈ 6.75 TB/year
Assume ≈8-year retention (illustrative, in the spirit of SEBI's multi-year record-keeping requirements) ≈ 54 TB raw+idx
× 3 replicas (quorum) ≈ 162 TB provisioned

Zerodha's own engineering blog describes its Postgres fleet, with logical partitioning and read replicas, as comfortably handling "hundreds of billions of records" — consistent with this being a storage-manageable, not storage-bound, problem once sharded and partitioned (see Data Sharding).

Reads per order on the synchronous path (client_order_id lookup, available-margin read-for-update, instrument-master lookup, exposure/leverage limit check) ≈4:

Calculation Peak sync read IOPS ≈ 6,000 × 4 = 24,000 IOPS
+ portfolio/holdings/order-status read traffic (assume ≈6× order RPS of pure reads, since Kite polls/streams portfolio views continuously) → provision ≈150k read IOPS

Crucially, almost none of that 150k read IOPS actually hits Postgres: it is absorbed by the Redis "hot cache" tier described in Caching Strategy, which is precisely why Zerodha's own architecture serves every Kite UI read — orders, positions, portfolio — as an O(1) in-memory lookup rather than a database query.

WebSocket tick fan-out throughput

This is the other scaling axis, and it dwarfs the order-write path in raw message volume.

Calculation Peak concurrent WebSocket connections ≈ 2M (grounded by the real, cited 2M+ concurrent logins at open)
Raw exchange tick ingest ≈ 2,000 actively-traded instruments × up to 10 updates/sec at peak volatility ≈ 20,000 ticks/sec into the Ticker Service (single ingest point)
Per-connection fan-out is coalesced/rate-limited to ≈1 update/sec per subscribed symbol (a human doesn't need every micro-tick); assume ≈8 actively-watched symbols/connection
Egress messages/sec ≈ 2M connections × 8 symbols × 1 msg/sec = 16M messages/sec
At ≈100 bytes/msg (compact binary tick packet) ≈ 1.6 GB/sec egress bandwidth at absolute peak

A fan-out ratio like this — one exchange feed in, 16M coalesced messages/sec out — is precisely why Zerodha built a bespoke, single-binary Go "ticker" service rather than routing client-facing ticks through a general-purpose message broker: a generic pub/sub layer pays serialization and per-message broker overhead that a purpose-built, zero-allocation broadcast loop does not (see Low-Level Design and Caching Strategy).

App-server instances at peak (Little's Law)

Calculation Order placement (sync hold ≈150 ms incl. RMS+OMS+exchange ack): 6,000 TPS × 0.15 s = 900 concurrent
Portfolio/order-status reads (served mostly from Redis, ≈50 ms hold): 20,000 RPS × 0.05 s = 1,000 concurrent
Total ≈ 1,900 concurrent requests (API/OMS tier, excludes the ticker tier below)
Result Instances (raw) = 1,900 ÷ 500 concurrent-per-instance ≈ 4 → provision 3 AZs × 4 instances = 12 API/OMS instances at peak.
Ticker tier sizing is a different calculation: a single lightweight Go binary handles ≈50k concurrent WS connections comfortably (per Zerodha's own published design) → 2M ÷ 50k = 40 ticker instances, provisioned ahead of the 9:15 AM open (see Feature-Specific Logic: market-open surge handling).
Takeaway
The transactional core (≈6,000 orders/sec peak, ≈100k write IOPS) is correctness-bound: the engineering effort goes into the RMS margin check, idempotent order handling, and reconciliation. The market-data path (16M coalesced messages/sec, 2M concurrent connections) is throughput-bound: the engineering effort there goes into a lean, purpose-built broadcast service. These are two different problems, sharded on two different keys (account_id vs symbol) — see Data Sharding and Partitioning.

Architecture overview

The diagram below turns the service list into a wiring diagram. A single API Gateway routes into the Order Management Service, which runs a synchronous margin/exposure check against the Risk Management Service and then — and only then — hands the order to the Exchange Connectivity Gateway. The Ticker Service ingests the exchange's live feed independently of the order path and fans it out to millions of WebSocket subscribers. Notification, Reconciliation, and Admin/Analytics sit off to the side as async consumers of the event bus, never on the order's critical path.

Figure 1. Service topology: the API Gateway routes into the Order Management Service, which runs a synchronous margin check against RMS before handing the order to the Exchange Connectivity Gateway — the only path to the exchange. The Ticker Service ingests the exchange feed independently and fans it out over WebSocket. Notification, Reconciliation, and Admin/Analytics are async consumers off the event bus.

Data flow (a): Order placement (limit order, equity)

  1. Client → Gateway →(client_order_id)→ OMS.
  2. OMS: idempotency check on client_order_id (new → insert PLACED).
  3. OMS → RMS: synchronous margin/exposure check.
  4. RMS: atomic conditional reserve of margin (available_margin ≥ required) — single local ACID transaction (see Low-Level Design 7.2).
  5. RMS → OMS: APPROVED (or REJECTED — INSUFFICIENT_MARGIN, order never leaves the broker).
  6. OMS → Exchange Connectivity Gateway → Exchange: route order (colocation/leased-line hop); state = ROUTED_TO_EXCHANGE.
  7. Exchange acks (exchange_order_id assigned) → OMS marks state = OPEN; return to client.
  8. (async, as fills arrive) Exchange → OMS: fill event(s) → PARTIALLY_FILLED/FILLED → Portfolio Service updates position → Funds/Ledger settles the margin reservation → outbox → Kafka → Notification.
Figure 2. Order placement: the margin check against RMS is synchronous and blocks routing to the exchange — an order that fails it never leaves the broker. Once acked by the exchange, fills arrive asynchronously and drive the portfolio/ledger updates off the critical path.

Data flow (b): Live market data (WebSocket tick fan-out)

Market data is a completely separate pipeline from the order path: one exchange feed in, fanned out to every subscribed client.

Figure 3. WebSocket tick fan-out: the Ticker Service ingests the exchange feed once, maintains a per-symbol topic/subscription table, and broadcasts — the same design Zerodha's own engineering blog describes for Kite, purpose-built rather than routed through a generic message broker because of the fan-out ratio involved.

Data flow (c): Add funds / withdraw funds

  1. Client → Gateway → Funds/Ledger Service: initiate deposit (UPI/net-banking) or withdrawal, referencing the client's own registered bank account — never another user's account or wallet.
  2. Funds Service calls the bank/payment rail; for deposits the confirmation may return synchronously (UPI) or via an async callback.
  3. On confirmed credit: append a ledger leg (CREDIT ledger_balance, DEBIT bank-transit suspense) and update the account's available_margin.
  4. On withdrawal: reserve/deduct ledger balance atomically (never allow withdrawal to push balance negative or below blocked margin), then initiate the bank payout; reverse the reservation if the payout fails.
  5. Outbox → Kafka → Notification ("funds credited/debited").
Figure 4. Fund transfer: money moves only between the client's own bank account and their trading ledger balance — there is no peer-to-peer wallet transfer between users, unlike a payments app (see Security Considerations).

What the server does with Client-Order-Id

  1. Compute request_hash = SHA-256(method + path + canonical(body)).
  2. Miss → atomically insert (client_order_id, account_id, request_hash, status=PLACED) guarded by a unique constraint; proceed to RMS check and routing.
  3. Hit, same hash, terminal status (OPEN/FILLED/REJECTED/CANCELLED) → return the stored order state verbatim. No re-routing to the exchange.
  4. Hit, in-flight (still being RMS-checked/routed) → return 409 Conflict with Retry-After; never place a second order for the same client_order_id.
  5. Hit, different hash → 422 Unprocessable Entity — a client reusing a key for a different order is a client bug, not something to silently mask.

Low-Level Design (Deep Dive)

7.1 Order lifecycle state machine

The OMS state machine is the backbone of the whole design. Every transition is durably persisted before the next step runs, so a crash mid-flight resumes from a known state rather than a guess.

States PLACED → RMS_CHECKED → ROUTED_TO_EXCHANGE → OPEN → PARTIALLY_FILLED → FILLED
Branches: RMS_CHECKED → REJECTED (insufficient margin, never reaches the exchange)
ROUTED_TO_EXCHANGE → REJECTED (exchange-side reject: price band breach, instrument halted, session closed)
OPEN / PARTIALLY_FILLED → CANCELLED (user cancel, or MIS auto square-off near session close)
Figure 5. Order lifecycle state machine: RMS_CHECKED is the SEBI-mandated gate — an order that fails it moves straight to REJECTED and never reaches ROUTED_TO_EXCHANGE. Fills and cancels are driven by asynchronous exchange events once the order is OPEN.

7.2 RMS pre-trade margin check — atomic reservation

The classic bug this must prevent: two concurrent orders from the same account both read "available margin = ₹50,000", each requiring ₹40,000, both pass the check, both get routed — the account is now exposed for ₹80,000 against ₹50,000 of margin. This is the exact same shape of bug as a wallet double-spend, applied to margin instead of cash balance.

def rms_check(order, account_id):
  instrument = get_instrument(order.tradingsymbol, order.exchange)
  required_margin = compute_required_margin(order, instrument)
  # SPAN+exposure for F&O; price*qty*margin_pct for MIS; full value for CNC delivery

  # single indivisible statement: the predicate is evaluated under the row lock
  rows = UPDATE trading_accounts
         SET available_margin = available_margin - :required_margin,
             blocked_margin   = blocked_margin + :required_margin,
             version = version + 1
         WHERE account_id = :account_id AND available_margin >= :required_margin

  if rows == 0:
    reject(order, reason="INSUFFICIENT_MARGIN")   # order never reaches OMS routing
    return

  reservation_id = insert_margin_reservation(account_id, order.order_id, required_margin)
  route_to_oms(order, reservation_id)              # ROUTED_TO_EXCHANGE

# on exchange reject/cancel: release_reservation(reservation_id) -> available_margin += required_margin
# on fill: settle_reservation(reservation_id) -> move blocked_margin into realized cost basis
Figure 6. RMS atomic margin reservation: the check-and-reserve is one indivisible SQL statement, so two orders racing against the same available margin cannot both pass — exactly analogous to the atomic-conditional-debit pattern used to prevent wallet double-spend in payments systems, applied here to margin instead of cash.

Why not a generic optimistic-lock retry loop? Read-balance-then-check-in-app-then-write has the same TOCTOU gap as the classic wallet double-spend bug: two reads can both see sufficient margin before either write lands. The conditional UPDATE closes that gap by making the check and the decrement one statement, evaluated under the row's lock. Why not pessimistic SELECT ... FOR UPDATE? It works too and is a reasonable alternative, but it holds a row lock for the duration of margin-calculation logic (SPAN/exposure computation can be non-trivial for F&O), which is worse under contention than a single conditional UPDATE that does the calculation first and the lock-and-check only at the very end.

7.3 WebSocket ticker fan-out design

  • Single ingest, per-symbol topic model. The Ticker Service maintains one process-local table mapping instrument_token → the set of subscriber connections currently interested in it. A tick for INFY is looked up once and written to every subscribed connection's send buffer — there is no per-subscriber re-fetch or re-computation.
  • Why a bespoke Go binary, not a generic pub/sub broker. At ≈16M coalesced messages/sec egress (see Capacity Estimation), a general-purpose broker's per-message overhead (serialization, broker hop, ack bookkeeping) becomes the bottleneck. A single lightweight, low-allocation binary handling connections as goroutines and doing raw byte-buffer broadcasts is dramatically cheaper per message — this is the actual, published reasoning behind Zerodha choosing Go for this component starting around 2014.
  • Per-connection coalescing. The exchange may emit several ticks/sec for a liquid instrument; the Ticker Service coalesces to the latest value per symbol per connection at a bounded rate (≈1/sec) rather than relaying every raw tick, which is both unnecessary for a human-readable UI and what keeps egress bandwidth bounded.
  • Order-update frames share the same socket. Fill/reject/cancel notifications for the user's own orders are multiplexed onto the same WebSocket connection as price ticks, avoiding a second connection per client.
  • Decoupled from the transactional core. The Ticker Service has no dependency on Postgres, RMS, or the OMS on its hot path — a slow or degraded order-placement path never throttles price streaming, and vice versa. This separation is what makes the dual-sharding-axis architecture in Section 9 possible.

7.4 Idempotency / duplicate-order protection on retries

A client that times out waiting for a response to POST /v1/orders and retries must never place the order twice. The mechanism is the same insert-first, unique-constraint pattern used for payments idempotency, keyed on client_order_id instead of a generic idempotency key:

def handle_place_order(client_order_id, account_id, req):
  h = sha256(canonical(req))
  try:
    INSERT INTO orders(client_order_id, account_id, request_hash, status)
    VALUES(client_order_id, account_id, h, 'PLACED')  -- unique(client_order_id, account_id)
  except UniqueViolation:
    row = SELECT ... WHERE client_order_id=client_order_id AND account_id=account_id
    if row.request_hash != h: return 422       # same key, different order
    if row.status in TERMINAL_STATES: return replay(row)   # OPEN/FILLED/REJECTED/CANCELLED
    return 409  # RMS-checking/routing in flight, do not place a second order

  # we won the insert -> we own this order's execution
  result = run_rms_check_and_route(req)
  UPDATE orders SET status=result.status, exchange_order_id=result.exchange_order_id
  WHERE client_order_id=client_order_id AND account_id=account_id
  return result

Why this matters more here than in a generic API: a duplicated order isn't just a duplicate database row — if it were re-routed, it would become a second live order in the market, potentially executing against real liquidity and creating a real, expensive-to-unwind position. The unique-constraint insert is the race-safe boundary that guarantees exactly one execution path per client_order_id, exactly as it does for a payment.

Data Sharding and Partitioning

This is the single most distinctive structural idea in this design: the system has two independent scaling axes that must not be conflated.

Axis 1 — the transactional core, sharded by account_id

Shard key: account_id. Orders, trades, holdings, positions, and funds_ledger rows for one account live together on one shard, so RMS's margin reservation, the order-state transitions, and the ledger legs for that account are all a single local ACID transaction. Consistent hashing maps account_id → shard.

Notably simpler than a P2P payments platform: because funds can only move between an account's own ledger and that same account's own registered bank account (see Security Considerations), there is no cross-shard money movement to reconcile — no saga, no two-phase commit, no cross-account distributed transaction is ever required for a fund transfer. The hard cross-shard problem that a wallet-to-wallet payments app must solve (two different accounts' balances, possibly on two different shards, both needing to change atomically) simply does not exist here, because Zerodha has no peer-to-peer wallet. The only place sharding gets interesting on this axis is a shard-migration/rebalance, which follows the same "freeze one account, replicate, cut over" playbook used for wallet migrations elsewhere (see Follow-up Interview Questions).

Axis 2 — market data, partitioned by symbol/instrument_token

Live ticks are a completely different workload shape: a single high-fan-in write path (one exchange feed, ≈20,000 ticks/sec across ≈2,000 instruments) feeding a massive fan-out read path (millions of WebSocket subscribers). There is no per-account concept here at all — the natural partition key is the instrument_token (symbol), and the store is a time-series-oriented system (ClickHouse/ScyllaDB in Zerodha's own real stack) rather than the OLTP Postgres fleet, because the query patterns (latest value per symbol, historical OHLC, high write throughput) are analytical/time-series, not transactional.

Figure 7. The dual sharding axis: the transactional core (orders/holdings/positions/funds_ledger) shards by account_id with no cross-shard money movement to coordinate, while market data partitions by symbol/instrument_token with a single high-fan-in write path and a massive read fan-out — two unrelated scaling problems, deliberately kept on separate infrastructure.

Retention/tiering: partition orders/trades/funds_ledger tables by month; hot months on fast storage, older partitions moved to cheaper storage (still queryable for SEBI/exchange audit) — keeps the multi-year, ≈160 TB footprint affordable while the hot working set stays small. Tick/time-series data in ClickHouse/ScyllaDB is retained separately and at much higher volume/velocity, with its own downsampling and cold-tiering policy, since it is an analytics/audit trail, not a transactional store.

10.2 Market-open surge handling (9:00–9:15–9:45 AM IST)

  • 9:00–9:08 AM — pre-open session: NSE/BSE collect orders and compute an equilibrium opening price without live matching; the broker's OMS accepts and queues orders normally, but nothing is ROUTED_TO_EXCHANGE-and-matched yet. This window is a natural place to pre-warm caches and pre-scale.
  • 9:15 AM — continuous trading begins: the scheduled, known-in-advance spike hits simultaneously across (a) the order-write path (RMS+OMS+Exchange Gateway) and (b) the WebSocket tier (mass reconnect/login as users open Kite at the bell). Because this spike is predictable and recurring daily — unlike a generic web app's organic traffic — capacity is scheduled/predictive-scaled ahead of 9:15 (e.g., pre-warm the 40 ticker instances and the 12 API/OMS instances from Capacity Estimation a few minutes before the bell) rather than purely reactive auto-scaling, which would react a few seconds too late for a spike this sharp.
  • WebSocket connection-storm handling: client SDKs reconnect with exponential backoff + jitter so a ticker-instance restart or deploy does not create a synchronized reconnect thundering herd; the Ticker Service load-balancer spreads new connections across instances with least-connections routing, and a connection admission limit per instance protects against a single instance being overwhelmed.
  • 3:00–3:30 PM close: a second, similarly predictable spike as intraday (MIS) positions are auto-square-off by RMS ahead of the close and users adjust final positions; sized identically to the open in this design.

10.3 Reconciliation against exchange end-of-day files

Purpose: prove the broker's own order/trade/position records agree with what the exchange's clearing corporation says was actually obligated/settled, and catch anything stuck.

  1. End of day (T+0 evening): ingest the exchange's contract note / obligation file per segment (rows of exchange_order_id/exchange_trade_id, quantity, price, net obligation).
  2. Match each external row to internal orders/trades by exchange_order_id / exchange_trade_id (unique index).
  3. Classify:
ClassificationMeaning / action
MATCHEDinternal FILLED, quantities/prices agree → ok
MISSING_INTERNALexchange shows a fill with no/incomplete internal record → investigate a possibly-lost fill event; correct the order/position, else escalate
MISSING_EXTERNALinternal FILLED, absent from the exchange file → chase the exchange feed; possible duplicate/phantom fill on our side → reverse if confirmed absent
AMOUNT_MISMATCHbrokerage/STT/exchange-fee differences → post adjustment ledger legs
STUCKinternal ROUTED_TO_EXCHANGE/OPEN past session close with no terminal exchange status → resolve via exchange status query / manual queue
  1. Every mismatch posts a ledger suspense entry plus an incident record — nothing is silently written off; reconciliation mismatch count is a first-class SLI (see Monitoring).
  2. Separately, recompute SUM(margin reservations released/settled) per account against trading_accounts.available_margin/blocked_margin as an internal self-recon, the same way a payments ledger self-reconciles SUM(legs) against a materialized balance.
  3. Settlement follows India's T+1 cycle for equities (as of Jan 2023), so the obligation file for a given trade date is confirmed the next business day; NSE has piloted an optional T+0 window for select securities, which reconciliation must be able to handle on a per-security basis.

10.4 Concurrent margin-check correctness walkthrough (the interview classic, adapted)

Scenario: account has ₹50,000 available margin; two concurrent MIS buy orders each require ₹40,000. Walkthrough with the chosen atomic conditional reserve (Section 7.2):

  1. Both orders carry different client_order_ids (different logical operations), so both pass idempotency and reach RMS.
  2. Both target the same account_id, which lives on one shard; requests serialize at the row (whether via single-writer routing or simply the DB's row lock) — order A is processed, then order B.
  3. Order A executes: UPDATE trading_accounts SET available_margin = available_margin - 40000 WHERE account_id=X AND available_margin >= 40000; → 1 row affected, available_margin 50000→10000. Margin reservation row inserted, order routed. Commit.
  4. Order B executes the same statement, but now 10000 >= 40000 is false → 0 rows affected → RMS returns INSUFFICIENT_MARGIN. Order B never reaches the Exchange Connectivity Gateway.

Even without single-writer routing, the conditional predicate inside the UPDATE (evaluated under the row lock) is what prevents the over-exposure: the two UPDATEs serialize at the row level, whichever commits first wins, and the second sees the decremented balance and fails. The bug this eliminates — "read available margin, check in the app layer, then write" — is exactly the wallet double-spend bug, just with margin instead of cash; the fix is the same atomic-check-and-decrement discipline.

Replication and Fault Tolerance

  • Synchronous / quorum writes for the orders/margin/funds_ledger DB. Writes on this path replicate synchronously to a quorum (primary + ≥1 sync replica) before ack. Rationale: with async replication, a primary crash after ack-but-before-replication can lose a committed order state or a margin reservation — a client believes an order is OPEN, or believes margin was released, and it silently reverts. For money and live-order state that is intolerable, so we accept a few extra milliseconds of intra-region write latency for durability. Same CP choice, same trade-off, as a payments ledger.
  • Multi-region: active-passive, not active-active, for the transactional core. A single region is authoritative for writes for a given account, with a warm standby kept current via replication and promoted on regional failure. Active-active for order routing is actively dangerous here, arguably more so than for a wallet: if two regions both accepted and routed live orders for the same account during a network partition, the failure mode is not just a reconciliation headache — it can mean two live orders in the real market that both execute, creating a real position that must be unwound at whatever price the market offers by the time anyone notices. We accept bounded failover downtime (measured RTO/RPO) over that risk. Read traffic (portfolio, quotes, history) can be served active-active from replicas/cache; writes are single-region-authoritative per account.
  • Circuit breakers around the Exchange Connectivity Gateway. If the FIX session's error-rate/latency crosses a threshold (network blip, exchange-side degradation), open the breaker: fail order-routing fast with a clear "exchange connectivity degraded" status rather than piling up threads waiting on a stalled session. Half-open probes restore traffic once the session recovers. This protects the OMS's own SLO from a slow external dependency, exactly as a payments platform circuit-breaks around a slow PSP.
  • Redis hot-cache tier: multiple independent instances, no single point of failure. Because every Kite UI read (orders, positions, portfolio) is served from this cache (see Caching Strategy), it cannot be a single instance. Multiple independent Redis instances (sharded by account_id, mirroring the DB shard boundary) provide HA; disk persistence is deliberately turned off for speed, so a lost instance is repopulated from the authoritative Postgres/event stream by the async cache-populator, not restored from an RDB/AOF file — the cache is disposable by design, the database is not.
  • Retry-with-backoff, made safe by idempotency. Client retries and internal service-to-service retries use exponential backoff + jitter and carry the same client_order_id/idempotency key, so a retry can never double-place an order or double-move funds — the same inseparable pairing of "retries are safe" and "everything is idempotent" as in a payments platform.

Caching Strategy

Guiding rule: cache reads that are safe to be slightly stale for display; never cache anything a margin/order-routing decision depends on.

The "dumb cache" read path, in Zerodha's own real design: a GET for orders/positions/portfolio does an O(1) in-memory lookup in Redis and writes the raw stored bytes straight onto the HTTP response — no query, no serialization, no computation on the hot path. A separate, decoupled async job is responsible for keeping that cache populated whenever the underlying state changes. Disk persistence is turned off on these Redis instances for raw speed (see Replication and Fault Tolerance for why that is safe). On top of this, an HTTP ETag/304 layer — a Redis-backed map of per-user-per-resource ETags — lets a client send If-None-Match and get back a bodyless 304 Not Modified when nothing changed (e.g., an unchanged order list on a background poll), which matters a great deal for mobile clients on cellular data.

Monitoring, Logging, Analytics and Notifications

Key SLIs / metrics:

  • Order acceptance latency P95/P99 (gateway→RMS→OMS→exchange ack), tracked separately from the exchange's own matching time, which is outside the broker's control.
  • RMS rejection rate (INSUFFICIENT_MARGIN and others) — a sudden spike can indicate a margin-calculation bug or a market-wide volatility event driving margin requirements up.
  • Tick delivery lag — exchange-feed timestamp vs. the time a tick is actually flushed to a subscriber connection; a growing lag under load is the earliest signal the Ticker tier needs to scale.
  • WebSocket reconnect-storm rate — a spike here after a deploy or an instance restart signals the backoff/jitter strategy isn't spreading reconnects enough.
  • Reconciliation mismatch count and aging — any nonzero mismatch against the exchange's EOD files is investigated; trend is a compliance signal, not just an ops one.
  • PSP/bank error rate for fund transfers, circuit-breaker state on the Exchange Connectivity Gateway, idempotency-replay rate (duplicate client_order_id hits), DLQ depth on the event bus.

Audit logging (immutable, tamper-evident): the orders/trades/funds_ledger tables are append-only for state history; hash-chaining each ledger entry (store prev_hash and hash = H(prev_hash || record)) makes retroactive tampering detectable, and SEBI/exchange inspection requires this trail to be retained for years and be reconstructable per client, per instrument, per day — not just aggregated.

Admin/Analytics dashboard (FR7) as a downstream consumer: the same order/trade/fund event stream that feeds Notification and Reconciliation also feeds the internal analytics pipeline — order funnel and drop-off (PLACED→REJECTED ratio by reason), DAU and order-volume trends, most-traded instruments, brokerage/STT revenue, and RMS rejection breakdowns. It reads the stream asynchronously and never sits on the order-placement critical path.

Notification triggers: order filled/rejected/cancelled, margin call (intraday margin utilization crossing a threshold, e.g. MIS position approaching auto-square-off), fund credit/debit confirmation. All delivered async off the event bus.

Security Considerations

  • 2FA/TOTP on every login. SEBI requires two-factor authentication for trading platforms; Kite enforces password + TOTP for user sessions. Kite Connect (API/algo) clients authenticate via an API-key + request-token/access-token exchange instead of a password, but the underlying account still sits behind the user's own 2FA-gated login when the token was issued.
  • KYC / Aadhaar e-KYC onboarding. PAN verification and Aadhaar-based e-KYC establish identity before an account can trade; segment enablement (equity/F&O/currency/commodity) is gated on KYC completeness and risk profiling (e.g., F&O suitability checks).
  • Encryption in transit & at rest. TLS 1.2+/mTLS between services; database and backups encrypted (AES-256); secrets/keys in a KMS/HSM with rotation.
  • Client funds are segregated and self-only — no peer-to-peer wallet. This is a load-bearing, easy-to-get-wrong point: unlike a payments app such as Paytm, there is no user-to-user transfer anywhere in this design. Fund management (FR5) moves money only between the client's ledger balance and that same client's own registered bank account. This follows SEBI's client-fund-segregation rules, which require a broker to keep client funds distinct from the broker's own funds and settle through client-designated bank accounts — it is a regulatory constraint, not a product choice, and it is exactly why the sharding story in Section 9 never needs a cross-account money saga.
  • The 2026 SEBI Algo-ID framework, for any algorithmic/API order flow. Since the framework took effect on April 1, 2026, every order placed algorithmically — including via the public Kite Connect API — must carry a unique, exchange-issued Algo-ID so exchanges can trace, monitor, and audit its origin and behavior. No algorithm may go live without prior exchange approval. Algorithms fall into two categories with different obligations:
    • White-box algos: fully transparent, documented logic, self-registered by the user/broker with the exchange. Suited to a retail trader running their own simple, disclosed strategy through the API.
    • Black-box algos: logic undisclosed to the end user. The provider of a black-box algo must hold a SEBI Research Analyst (RA) license and publish periodic performance/risk disclosures — a materially higher compliance bar than white-box, because the end user cannot independently verify what the algorithm is doing.
    Architecturally, this means the Auth Service/API-key issuance path for Kite Connect must capture and validate an Algo-ID on every order that originates from a registered algorithmic client, and RMS/OMS must be able to reject or flag an order arriving without one once the client is enrolled as an algo trader.
  • Rate limiting & anomaly detection. Per-account/per-API-key rate limits at the gateway; velocity checks in RMS (unusually large order sizes/frequency relative to the account's history) can trigger a step-up or a manual review, independent of the margin check.
  • Idempotency as a security control. Just as in a payments platform, the client_order_id/unique-constraint discipline prevents replay-based duplicate order placement, whether from a client bug or a malicious retry.

Concurrency, Correctness & Margin

Q1. Two concurrent orders hit the same account with ₹50,000 available margin, each requiring ₹40,000 — walk me through exactly how you prevent both from being routed.
Both requests reach RMS and execute UPDATE trading_accounts SET available_margin=available_margin-40000 WHERE account_id=X AND available_margin>=40000. The first commits (50000→10000); the second's predicate 10000>=40000 is false → 0 rows affected → INSUFFICIENT_MARGIN, and that order never reaches the Exchange Connectivity Gateway. The check is atomic with the decrement inside the row lock, so there is no read-then-check gap.
Q2. Why can't you just cache the available-margin figure to make the RMS check faster?
Because the RMS check is a write decision, not a read: it must reserve margin atomically at the moment it evaluates it, or two orders can race the same stale cached value and both pass. A cache is fine for a display of margin in the UI, but the actual reserve-and-route decision must hit the authoritative, row-locked value in trading_accounts every time — exactly the same reasoning that keeps a payments platform's wallet-debit decision off any cache.
Q3. A client's order request times out and they retry 30 seconds later. How do you guarantee they don't end up with two live orders in the market?
The retry carries the same client_order_id. The unique-constraint insert on (account_id, client_order_id) means only one execution path ever owns RMS-checking and routing for that key; a retry either replays the stored terminal state (if the first attempt finished) or gets a 409 if it's still in flight. This matters more here than in a generic API: a duplicate isn't just a duplicate row, it's a second live order that could actually execute against the market.
Q4. RMS rejects an order for insufficient margin, but the client swears they had enough. What's your debugging path?
Check the margin_reservation history for the account around that timestamp — a prior order (possibly still OPEN) may have already reserved margin that hasn't been released yet, e.g., a stale reservation from a cancelled order whose release event was delayed. Cross-check trading_accounts.blocked_margin against SUM of active reservations as a self-recon; a mismatch there is a bug, not a normal condition, and should be treated as an incident exactly like ledger balance drift in a payments system.
Q5. Why is a pessimistic SELECT ... FOR UPDATE not the first choice here, given F&O margin math can be non-trivial?
Because SPAN/exposure margin computation for F&O can take real time, and holding a row lock for the duration of that computation serializes unrelated orders on the same account for longer than necessary. Doing the margin calculation first and only taking the lock for the final conditional UPDATE minimizes lock hold time; the conditional UPDATE alone is sufficient to prevent the race because the check and the decrement are one statement.

Sharding, Replication & Recovery

Q6. Why does this system shard by account_id for the transactional core but by symbol for market data — why not one scheme for everything?
They are different workloads with different natural keys. Orders/margin/funds are per-account, low-fan-out, correctness-bound — account_id co-locates everything one account needs for a local ACID transaction. Ticks are a single high-fan-in stream (one exchange feed) with a massive read fan-out to millions of subscribers, with no per-account meaning at all — symbol/instrument_token is the only sensible partition key, and the store is time-series-oriented (ClickHouse/ScyllaDB), not OLTP Postgres. Forcing one scheme onto both would either over-provision the transactional core for tick throughput or force the tick pipeline to carry ACID overhead it doesn't need.
Q7. Why active-passive rather than active-active across regions for order routing, especially compared to a payments platform where the same question comes up?
Active-active risks two regions both accepting and routing live orders for the same account during a partition. For a wallet, that risk is a divergent balance that reconciliation must repair. For order routing it can be worse: both regions could route conflicting live orders into the actual market, and once an order matches at an exchange, it cannot be undone by fixing a database row afterward — you now have a real position at a real price. We accept bounded failover downtime (active-passive, fast promotion) rather than risk that.
Q8. One of your Redis "dumb cache" instances dies mid-market-hours. What happens to reads?
Reads for the accounts on that shard fail over to another independent Redis instance (multiple instances per shard boundary, no single point of failure); since disk persistence is intentionally off, the failed instance's content isn't recovered from disk — it's simply repopulated by the async cache-populator reading current state from Postgres/the event stream. The cache is disposable by design; only the underlying database and event log are durable sources of truth.
Q9. How would you migrate one account from one shard to another with zero downtime?
Replicate the account's orders/holdings/positions/funds_ledger rows to the target shard while the source stays authoritative; once caught up, briefly freeze writes for that single account_id (a tiny blast radius, since RMS is single-writer-per-account anyway), flip the routing entry, verify available_margin/blocked_margin match on the target, then unfreeze. Only that one account sees a sub-second pause; nothing else on either shard is affected.
Q10. Why synchronous quorum replication for the orders/margin database given the latency cost, instead of async replication?
With async replication, a primary crash right after acking a commit but before it replicates can lose a committed order state or a margin release — the client believes an order is OPEN or margin is available, and that fact silently reverts on failover. For live-order state and money that's unacceptable and hard to audit after the fact. Quorum writes cost a few extra milliseconds intra-region but guarantee a committed transition survives a single node loss.

Market Data, Reconciliation & Extensions

Q11. Why doesn't the broker itself match buy and sell orders? Where exactly does matching happen?
Matching (price-time priority) happens inside the exchange's own matching engine — NEAT at NSE, and BSE's equivalent — not inside the broker. The broker's Order Management Service is a router and state machine: it validates the order, risk-checks it via RMS, and forwards it to the exchange over the Exchange Connectivity Gateway; everything after that (does this order actually trade, at what price, against whom) is decided by the exchange and reported back to the broker as acks and fills. This is different from, say, an internal dark pool or a broker that internalizes order flow — here the broker is a client of the exchange's order book, never the order book itself.
Q12. Walk me through the 2026 SEBI Algo-ID rule and how it constrains the Kite Connect API surface.
Since April 1, 2026, every algorithmic order — including ones placed via the public Kite Connect API — must carry a unique, exchange-issued Algo-ID, and the algorithm must have prior exchange approval before it can go live. Algorithms are classified as white-box (transparent, documented, self-registered) or black-box (logic hidden from the end user, in which case the provider must hold a SEBI Research Analyst license and publish periodic disclosures). Architecturally this means the Auth/API-key layer has to track which callers are enrolled as algo traders, capture their Algo-ID on order submission, and RMS/OMS must be able to reject an algo-flagged order that arrives without a valid, approved Algo-ID.
Q13. What happens if the exchange's end-of-day obligation file shows a trade your system has no record of?
That's a MISSING_INTERNAL case in reconciliation: the exchange asserts a fill happened, but the broker's own orders/trades tables don't reflect it, most likely a lost or delayed fill event. Reconciliation raises an incident, attempts to reconstruct the fill from the exchange's authoritative record, updates the account's position/holdings/ledger to match, and never silently ignores the discrepancy — a wrong guess here either creates a phantom position or leaves the client's real position unrepresented.
Q14. Your WebSocket tier restarts for a deploy at 11 AM. What stops every client from reconnecting at the exact same instant and overwhelming the tier?
Client SDKs reconnect with exponential backoff and jitter rather than an immediate retry, which spreads the reconnect load over a window instead of a single instant. The load balancer in front of the Ticker Service instances uses least-connections routing so new connections spread evenly, and each instance enforces a connection admission cap so it fails fast rather than degrading for everyone if it's approaching its limit.
Q15. A single popular stock like RELIANCE is ticking 10 times/sec and has hundreds of thousands of subscribers. How do you keep that from blowing up bandwidth?
The Ticker Service coalesces per-symbol updates to roughly one update per second per connection — a human doesn't need every micro-tick, so intermediate ticks for a hot symbol are simply superseded by the latest value before the next scheduled flush to each connection. This keeps egress bandwidth bounded regardless of how fast the underlying instrument is actually trading, at the cost of showing a slightly-lagged (sub-second) price, which is an acceptable trade-off for a display use case.
Q16. How do you keep transaction/order history reads fast without ever risking the write path?
History is served from the Redis hot cache/read replicas, populated asynchronously by consumers of the order/trade event stream; it never takes a lock that the RMS margin-reservation or OMS state-transition path depends on. If a client needs a guaranteed-fresh view (e.g., right after placing an order), the order-status endpoint can fall back to reading the authoritative row directly, but the default, high-volume history/portfolio traffic is fully decoupled from the write path.

Key Takeaways

Remember
This design has two genuinely different systems sharing one product surface, and conflating them is the most common mistake. The transactional core (orders, margin, funds) is correctness-bound and sharded by account_id: RMS's atomic conditional margin reservation is the load-bearing pattern — check-and-reserve as one indivisible statement, exactly like a wallet-debit CAS but for margin instead of cash — backed by quorum-replicated, append-only ledger legs and idempotent order handling on client_order_id. Because funds only ever move between an account's own ledger and its own bank account, this core never needs a cross-shard money saga, which is a genuine simplification versus a peer-to-peer payments platform. The market-data path (ticks) is throughput-bound and partitioned by symbol: one exchange feed in, a purpose-built low-allocation broadcast service, and a "dumb cache" read path (raw-bytes Redis GETs plus ETag/304) that lets millions of portfolio/order reads never touch Postgres. Layered on both: the broker routes and risk-checks, but never matches — that happens at the exchange — and SEBI's Algo-ID regime, client-fund-segregation rules, and the mandatory RMS gate are regulatory constraints, not optional hardening, that shape the design as much as any scaling number does.
