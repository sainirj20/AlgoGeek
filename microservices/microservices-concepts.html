Overview

Microservices architecture comes with a large vocabulary, and interviewers usually care less about whether a candidate has heard a term before than whether they understand what problem it solves, what it costs, and when it doesn't apply. This page works through 51 of the concepts that come up most often, grouped into nine categories that roughly track the lifecycle of a real system: how services are structured and decomposed, how they talk to each other, how they stay resilient under failure, how they manage data across ownership boundaries, how they get deployed and run, how they scale, how they're secured, how they're observed in production, and how teams govern and operate them day to day.

A handful of these concepts — API Gateway, Service Mesh's sidecar proxies, Circuit Breaker, Bulkhead, Saga, CQRS, Rate Limiting, Load Balancing, the Strangler Fig pattern, Configuration Management, and API Gateway authentication — already have a fuller, dedicated treatment elsewhere on this site. They're covered here concisely, as part of the complete picture, with a note that deeper coverage exists elsewhere. Everything else gets full depth in place.

Structure & Design

Service Decomposition

Service decomposition is the process of splitting a system — usually a monolith, but sometimes a greenfield design — into a set of smaller, independently deployable services along boundaries that actually mean something to the business, rather than boundaries that are merely convenient to draw. The two dominant strategies are decomposing by business capability (what the organization does — "billing," "shipping," "catalog") and decomposing by subdomain, which borrows directly from Domain-Driven Design and tends to produce sharper, more defensible boundaries than capability lists alone.

A useful sizing heuristic, popularized by Amazon, is the "two-pizza team" rule: a service should be owned end-to-end — designed, built, deployed, and operated — by a team small enough to be fed by two pizzas, roughly five to eight people. The point isn't the exact headcount; it's that ownership should be small and complete enough that the team can move independently without needing sign-off from five other groups, embodying Amazon's "you build it, you run it" philosophy where the people who write a service are also the people paged when it breaks.

Two anti-patterns show up repeatedly. The first is decomposing by technical layer instead of business capability — splitting into a "presentation service," a "business logic service," and a "data access service" — which just re-creates a distributed monolith: the same tight coupling as before, except now every layer-crossing call goes over the network, adding latency and failure modes without adding any real independence, since a single business change still ripples through all three services. The second is over-decomposition into nano-services, where the drive to keep services small goes past the point of diminishing returns.

Watch for nano-services
Slicing a system into dozens of tiny services, each responsible for a sliver of functionality too small to justify its own deployment pipeline, monitoring, and on-call rotation, trades one set of problems (a large, coupled codebase) for another (enormous operational overhead, chatty network calls for what used to be a single function call, and a service map too tangled for any one engineer to reason about). Decomposition is not free — pay for it only when the organizational and product complexity actually justifies the cost.

Domain-Driven Design (DDD) / Bounded Context

Domain-Driven Design, introduced by Eric Evans, is an approach to modeling software around the real business domain rather than around technical convenience. Its central tool for microservices architecture is the bounded context — an explicit boundary within which a particular model, and the words used to describe it, have one specific, consistent meaning. Outside that boundary, the same word can mean something entirely different, and that's not a bug, it's the point.

"Order" is the textbook example. In a Shipping bounded context, an Order is mostly a physical thing — a set of items, a weight, a destination address, a delivery date. In a Billing bounded context, that same real-world event is an invoice — a payment method, a tax calculation, a currency, a due date. Both contexts are legitimately talking about "the order," but forcing them to share one canonical "Order" model creates an anemic, over-generalized object that satisfies neither team well and couples two services that don't actually need to agree on anything beyond an order ID.

Within a bounded context, DDD insists on a ubiquitous language — a vocabulary shared between engineers and domain experts (product managers, business analysts, actual warehouse or finance staff) that's used consistently both in conversation and directly in the code — class names, method names, event names. This closes the usual gap between how the business describes a process and how the code models it, so a domain expert can read through a well-named service's core objects and roughly follow the logic.

DDD also distinguishes core subdomains (the differentiating part of the business that deserves the most engineering investment — for a payments company, that might be fraud detection or settlement), supporting subdomains (necessary but not a competitive differentiator), and generic subdomains (already-solved problems like authentication or email delivery, often better bought than built). Each subdomain is a natural candidate for its own service or small cluster of services, which is why DDD subdomain analysis is frequently used as the actual mechanism behind "decompose by business capability" rather than a competing idea. The trade-off is upfront cost: proper bounded-context modeling usually involves event-storming workshops and real time with domain experts, which is overkill for a small CRUD app but pays for itself once a system has enough complexity that getting the boundaries wrong would be expensive to unwind later.

Single Responsibility Principle (per service)

The Single Responsibility Principle is the "S" in SOLID, and in its original form it's a class-level rule: a class should have one job, and therefore only one reason to change. Applied to microservices, the same idea scales up one level of granularity — a service, not a class, should own one cohesive business capability and have one reason to change. A pricing change shouldn't require redeploying the order service; a shipping-carrier integration shouldn't live inside the payments service just because it was convenient to bolt on at the time.

The failure mode when this discipline slips is the god service — a service that started out scoped reasonably but, feature by feature, accumulated responsibilities that don't actually belong together, usually because it was the path of least resistance for whoever needed to ship something quickly. God services become organizational bottlenecks: many unrelated teams need to change and deploy the same codebase, deployments get riskier because unrelated features are coupled in the same release, and the service becomes a single point of failure for far more of the business than its name suggests. It's the microservices version of the "big ball of mud" — a monolith wearing a service's clothing.

The real skill is picking the right level of cohesion — one reason to change, not one database table or one API endpoint per service. Pushed too far, SRP at the service level collapses into the nano-services anti-pattern discussed above; applied thoughtfully, it produces the same "one team, one service" ownership model that companies like Amazon and Netflix use to let hundreds of teams ship independently without stepping on each other.

Database per Service

Database-per-service means exactly what it says: each microservice owns its own database, and no other service is permitted to read or write it directly. Any other service that needs that data must go through the owning service's API. This is the same encapsulation idea as hiding a class's private fields, just applied at the scale of a whole service and its schema.

The reason this matters is that a shared database is a form of coupling that's invisible until it breaks. If two services read and write the same tables, each one can end up silently depending on undocumented details of the schema — a column another team relies on informally, a trigger, a stored procedure someone added years ago — with nothing like a compiler or an API contract to catch a breaking change. A shared database quietly re-couples every service that touches it back into a single release train, defeating the independent deployability microservices are supposed to buy in the first place.

The obvious cost is that you lose the ability to write a single SQL join across two services' data, since it now lives in two different databases that shouldn't be queried directly from outside their owning service. Two patterns fill that gap. API composition has the querying service (or a BFF, or an aggregating gateway) call each owning service's API and stitch the results together in application code — simple to reason about, but it adds latency per call and doesn't scale well once a query needs to fan out across many services or perform real aggregation. CQRS-style read models take a different approach: a dedicated service subscribes to events published by the owning services and builds its own purpose-built, denormalized view optimized for exactly the query it needs to serve — an "order summary" view combining order, customer, and shipping data, kept in sync asynchronously rather than joined live. This trades storage duplication and eventual consistency for read performance and flexibility.

The deeper implication is on data consistency. A single business operation that used to be one ACID transaction against one database — place an order, reserve inventory, charge a card — now spans multiple independently-owned databases that can't participate in a single transaction together.

This is exactly what the Saga pattern exists for
Without a shared database, a multi-service business transaction has to be broken into a sequence of local transactions, each with a defined compensating action if a later step fails (cancel the reservation if the charge fails, refund the charge if shipping can't fulfill it). Teams that adopt database-per-service without a real plan for this — usually reaching for two-phase commit across services, which doesn't hold up well at scale — tend to rediscover the need for sagas the hard way, after an outage leaves data in a half-committed state.

Shared-Nothing Architecture

Shared-nothing is a broader architectural principle than database-per-service, even though the two are often mentioned in the same breath. Database-per-service is one specific application of shared-nothing thinking, applied to persistent storage. The full shared-nothing principle goes further: no node in the system shares any state with another node — not a database, but also not shared memory, not a shared local file system, and not a shared in-process cache. Every node is self-sufficient and communicates with others only over the network, never through some resource they both happen to have access to.

The scaling payoff is significant. Because there's no shared resource for nodes to contend over, new nodes can be added horizontally without running into the lock contention or throughput ceiling that shared-everything designs eventually hit — the classic example being a fleet of application servers all reading and writing through one central shared disk array (a SAN or NAS), which becomes both a bottleneck and a single point of failure as more servers are added to it. Shared-nothing designs, by contrast, scale close to linearly: add a node, get roughly proportional extra capacity, because there's nothing centrally shared to fight over.

In practice, following shared-nothing discipline means explicitly externalizing anything that would otherwise live conveniently in local memory — a user's session, a locally-cached lookup table, an in-process rate limiter's counters — out to a shared external store like Redis, a database, or an object store, so that any node can be added, removed, or restarted without another node needing to coordinate around what it was holding. The cost is mostly design discipline: it's tempting to keep small pieces of state in local memory for speed, and shared-nothing requires resisting that unless the state is either genuinely disposable or rebuildable. It also doesn't mean "coordinate nothing" — nodes still need to talk to each other over the network for anything a shared-memory system would get for free, which means more network hops and more failure modes to handle deliberately.

Backend for Frontend (BFF)

The Backend for Frontend pattern replaces one generic, one-size-fits-all API with a set of smaller backends, each built specifically for one type of client — one for the mobile app, one for the web app, one for a smart TV. Rather than every client consuming the same shape of data and figuring out what to do with it, each BFF exists purely to shape, aggregate, and filter the underlying domain services' responses into exactly what its one client needs.

The problem BFF solves shows up gradually. A single generic API trying to serve very different clients tends toward one of two bad outcomes: it returns a bloated, generic payload that's wasteful for a mobile client on a slow connection, or it forces the client to make many small round trips to assemble what it actually needs (a classic "chatty API"). As more client types get added, the generic API accumulates conditional logic and query parameters trying to please everyone — a `?fields=` parameter here, a `?platform=mobile` branch there — until it pleases no one particularly well and becomes hard to change safely for any single client.

Netflix and SoundCloud are the two examples most commonly credited with popularizing BFF. Netflix built a distinct backend per device family — one shaped for a TV's coarse remote-control interactions and constrained rendering, a different one for a touch-driven mobile browsing experience — because the same underlying catalog and playback services needed to be presented in genuinely different shapes depending on the device, not just styled differently on the same data.

The trade-off is real: BFFs introduce code duplication, since very similar aggregation and orchestration logic often gets rewritten across several BFFs that overlap more than anyone expects (a "get home screen" BFF for mobile and one for web frequently call the same downstream services and reshape similar data). It also means more services to build, deploy, and own. BFF earns its cost when client needs genuinely diverge — mobile vs. web vs. TV vs. a voice assistant — and is overkill when there's really only one meaningful client or all clients need nearly identical data.

Communication

Service Discovery

Service discovery is how a service instance finds the actual network location — IP and port — of another service instance it needs to call, in an environment where instances are constantly being created, destroyed, rescheduled, and scaled up or down. Hardcoding IP addresses simply doesn't work once instances are this dynamic, so something has to track "which instances of this service currently exist and are healthy" and hand that answer out on demand.

There are two structural approaches. In client-side discovery, the calling service itself queries a registry, gets back a list of currently healthy instances, and picks one (often applying its own load-balancing logic) — Netflix's Eureka, paired with the Ribbon client-side load balancer, is the canonical example of this model. In server-side discovery, the caller doesn't do any of that — it just sends its request to a well-known, stable address (a load balancer or router), which is itself responsible for querying the registry and forwarding the request to a healthy instance. AWS's Elastic Load Balancer and Kubernetes Services both work this way, keeping discovery logic out of application code entirely.

Within server- and client-side discovery, there are also different backing mechanisms. Registry-based systems like Netflix Eureka or HashiCorp Consul have each service instance register itself on startup and send periodic heartbeats; the registry prunes any instance that stops heartbeating. DNS-based discovery resolves a plain service name to an IP via ordinary DNS lookups — simple and compatible with virtually every existing tool, but traditional DNS wasn't built for the sub-second churn of containers scaling in and out, and DNS caching (TTLs) can hand back stale, already-dead instances. Kubernetes-native discovery sidesteps both: every Kubernetes Service gets a stable DNS name via kube-dns/CoreDNS that always resolves to the current set of healthy backing Pod IPs, automatically updated as Pods come and go, with kube-proxy handling the load balancing across them — which is why most services running on Kubernetes get working discovery for free without ever standing up Eureka or Consul themselves.

-- Consul/Eureka-style: a service registers itself on startup
{
  "service": {
    "name": "inventory-service",
    "address": "10.0.4.17",
    "port": 8080,
    "check": { "http": "http://10.0.4.17:8080/health", "interval": "10s" }
  }
}

-- A calling service then looks up healthy instances before calling one
GET /v1/health/service/inventory-service?passing=true

-- On Kubernetes, the same lookup is just a DNS name — no registry client needed
curl http://inventory-service.orders-namespace.svc.cluster.local/items/42

The trade-off is operational: registries like Eureka or Consul are one more piece of infrastructure that itself has to be made highly available, since if the registry is down, nothing can find anything. Kubernetes-native discovery removes that specific burden for teams already running on Kubernetes, but ties the discovery mechanism tightly to the orchestrator, which matters if a system spans multiple clusters or non-Kubernetes environments.

API Gateway

An API gateway is a single entry point that sits in front of a set of backend microservices and handles the concerns that would otherwise have to be reimplemented, inconsistently, inside every one of them: routing an incoming request to the right backend service, authenticating and authorizing the caller once at the edge rather than in every service individually, rate limiting to keep any one client from overwhelming the backend, and response aggregation — combining calls to several backend services into one response tailored for a caller, conceptually similar to a BFF but usually shared across client types rather than built per client.

Concretely, a gateway like Kong or AWS API Gateway is configured with routes that map an external path to an internal service, plus policies layered on top of that route:

routes:
  - path: /api/orders/*
    service: order-service
    plugins:
      - jwt-auth
      - rate-limit: { minute: 100 }
  - path: /api/catalog/*
    service: catalog-service
    plugins:
      - rate-limit: { minute: 500 }

This page keeps the treatment intentionally brief — a dedicated page elsewhere in this site's Microservices section, and another in its Software Architecture Patterns section, go much deeper on gateway topology, aggregation strategies, and product-by-product comparisons. The trade-off worth remembering here is that a gateway is a single front door: it centralizes cross-cutting concerns nicely, but it also becomes a shared dependency and a potential bottleneck or single point of failure for every request into the system if it isn't itself deployed redundantly.

Service Mesh

A service mesh is a dedicated infrastructure layer for service-to-service communication — the traffic that flows between services inside the system, as opposed to the traffic an API gateway manages coming in from outside it. Rather than building retry logic, timeouts, mutual TLS, and load balancing into every service's own code, a mesh attaches a small proxy — a sidecar — next to every service instance, and routes all of that instance's inbound and outbound traffic transparently through its own sidecar.

Envoy is the most widely used sidecar proxy: each service pod runs an Envoy instance alongside it, invisible to the service's own code, and a control plane configures every sidecar centrally with routing rules, TLS certificates, and traffic policy. Istio's control plane (istiod) and Linkerd's control plane both do this job, just with different proxies and different design philosophies underneath.

What a mesh adds beyond an API gateway comes down to direction of traffic: a gateway secures and manages north-south traffic (outside world into the cluster); a mesh manages east-west traffic (service-to-service, entirely inside the cluster) — automatically encrypting all internal calls with mutual TLS, applying a consistent retry and timeout policy across every service-to-service call without each team reimplementing it, enabling fine-grained traffic shifting for canary releases or A/B testing between service versions, and producing per-hop tracing and metrics with zero code changes in any service.

Istio and Linkerd are the two most commonly cited implementations, and they differ in a genuine, practical way. Istio uses Envoy as its sidecar and offers the richest, most configurable feature set — complex routing rules, traffic mirroring, deep ecosystem integration — but has a reputation for being heavier to operate and steeper to learn. Linkerd was built around a "keep it simple and fast" philosophy, uses its own lightweight Rust-based micro-proxy instead of Envoy, and is generally lower-overhead and faster to get running, at the cost of some of Istio's more advanced routing capabilities.

A mesh isn't free
Every call now passes through an extra proxy hop — typically low single-digit milliseconds, but non-zero — and running a control plane plus a sidecar per instance across an entire fleet is real, ongoing operational overhead. It tends to pay off once an organization has enough services calling each other that hand-rolled retry, TLS, and observability logic per service is genuinely painful; it's usually not worth adopting before that point.
A service mesh: sidecar proxies handle all traffic between services, configured by a central control plane.

Synchronous Communication (REST, gRPC)

Synchronous communication means the calling service sends a request and blocks — pauses and waits — until a response comes back before it does anything else. REST and gRPC are the two protocols that dominate synchronous service-to-service calls today, and while both are "synchronous," they make very different trade-offs underneath.

REST typically sends JSON over HTTP/1.1: human-readable, trivially testable with curl, Postman, or a browser's dev tools, and callable directly from a browser with no special tooling — which is exactly why it's the default choice for public APIs and anything a browser needs to talk to. gRPC sends Protocol Buffers (protobuf) — a compact binary serialization format — over HTTP/2, which gives it smaller payloads, lower latency, and native support for streaming (client-streaming, server-streaming, and full bidirectional streaming), making it a common choice for high-volume internal service-to-service calls, notably inside Google (where it originated) and widely within Netflix's and Square's internal service layers.

// order.proto — a gRPC service contract
syntax = "proto3";

service OrderService {
  rpc GetOrder (OrderRequest) returns (OrderResponse);
}

message OrderRequest {
  string order_id = 1;
}

message OrderResponse {
  string order_id = 1;
  string status = 2;
  double total_amount = 3;
}

The cost of gRPC's efficiency is accessibility: its binary payloads aren't readable off the wire without the shared .proto contract, and browsers can't natively speak the raw HTTP/2 framing gRPC needs, so browser clients typically require grpc-web plus a translating proxy in front of the service. That combination makes gRPC a poor fit for public third-party APIs, where broad tooling support and easy debugging by external partners matter more than shaving milliseconds — REST/JSON remains the sensible default there, while gRPC earns its complexity for internal, latency-sensitive, high-throughput calls between services you control on both ends.

Asynchronous Messaging

Asynchronous messaging replaces a direct, blocking call with a message that passes through a broker: the producing service publishes a message and moves on immediately, without waiting for whoever eventually handles it. The broker durably holds the message until a consumer is ready, and the consumer processes it at its own pace — a slow or temporarily unavailable consumer never blocks the producer the way a slow synchronous dependency would.

Kafka and RabbitMQ are the two brokers most often named in this context, and they represent somewhat different philosophies. Kafka is a distributed, log-based broker built for high-throughput, ordered, replayable streams of events — messages aren't deleted the moment they're read, they persist for a configured retention period, which lets multiple independent consumers replay the same stream from different points, making it a strong fit for event sourcing and analytics pipelines. RabbitMQ is a more traditional broker built around flexible routing — exchanges and bindings that can fan a message out, filter it, or route it to exactly one worker — and tends to show up more in work-queue and command-style messaging between services, where a task needs to land with exactly one worker rather than be replayed as a historical stream.

The cost of this decoupling is eventual consistency and reduced traceability. Data across services is only eventually correct rather than instantly correct, since each consumer catches up on its own schedule, and debugging becomes harder because a single business event's effects are now scattered across several services reacting over time rather than living in one linear call stack — understanding "what actually happened for this order" means correlating logs and traces across multiple services instead of reading a single stack trace. That's the real trade-off against synchronous calls: synchronous is simple to follow but couples services in time and in availability; asynchronous messaging decouples them at the cost of that same simplicity.

Synchronous calls block waiting for a response; asynchronous messaging lets the sender move on immediately.

Publish/Subscribe Pattern

Publish/subscribe is a messaging pattern where a producer — the publisher — emits an event without knowing or caring whether anyone is listening, and any number of independent consumers — subscribers — can each receive their own copy of it. It's a meaningfully different shape from a simple point-to-point queue, where one message is consumed by exactly one consumer: with competing consumers pulling from the same plain queue, once one of them takes a message, it's gone for the rest. In pub/sub, the same message is broadcast to every subscriber, and each subscriber tracks its own independent position in the stream — one subscriber falling behind or being slow to process has no effect on any other subscriber's view of what's happened.

A concrete example makes this concrete: an "order placed" event is published once, to one topic, and three completely independent services subscribe to it — the inventory service reserves stock, the shipping service schedules a shipment, and the notification service sends a confirmation email. None of the three knows the others exist, none of them blocks the others, and a fourth subscriber (say, a fraud-review service added a year later) can start listening to the exact same event stream without the producer or any existing subscriber changing a single line of code. Kafka topics with independent consumer groups, and RabbitMQ's fanout and topic exchanges, are the concrete mechanisms most systems use to implement this.

// order.placed event — published once, consumed independently by N subscribers
{
  "eventType": "order.placed",
  "orderId": "ord_9F21",
  "customerId": "cus_884",
  "items": [{ "sku": "SKU-1042", "qty": 2 }],
  "totalAmount": 58.40,
  "occurredAt": "2026-07-17T14:02:31Z"
}

The trade-off is discoverability rather than performance: because subscribers are decoupled and unaware of one another by design, it becomes genuinely easy to lose track of who actually depends on a given event in a large system — an implicit coupling forms across every team that subscribes to a topic, and unlike a REST API's explicit contract, there's no simple way to "find all callers" of an event without deliberate cataloging (a schema registry, event catalog, or team discipline around documenting subscriptions).

Request/Response vs. Event-Driven Patterns

Pulling the synchronous and asynchronous discussion together into a decision: request/response is the right shape whenever the caller genuinely needs an answer before it can proceed — checking whether a payment authorized before showing the user a success screen, fetching a profile to render a page, validating a coupon code at checkout. Event-driven is the right shape whenever the caller doesn't need an answer at all, just needs to notify the rest of the system that something happened and let independent downstream consumers react whenever they get to it — an order being placed, a user finishing sign-up, a shipment being marked delivered.

A simple test for which one fits: does the caller need to act differently right now depending on the result? If yes, that's request/response, synchronous or otherwise. Does the system just need to durably record that something happened and let others react eventually, on their own schedule? That's event-driven. It's rarely an either/or choice for an entire system — it's a per-interaction choice made deliberately, interaction by interaction.

Most real systems mix both intentionally within a single business flow. A checkout, for example, typically calls payment authorization synchronously — the user is staring at a spinner, genuinely waiting to know whether the charge worked — while everything that follows a successful charge (updating inventory, generating a shipping label, sending a confirmation email, feeding analytics) fires asynchronously as events, because none of it needs to hold up the response the user is waiting on. When that mixed flow spans multiple services and needs to stay consistent even if a later async step fails, that's precisely the gap the Saga pattern is designed to close, breaking the overall operation into local transactions with compensating actions rather than relying on one all-or-nothing transaction across services.

Resilience & Reliability

Circuit Breaker

A circuit breaker is a gatekeeper placed in front of a network call to a dependency — it tracks recent successes and failures, and once failures cross a threshold, it stops sending traffic to that dependency for a while instead of letting every caller queue up waiting on a service that's already struggling. It runs as a state machine with three states: closed (the healthy default, where requests flow through while the breaker quietly counts outcomes over a rolling window), open (once too many recent calls have failed, the breaker trips and fails fast — returning an error or fallback immediately rather than making the real call), and half-open (after a cool-down period, a small number of test requests are let through to check whether the dependency has recovered before fully reopening the flow).

Libraries like Resilience4j (the modern standard for Java) and the now-retired Netflix Hystrix implement this pattern directly, and service meshes such as Istio/Envoy can apply it at the network layer with no application code changes at all. The problem a circuit breaker solves is cascading failure: in a call chain of several services, a single slow dependency that doesn't fail fast can tie up threads or connections in every caller above it, and that congestion can climb the chain until parts of the system with nothing to do with the original problem start failing too. The main pitfall is tuning — too sensitive a threshold and the breaker trips on ordinary blips; too lenient and it never trips before the cascade has already happened. (This site's dedicated Circuit Breaker Pattern page, under Software Architecture Patterns, walks through the full state machine and a worked payment-charging example in more depth.)

Retry & Timeout Patterns

Retries and timeouts are two of the most basic resilience tools available, and they're almost always used together. A timeout puts a hard limit on how long a caller will wait for a response from a dependency — if the dependency doesn't answer within, say, 500ms, the caller gives up and moves on rather than blocking indefinitely. A retry then gives the caller a second (or third) chance to succeed after a failure, on the theory that many real-world failures are transient — a dropped packet, a momentary blip in a load balancer, a brief garbage-collection pause on the other end — and will very likely succeed if simply tried again a moment later.

Done naively, retries are dangerous rather than helpful. If a dependency is failing because it's overloaded, and every caller immediately retries every failed call, the retries themselves add more load to an already-struggling service — a feedback loop known as a retry storm that can turn a minor slowdown into a full outage. This gets worse at scale: if thousands of client instances all retry on the same fixed schedule, they can also synchronize into damaging traffic spikes that hit the dependency at the exact same instant, right as it's trying to recover.

The standard fix is exponential backoff with jitter: each successive retry waits longer than the last (typically doubling the delay, capped at some maximum), and a small random amount of jitter is added to each wait so that many callers retrying around the same time don't all land on the exact same instant.

delay = min(cap, base * 2^attempt)
delay = random_between(0, delay)   // full jitter
sleep(delay)
retry()

Even with backoff and jitter, retries are only safe to perform automatically if the operation is idempotent — running it twice must produce the same result as running it once. Timeouts play the complementary role on the other side of the same problem: without one, a caller can end up holding a thread, a connection, or a memory buffer open indefinitely waiting on a dependency that will never respond, and enough of those held resources can exhaust the caller's own capacity, turning someone else's slow dependency into your own outage.

Idempotency is a precondition, not a nice-to-have
Never wrap a non-idempotent call in an automatic retry without deduplication in place — an idempotency key or unique request ID the downstream service checks before executing. Otherwise a retry after an ambiguous failure (the first call may have actually succeeded before the response was lost) can silently duplicate a side effect like a payment, a shipment, or an email send.

Bulkhead Pattern

The Bulkhead pattern borrows its name and logic directly from ship design: a ship's hull is divided into watertight compartments so that if one compartment floods, the water is contained there and the rest of the ship stays afloat. Applied to software, a bulkhead means partitioning the resources a service uses to call its various dependencies — most commonly thread pools or connection pools — so that a single overwhelmed or slow dependency can only exhaust the slice of resources dedicated to it, not the resources every other dependency also needs.

Without bulkheads, a service that shares one common thread pool across all its outbound calls is exposed to a single point of failure: if a low-priority dependency (say, a recommendations or logging service) starts hanging, it can quietly consume every available thread in the shared pool, leaving nothing for calls to critical dependencies like the payment or inventory service — even though those are completely healthy. With bulkheads in place — a separate, sized pool per dependency — that same hang only starves calls to the one problem dependency, and everything else keeps working normally.

Resilience4j implements this directly as a configurable bulkhead alongside its circuit breaker support, and the two are typically deployed together: the bulkhead limits how much damage a hung dependency can do while it's failing, and the breaker stops sending it traffic once failures cross a threshold. The trade-off is upfront capacity planning — every isolated pool has to be sized well enough for its own normal load, and pools that are too small will throttle healthy traffic unnecessarily, so bulkheading trades some resource-utilization efficiency for fault isolation. (A dedicated Bulkhead Pattern page under Software Architecture Patterns covers thread-pool vs. semaphore isolation strategies and pool sizing in more depth.)

Bulkhead pattern: isolated thread pools contain a struggling dependency instead of letting it starve every other call.

Rate Limiting / Throttling

Rate limiting (also called throttling) caps how many requests a caller is allowed to make in a given time window, rejecting or delaying anything past that cap rather than letting a consumer overwhelm whatever it's calling. The two classic algorithms are the token bucket (a bucket refills with tokens at a fixed rate, and each request consumes one token, so short bursts are allowed as long as tokens are available) and the leaky bucket (requests queue up and are processed — or "leak out" — at a strictly constant rate, smoothing bursts rather than permitting them). This site has a dedicated deep-dive on rate limiter design elsewhere, so the mechanics are only sketched here.

What's worth emphasizing in a microservices context specifically is that rate limiting isn't just something applied at the public API gateway to protect against abusive external clients — it matters just as much, if not more, between internal services. A single internal service with a bug (a retry loop gone wrong, a batch job that fans out too aggressively) can overwhelm a downstream internal dependency just as easily as an external attacker could, and because internal callers are usually trusted, that kind of self-inflicted overload is easy to overlook until it takes down a shared dependency — a database or a search index — that half the system relies on. Well-run microservices architectures apply per-client rate limits at internal service boundaries too, often paired with bulkheads and circuit breakers, so one noisy internal caller can't quietly starve a shared resource for everyone else.

Graceful Degradation

Graceful degradation is the practice of designing a system so that when a non-critical dependency fails, it serves a reduced, cached, or default version of the experience instead of returning a hard error to the user. The core idea is prioritization: not every feature on a page or in a workflow is equally important, and a system that treats a failure in a "nice to have" feature the same way it treats a failure in a "must have" feature is wasting an opportunity to keep users happy.

The canonical example is an e-commerce product page that shows personalized recommendations ("customers who bought this also bought...") alongside the core product information. If the personalization service that generates those recommendations goes down, a gracefully degraded page doesn't throw an error or go blank — it falls back to something reasonable instead: recommendations from a cached response from the last time the service was healthy, a generic "popular items" list computed offline and refreshed periodically, or simply omitting the recommendations module entirely while the rest of the page (price, description, add-to-cart button) loads and works normally. The customer can still complete their purchase; they just don't see a personalized upsell that day.

Implementing graceful degradation usually means every call to a non-critical dependency is wrapped in fallback logic — often the same mechanism a circuit breaker returns when it's open — and it frequently leans on feature flags so a whole feature can be disabled instantly and deliberately during an incident, without a code deploy, rather than waiting for it to fail dependency-by-dependency. Distinguishing "critical" from "non-critical" dependencies ahead of time, during design, is most of the work; degrading gracefully in response to a failure nobody anticipated as optional is much harder to do well in the moment.

Degradation can hide problems if you don't watch for it
A service quietly serving stale cached recommendations for hours looks, on a pure uptime dashboard, identical to one working perfectly. Without explicit alerting on how often the fallback path is being taken — and for how long — a team can remain unaware that a dependency has been down and degraded for days until someone notices the recommendations haven't changed in a week.

Health Checks / Liveness & Readiness Probes

A health check is an endpoint or mechanism a service exposes so that something else — a load balancer, an orchestrator, a monitoring system — can ask, in a lightweight and automatic way, "are you working correctly right now?" rather than waiting to notice failures indirectly through user complaints or error-rate dashboards. In a microservices architecture running dozens or hundreds of independently deployed instances, health checks are what let infrastructure automatically route around unhealthy instances and restart broken ones without a human having to notice and intervene.

Kubernetes (and most modern orchestrators) actually distinguish between two different questions, because conflating them causes real operational problems. A liveness probe asks "is this process still alive and functioning, or is it stuck in a way it will never recover from on its own?" — a failed liveness check tells the orchestrator to kill and restart the container, on the assumption that a restart is the only way to recover. A readiness probe asks a narrower, more frequent question: "is this instance ready to receive traffic right now?" — a failed readiness check simply removes the instance from the load balancer's rotation without killing it, which is exactly what you want during a slow startup (still loading a cache, waiting on a database connection pool to warm up) or a temporary overload, where restarting the process would only make things worse.

Chaos Engineering

Chaos engineering is the practice of deliberately injecting failure into a system — often in production, against real traffic — in order to discover weaknesses before those weaknesses cause a real, unplanned incident. It inverts the usual instinct to avoid failure at all costs: teams intentionally cause small, controlled failures on a regular basis, on the theory that a distributed system's resilience mechanisms (retries, circuit breakers, bulkheads, failover) are only proven to actually work once they've been exercised by a real failure — and it's far better to trigger that failure yourself, during business hours, with everyone watching, than to have it happen unannounced at 3am during a genuine outage.

Netflix pioneered the discipline and remains its best-known practitioner. Chaos Monkey, the original tool, randomly terminates production instances during business hours, forcing every service to prove it can tolerate the sudden loss of any single instance. Netflix later built out a wider Simian Army of related tools, each simulating a different failure mode — Latency Monkey injected artificial delay into network calls, Conformity Monkey found instances that didn't follow best practices, and Chaos Gorilla and Chaos Kong escalated the idea from killing single instances to taking down an entire availability zone or region, to validate that Netflix's regional failover actually worked under a real, large-scale event rather than just on paper.

Doing this responsibly is a discipline, not just "randomly break things." Teams start by defining the system's steady state — the normal, healthy metrics (error rate, latency, throughput) they'd expect to see — and form a specific hypothesis: "if we kill one instance of the recommendations service, checkout latency should be unaffected because of the circuit breaker and fallback we built." They then run a controlled experiment, often called a game day, injecting the failure in a limited blast radius (a small percentage of traffic, a single availability zone, a quiet time window) while closely monitoring the system, and comparing what actually happened against the hypothesis — if checkout latency spiked anyway, that's a real gap in the resilience design that was just found safely, instead of during an actual outage.

The obvious trade-off is risk: deliberately injecting failure into a live production system is inherently dangerous if done carelessly, which is why mature chaos engineering practice insists on tight blast-radius controls, an easy kill switch to abort the experiment immediately, and testing first against staging or a small traffic slice before ever touching full production. It's also not a good starting point for a system that doesn't already have solid monitoring and basic resilience patterns in place — chaos engineering is meant to validate resilience mechanisms that already exist, not substitute for building them in the first place.

Data Management

Saga Pattern

A saga is a way of managing a business transaction that spans multiple services — and therefore multiple separate databases — without relying on a single all-or-nothing distributed transaction. Instead, the overall operation is broken into a sequence of local transactions, each executed and committed independently by a single service, and if a step partway through fails, the saga runs compensating transactions to semantically undo whatever the earlier steps already committed, rather than rolling back a shared transaction that never existed in the first place.

Sagas can be coordinated two ways. In choreography, there's no central coordinator — each service publishes an event when it finishes its step, and the next service in line reacts to that event and does its own work, so the flow emerges from a chain of services each reacting to the last. In orchestration, a dedicated orchestrator service explicitly tells each participant what to do next and tracks the overall state of the saga, trading some coupling to the orchestrator for a single place to see and reason about the whole flow.

A typical example is placing an order that touches a payment service and an inventory service: reserve inventory, charge the payment method, then confirm the order. If the payment step fails after inventory was already reserved, the saga doesn't have a database transaction to roll back — instead it triggers a compensating action, releasing the previously reserved inventory, so the system ends up in a consistent state even though nothing was ever locked across services. (This site's dedicated Saga Pattern page under Software Architecture Patterns goes deeper on failure ordering, compensating-transaction design, and orchestration frameworks.)

Saga coordination styles: choreography (services react to each other's events) vs. orchestration (a central coordinator directs each step).

Event Sourcing

Event sourcing is a way of persisting data where, instead of storing only the current state of an entity (a bank account's current balance, a user's current address), the system stores the complete, ordered, immutable sequence of events that led to that state — "AccountOpened," "FundsDeposited," "FundsWithdrawn" — and treats the current state as something derived, not something stored directly. This is a genuine inversion of how most applications are built: a traditional CRUD system overwrites a row when something changes, and the history of how it got there is gone unless it happened to be logged separately; an event-sourced system never overwrites anything, it only ever appends a new event.

To get the current state of an entity, the system replays every event for that entity, in order, applying each one to rebuild the state from scratch — a bank account's balance is simply the sum of every deposit and withdrawal event ever recorded for it. Replaying potentially thousands of events every time state is needed is expensive, so real systems periodically take a snapshot (the fully computed state as of some point) and only replay events that occurred after it, rather than replaying the entire history every time.

The major benefit is a complete, tamper-evident audit trail that falls out of the design for free — regulators and auditors in domains like banking and payments often want exactly this kind of record — plus the ability to do "time-travel debugging," reconstructing exactly what the system's state looked like at any point in the past, or replaying events into a different model entirely to answer a question nobody thought to ask when the system was first built. It also pairs naturally with CQRS, since the event log becomes the single source of truth that one or more read-optimized projections can be built from.

The cost is real complexity that a simple CRUD model doesn't have to deal with. Event schemas change over time as the business evolves, and because old events are immutable and can never be rewritten, the system has to keep being able to interpret every version of every event type it has ever emitted — commonly handled with upcasting (transforming an old event shape into the current shape on read) or versioned event types living side by side. Deleting or correcting data also becomes awkward — you can't just delete a customer's history for a privacy request, since the whole point is that events are immutable, so compliant systems need a deliberate strategy (like crypto-shredding, where the event body is encrypted and only the key is later deleted) to reconcile "immutable log" with "right to be forgotten."

CQRS (Command Query Responsibility Segregation)

CQRS splits an application's write path from its read path into two separate models instead of using one model for both. Commands (writes that change state) go through a model optimized for enforcing business rules and consistency, while queries (reads) go through a separate model — often a different, denormalized data store entirely — optimized purely for how the data actually needs to be displayed or queried, without worrying about write-side concerns like validation or transactional integrity.

The two models are kept in sync asynchronously: a write updates the write-side store and publishes an event describing what changed, and a separate process consumes that event to update the read-side projection, meaning the read model is generally eventually consistent with the write model rather than instantly consistent. This is exactly why CQRS is so frequently paired with Event Sourcing: the event log that event sourcing already produces as its source of truth is a natural feed for building and rebuilding one or more read-side projections, and if a new query pattern is needed later, a new projection can be built by replaying the existing event history rather than migrating a live schema.

A concrete example is an e-commerce catalog: the write model enforces rules like "a SKU can't go negative" when inventory changes, while the read model that powers the product listing page is a heavily denormalized, cache-friendly view (product, price, and stock data joined into one document) that would be wasteful to compute from the normalized write-side schema on every single page view. (See this site's dedicated CQRS page under Software Architecture Patterns for a deeper treatment of projection rebuilding and when CQRS is overkill for a simpler CRUD use case.)

CQRS: writes and reads flow through separate models, kept in sync asynchronously via events.

Eventual Consistency

Eventual consistency is a consistency model that makes a specific, narrower promise than it sounds like it's making: if no new writes happen to a piece of data, all replicas of that data will eventually converge to the same value. Notice what it doesn't promise — there's no bound on how long "eventually" takes (it could be milliseconds or, under a bad network partition, much longer), and it says nothing about what value a read returns in the meantime, which could be stale.

This sits in direct contrast to strong consistency, where every read is guaranteed to return the most recent write no matter which replica handles it — achieved by having replicas coordinate, through consensus or synchronous replication, before a write is considered complete. Strong consistency is easier to reason about but comes at the cost of latency and availability, since a replica has to wait to confirm with others before it can respond; eventual consistency drops that coordination requirement, so writes can be acknowledged immediately and reads can be served by whichever replica is closest or least loaded, at the cost of that replica occasionally answering with older data.

A concrete, everyday example is DNS: when a domain's IP address is updated, that change doesn't take effect everywhere instantly — it propagates through a hierarchy of caching resolvers over a period defined by the record's TTL, so different users around the world can legitimately see the old and new IP address at the same time, and DNS makes no promise about exactly when everyone converges, only that they eventually will. Distributed databases like Cassandra and Amazon DynamoDB (in its default configuration) apply the same idea deliberately: a write is acknowledged once it's durable on a quorum of replicas, and it propagates to the rest asynchronously, trading a small, bounded window of staleness for lower write latency and continued availability during network trouble.

The real-world pitfall shows up in application logic that assumes strong consistency without realizing it. A user who updates their profile picture and immediately reloads the page might briefly see the old picture if their read gets routed to a replica that hasn't caught up yet — a problem specifically called the "read-your-own-writes" problem — and systems that care about it either route a user's subsequent reads back to the replica that handled their write, or hide the delay behind an optimistic UI update showing the new value locally while the backend catches up. Designing for eventual consistency well means deliberately choosing which parts of the system can tolerate staleness (a like count, a view count, a recommendations feed) and which genuinely can't (an account balance, an inventory count that must never oversell) rather than applying it uniformly everywhere.

Two-Phase Commit

Two-phase commit (2PC) is a protocol for making a single transaction succeed or fail atomically across multiple separate databases or resource managers, coordinated by a central transaction coordinator. It gets its name from its two distinct phases: in the prepare (voting) phase, the coordinator asks every participant "can you commit this transaction?", and each participant does whatever work is needed to guarantee it can commit if asked — validating the change, acquiring locks, writing to a durable log — before replying yes or no, without actually committing yet.

In the commit phase, if every participant voted yes, the coordinator tells all of them to commit for real; if even one voted no (or didn't respond), the coordinator tells everyone to abort instead, so the transaction is genuinely all-or-nothing across every participant. This delivers a strong guarantee — real atomicity across multiple databases, which is exactly what you'd want for a bank transferring money between two accounts held in different databases.

The blocking coordinator problem
If the coordinator crashes after participants vote yes but before it sends the final commit/abort instruction, every participant is left holding its locks indefinitely, unable to independently decide whether to commit or roll back. This is a real single point of failure, not a hypothetical one — and it's the main reason 2PC is considered risky, not just slow.

This is precisely why 2PC is considered a poor fit for microservices and is much less common there than in traditional monolithic or tightly-coupled systems: microservices are deliberately designed so each service owns its own database and its own transaction boundary, and 2PC fundamentally requires participants to hold a shared lock and defer their commit decision to an external coordinator across a network boundary — exactly the kind of tight coupling and availability risk (one slow or unreachable participant blocks everyone else's transaction) that microservices architecture tries to avoid in the first place. This is exactly the gap the Saga pattern was designed to fill: instead of one blocking, coordinator-managed atomic transaction, a saga accepts that each service commits its own local transaction independently and immediately, and handles failure after the fact through compensating transactions rather than by never letting anyone commit until everyone agrees.

Change Data Capture (CDC)

Change Data Capture (CDC) is a technique for detecting and publishing every insert, update, and delete that happens to a database, as a stream of events, by reading directly from the database's own internal transaction log — its write-ahead log (Postgres), binlog (MySQL), or equivalent — rather than by having application code explicitly publish an event every time it writes. Because CDC reads the log the database was already going to write anyway as part of committing a transaction, it captures every change made to a table, including changes made by other applications, scripts, or direct SQL that never went through the code path a developer might have remembered to instrument.

Debezium is the dominant open-source implementation of this idea in practice: it runs as a Kafka Connect connector, attaches to a database's replication log the same way a native replica would, and turns each committed change into a structured event (what table, what row, the before and after values) published onto a Kafka topic, where any number of downstream consumers can subscribe to it independently of the source application. Because it reads the transaction log rather than polling the tables themselves, CDC has minimal impact on the source database's performance and sees changes essentially the instant they're committed rather than on some polling interval.

CDC solves a specific and important problem: without it, keeping other systems in sync with a database's changes requires either polling for changes (slow, and easy to miss deletes) or having application code dual-write — updating its own database and separately publishing an event — which introduces the classic dual-write problem, since those two writes aren't atomic and one can succeed while the other fails. This is exactly why CDC is so often paired with the Outbox pattern: an application writes its business change and an outbox event both in one local database transaction, and CDC then tails that same database's log to reliably relay the outbox events onward to Kafka or another broker — CDC becomes the relay mechanism, so the application never has to publish to a separate messaging system directly at all.

The trade-offs are mostly operational. Standing up and running Debezium and Kafka Connect is real infrastructure to own — someone has to manage connector configuration, monitor replication lag, and handle the initial snapshot problem (when a connector first attaches to a large existing table, it has to take a full snapshot of current data before it can start streaming incremental changes, which can be slow and resource-intensive). Schema changes on the source table also ripple downstream to every consumer of the CDC stream, so schema evolution has to be managed carefully (often with a schema registry), and consumers need to be built to handle occasional duplicate or out-of-order events, since most CDC pipelines offer at-least-once rather than strict exactly-once delivery.

Outbox Pattern

The dual-write problem is at the heart of why the Outbox pattern exists: whenever a service needs to both update its own database and publish an event about that change to something like Kafka or RabbitMQ, those are two separate writes to two completely different systems, and there is no way to make them atomic using an ordinary database transaction — the database commit can succeed while the message broker publish fails (or the reverse), leaving the database and the event stream permanently out of sync with no automatic way to detect or fix it.

The Outbox pattern solves this by turning two writes into one. Instead of publishing directly to the message broker, the service writes the event as a row into an "outbox" table that lives in the very same local database as the business data — and since both the business table update and the outbox row insert happen inside a single local database transaction, they're atomic by construction: either both commit together or neither does, with no window where one succeeded and the other didn't.

BEGIN;

UPDATE orders
   SET status = 'CONFIRMED'
 WHERE order_id = 12345;

INSERT INTO outbox (id, aggregate_type, aggregate_id, event_type, payload, created_at)
VALUES (gen_uuid(), 'Order', 12345, 'OrderConfirmed', '{"orderId":12345,...}', now());

COMMIT;

Writing the event locally solves the atomicity problem, but the event still needs to actually reach the broker — that's the job of a separate relay process. The relay can either poll the outbox table for new, unpublished rows and publish them one by one, or, more commonly in modern systems, a CDC tool like Debezium tails the database's own transaction log and picks up the outbox table's inserts as they're committed, publishing each one onward without the application ever calling the message broker directly. Either relay approach typically delivers at-least-once semantics — the relay might publish the same event twice if it crashes and retries after a row is published but before it's marked as done — so downstream consumers of outbox-sourced events need to be built to handle duplicate delivery, usually by making their own event processing idempotent, the same idempotency requirement that shows up everywhere else retries and at-least-once delivery are involved.

Outbox pattern: the business update and the outbox event commit atomically in one local transaction, then a relay delivers the event onward.

Deployment & Infrastructure

Containerization (Docker)

A container packages an application together with everything it needs to run — code, runtime, system libraries, and configuration — into a single, portable unit that behaves identically no matter where it's deployed. The point worth getting exactly right in an interview: a container is not a lightweight virtual machine. A VM virtualizes an entire computer, including its own guest operating system and kernel, which a hypervisor then runs on top of the host's hardware — every VM carries the weight of a full OS. A container instead virtualizes at the operating-system level: it shares the host machine's kernel and leans on OS features (Linux namespaces for isolation, cgroups for resource limits) to make a process believe it has its own filesystem, network stack, and process tree, when in reality it's just an ordinary process running alongside others on the same kernel. That's why containers start in milliseconds instead of the tens of seconds a VM needs to boot, and why a single host can pack in far more containers than VMs for the same hardware.

Containers exist largely to kill the "works on my machine" problem — the frustrating gap between a developer's laptop, a CI runner, and a production server, where subtly different library versions, OS patches, or environment variables cause code that works perfectly in one place to fail in another. Docker solves this by baking the exact runtime, dependencies, and configuration a piece of software needs directly into an image, so the same image runs identically whether it's launched on a laptop, a test cluster, or a fleet of production servers. Nothing about the target machine's own installed software matters anymore — the container brings its whole environment with it.

Mechanically, a Docker image is built from a Dockerfile, a plain-text recipe of instructions, where each instruction produces a cached, reusable layer — which is why rebuilding an image after a small code change is usually fast, since only the changed layers need to be rebuilt. Built images are stored in and pulled from registries (Docker Hub, Amazon ECR, Google Artifact Registry), and docker run starts a container from an image as an isolated process, while tools like Docker Compose spin up several linked containers together for local development.

FROM node:20-slim
WORKDIR /app
COPY package*.json ./
RUN npm ci --omit=dev
COPY . .
EXPOSE 3000
CMD ["node", "server.js"]

The trade-off is that a shared kernel is a weaker isolation boundary than a VM's dedicated one — a kernel-level vulnerability or a container "escape" can, in principle, expose the host or other containers on it, so containers alone aren't treated as a hard security boundary for genuinely untrusted multi-tenant workloads. There's also an ongoing hygiene cost: base images accumulate bloat and unpatched packages over time if teams don't deliberately choose slim or distroless base images and rebuild regularly, which is why image scanning is a standard part of most container CI pipelines.

Orchestration (Kubernetes)

Running one container is easy — docker run starts it, and that's the whole job. Running hundreds of containers, across dozens of machines, in a way that survives crashes, traffic spikes, and constant deployments is a different problem entirely, and that's the gap an orchestrator like Kubernetes fills. Beyond simply starting containers, an orchestrator adds scheduling (deciding which machine in a cluster should actually run a given container, based on available CPU/memory), self-healing (noticing a container crashed or a health check is failing and automatically restarting or replacing it, with no human paged), service discovery (letting one service find and call another even as individual container instances come and go and their IP addresses change constantly), rolling updates (swapping old versions for new ones gradually, watching health checks along the way), and auto-scaling (adding or removing instances of a service as demand changes). Hand-rolling all of this with scripts is exactly the kind of undifferentiated, error-prone infrastructure work orchestration is meant to eliminate.

Kubernetes organizes all of this around a small set of core objects. A Pod is the smallest deployable unit — one or more containers that are scheduled together, share a network address and storage, and live and die as a single unit (usually just one main application container per pod, sometimes paired with a sidecar). A Deployment is a declarative statement of desired state — "I want 5 replicas of this pod running this exact image" — and Kubernetes' control loop continuously compares that desired state against what's actually running, creating, replacing, or removing pods until reality matches the declaration; this is also what drives rolling updates, since a Deployment update just changes the desired image version and Kubernetes migrates pods over gradually. A Service gives that constantly-churning set of pods a single, stable network name and load-balanced entry point, so callers never need to track individual pod IPs that change every time a pod is rescheduled.

    spec:
      containers:
        - name: order-service

The trade-off is real operational complexity: Kubernetes has a genuinely steep learning curve, a large and YAML-heavy configuration surface (Deployments, Services, Ingress, ConfigMaps, RBAC policies, and more), and its own failure modes to operate and monitor. Managed offerings — Amazon EKS, Google GKE, Azure AKS — remove the burden of running the control plane itself, but the application-level complexity of designing correct Deployments, Services, and autoscaling policy remains, which is why teams below a certain scale sometimes find a simpler platform (or a single well-configured VM group) gets them further with less overhead than adopting Kubernetes prematurely.

Blue-Green Deployment

Blue-green deployment works by running two identical, full production environments at once — conventionally called "blue" and "green" — where only one is actually serving live traffic at any given moment. If blue is currently live, green sits idle, provisioned with exactly the same infrastructure and configuration as blue but running the new version of the application.

The new version is deployed entirely into the idle environment and can be fully tested and validated there — smoke tests, health checks, even real internal traffic — without any live customer ever touching it. Once the team is confident it's healthy, the cutover itself is a single, near-instant switch: the router or load balancer in front of both environments is repointed from blue to green, typically just a change to a load balancer's target group, a DNS record, or an ALB routing rule, and 100% of traffic starts hitting the new version at once.

The biggest advantage is rollback speed: because the old environment (blue) is left running, untouched, immediately after cutover, rolling back from a bad release is just flipping the router back — no redeploy, no waiting for a broken build to be reverted and rebuilt. The cost is that you're running double the infrastructure, at least temporarily, since both the old and new full production environments need to exist simultaneously during the overlap window, which can meaningfully raise the bill for anything beyond a short validation period.

The database is the part that isn't instant
Blue-green deployment cleanly solves the application-layer cutover, but most systems share a single database between blue and green rather than running two. That means any schema change has to stay backward- and forward-compatible with both the old and new application code during the switch window — you generally can't just drop a column or rename a table as part of the same release, or you'll break whichever environment you might need to roll back to.

Canary Releases

A canary release takes a different shape entirely: instead of an instant full cutover, traffic is shifted to the new version gradually, in small increasing steps — commonly something like 5% of traffic first, then 25%, then 50%, then finally 100% — while error rates, latency, and other health signals are watched closely at each step before ramping further. The name comes from the old mining practice of bringing a canary underground as an early warning for dangerous gas; here, the small slice of users on the new version serves the same purpose, surfacing problems before they can affect everyone.

Mechanically, this is implemented with weighted routing at the load balancer or API gateway, or with a service mesh's traffic-splitting rules (an Istio VirtualService assigning percentage weights across two versions of a service, for example). Purpose-built canary tooling — Argo Rollouts, Spinnaker, Flagger — goes further, automatically querying metrics (error rate, p99 latency, custom business metrics) at each traffic step and promoting the rollout automatically if they look healthy, or automatically rolling back if they don't, without a human needing to watch a dashboard and decide.

The direct contrast with blue-green is exactly this gradualness: blue-green is an all-or-nothing cutover — the whole audience gets the new version at once, whether it's good or bad, with rollback being just as instant and total. Canary limits the blast radius of a bad release to only the small percentage of users who happened to see it during the early steps, catching a problem before it reaches everyone — at the cost of running two live versions side by side for longer, which can surface awkward inconsistencies if the same user bounces between the old and new version across requests, and requires genuinely reliable automated monitoring to be safe rather than just a comforting-looking rollout percentage.

Sidecar Pattern

The Sidecar pattern deploys a helper component right alongside a main application — most often as a second container in the same deployment unit — so it can add capability without changing the application's own code. It's easy to associate the pattern only with service mesh proxies like Envoy, but the same co-location idea shows up for much simpler helpers too: a log-shipping sidecar that tails an application's log files and forwards them to a centralized logging backend, or a config-reloading sidecar that watches a config source and refreshes files on disk for the main application to pick up, with the app itself never needing to know either job is happening.

In Kubernetes, this maps directly onto the pod: a pod can hold multiple containers that share a network namespace and a lifecycle, so the main application container and its sidecar start together, stop together, and can talk to each other over localhost as if they were just two threads of the same program, even though they're fully separate processes, possibly written in entirely different languages.

A fuller treatment of the pattern — including the service mesh data plane/control plane model and its Ambassador and Adapter variants — lives on its own dedicated page elsewhere on this site; the takeaway for this glossary is simpler: pulling cross-cutting concerns out of application code and into a co-located helper process is a recurring, reusable trick that shows up constantly in container-based deployment design.

Sidecar pattern: a helper container runs alongside the app in the same pod, adding capability without touching application code.

Infrastructure as Code (IaC)

Infrastructure as Code means defining servers, networks, load balancers, and every other piece of infrastructure in versioned, declarative configuration files, rather than provisioning them by hand through a cloud console — sometimes disparagingly called "ClickOps." Those config files live in the same kind of version control as application code, get reviewed through pull requests, and are the actual source of truth for what infrastructure should exist.

Two tools anchor most real-world IaC conversations. Terraform (HashiCorp) is cloud-agnostic — the same declarative language (HCL) can define resources across AWS, GCP, Azure, and dozens of other providers, and it keeps a state file tracking what it believes is actually deployed so it can compute the difference between desired and current state on every run. AWS CloudFormation is AWS's own native equivalent — YAML or JSON templates describing a "stack" of resources, tightly integrated with the rest of AWS but limited to it.

resource "aws_instance" "web" {
  ami           = "ami-0abcd1234efgh5678"
  instance_type = "t3.micro"
  tags = {
    Name = "web-server"
  }
}

The defining behavior both tools share is idempotent apply: running terraform apply (or updating a CloudFormation stack) repeatedly against the same template converges the real infrastructure to the same end state regardless of what currently exists — if nothing has changed, nothing happens; if only one field differs, only that field is updated. This is also exactly how IaC solves the drift problem: without it, someone manually bumping an instance size or opening a port through the console during an incident causes the real infrastructure to silently diverge from whatever's documented or assumed, and nobody notices until something breaks mysteriously. With IaC, the config file is the source of truth, drift can be detected by comparing it against actual state, and re-applying the template corrects it.

Immutable Infrastructure

Immutable infrastructure means never logging into a running server to patch, upgrade, or reconfigure it in place. Instead, any change — a new application version, an OS security patch, a config update — is baked into a brand-new image or artifact, and the running instance is destroyed and replaced wholesale with a fresh one built from that image, rather than edited.

This is a deliberate rejection of the traditional model of long-lived, hand-maintained "snowflake servers" — machines that have been SSH'd into and tweaked, patched, and configured by hand so many times over months or years that no one fully remembers or can reproduce their exact configuration, making each one unique, fragile, and terrifying to touch or rebuild from scratch. Immutable infrastructure treats servers as interchangeable "cattle" rather than irreplaceable "pets": any instance can be killed and replaced at any time with zero ceremony, because its entire definition lives in a versioned artifact rather than in years of undocumented manual changes.

The approach pairs naturally with both containers and Infrastructure as Code: a container image is inherently immutable already — you rebuild it rather than patch a running one — and an IaC template naturally describes "replace this resource with this new definition" rather than an in-place edit, so all three ideas reinforce the same underlying discipline. The trade-off is that rebuilding and redeploying a whole new image or instance for even a small change is slower than a quick manual patch, which requires a genuinely fast, reliable CI/CD pipeline to stay practical at speed, and it demands that any state that needs to persist — user data, uploaded files, session data — live outside the instance entirely (in a database or object store), since replacing an instance destroys anything stored locally on it.

Serverless / Function as a Service (FaaS)

AWS Lambda is the concrete reference point for FaaS: a developer writes a small function or handler, uploads it, and AWS takes care of provisioning, scaling, and patching the servers it runs on — there is no server for the developer to manage at all, and billing is based on the number of invocations and the compute time each one actually consumes, not on keeping a server running around the clock.

Lambda functions are event-triggered rather than always-on: a function sits idle until something invokes it — an HTTP request arriving through API Gateway, a new object landing in an S3 bucket, a message appearing on an SQS queue, a scheduled CloudWatch Events rule firing — and the platform transparently manages a pool of execution environments behind the scenes, spinning up more of them automatically to absorb a burst of concurrent invocations.

This is what makes scale-to-zero billing possible: when nothing is invoking a function, zero instances are running for it and the cost is literally zero, which is a meaningful difference from an always-on container or VM that costs money every hour whether or not it's serving any traffic — a natural fit for spiky, infrequent, or unpredictable workloads like resizing an uploaded image or sending a confirmation email.

The trade-offs are real, not just theoretical
FaaS has genuine limits worth naming precisely: cold starts, where the first invocation after a period of idleness has to spin up a fresh execution environment from scratch, adding noticeable latency (worse for runtimes with heavier startup/initialization costs); a maximum runtime (Lambda caps functions at around 15 minutes), which rules out long-running batch jobs; and limited control over the runtime environment — a fixed menu of supported language runtimes and versions, little ability to customize the underlying OS, and no durable local disk state between invocations. This page focuses on FaaS mechanics specifically; the broader "when to choose microservices vs. serverless" architectural comparison is covered elsewhere in this section.

Scalability & Load Management

Load Balancing

At its core, load balancing is the mechanism that spreads incoming requests across multiple running instances of a service so that no single instance gets overwhelmed, and so traffic can keep flowing smoothly even if one instance becomes unhealthy or disappears entirely. Conceptually, it's the piece of infrastructure that makes it possible to have more than one copy of anything running at once and still present callers with a single, reliable thing to talk to.

Microservices architectures specifically need load balancing at more than one layer: a client-facing layer at the edge of the system (an external load balancer or API gateway spreading incoming user traffic across many instances of a public-facing service), and a service-to-service layer inside the system, where dozens of internal services calling each other also need their traffic balanced across whichever replica pods currently exist for the callee — commonly handled by a Kubernetes Service or a service mesh sidecar rather than one central load balancer. A full breakdown of the specific algorithms load balancers use to make that per-request decision — round robin, least connections, consistent hashing, and the rest — lives on its own dedicated page elsewhere in this Microservices section.

A load balancer spreads requests across healthy instances and routes around ones that fail their health checks.

Horizontal Scaling / Auto-Scaling

Horizontal scaling means handling more load by adding more instances of a service, rather than making one existing instance bigger (vertical scaling, more CPU/RAM on the same machine). Vertical scaling has a hard ceiling — there's always a biggest machine available — while horizontal scaling can, in principle, keep adding commodity instances indefinitely, which is why it's the default strategy for anything expected to grow significantly.

Auto-scaling automates the "add more instances" decision by watching metrics continuously and reacting to thresholds rather than waiting for a human to notice load climbing. The simplest signals are CPU and memory utilization, but production systems increasingly scale on custom, business-relevant metrics too — the classic example being queue depth, where a fleet of worker instances scales up as messages pile up faster than they're being consumed, and back down once the backlog clears.

In Kubernetes specifically, this is the job of the Horizontal Pod Autoscaler (HPA): it watches a target metric for a Deployment (CPU/memory by default via the cluster's metrics-server, or custom and external metrics through metrics adapters) and automatically adjusts the number of running pod replicas up or down within configured minimum and maximum bounds. The equivalent idea outside Kubernetes is an AWS Auto Scaling Group adjusting the number of EC2 instances behind a load balancer based on similar metrics.

None of this works cleanly unless the instances being added and removed are stateless — any instance has to be able to handle any incoming request, because it isn't holding onto client- or session-specific memory locally. If instances did hold local state, killing one to scale down would silently lose that state, and routing would need to get "sticky" to specific instances anyway, undermining the whole point of elastic scaling. That's exactly why teams push session data, carts, and similar state out into a shared external store like Redis or a database — it's what makes horizontal scaling and auto-scaling actually safe to automate.

Strangler Fig Pattern

The name comes from a real plant: a strangler fig seed sprouts in the crack of a large host tree, slowly grows around it over years, and eventually becomes strong enough to stand on its own — until the original tree underneath rots away, leaving only the fig standing in its shape. Applied to software, it describes replacing a legacy monolith gradually, piece by piece, rather than attempting a risky big-bang rewrite that replaces everything at once.

In practice, this means incrementally routing traffic for specific routes or features away from the legacy monolith and toward new services, usually through a facade or proxy sitting in front of both — the proxy inspects each incoming request and decides, transparently to callers, whether it should still go to the old monolith or to whichever new service has already taken over that particular feature. As more features migrate, the proxy routes more traffic to the new services, until eventually nothing depends on the monolith anymore and it can be safely retired.

A full dedicated deep dive on this pattern — including its migration phases and the anti-corruption layer used to bridge old and new systems mid-transition — lives on its own page elsewhere on this site. The core interview-relevant point is the trade-off itself: incremental migration delivers value sooner and keeps the blast radius of any single change small and reversible, at the cost of running a longer, more operationally complex transition than a single clean rewrite would (in theory) take.

Security

API Gateway Authentication/Authorization

The dedicated Security page in this section covers authentication, authorization, OAuth 2.0, JWTs, and encryption at the whole-system level — worth having solid first, since this entry assumes those basics and zooms in on one specific place they get applied: the API gateway. The gateway is the single front door every external request passes through before it ever reaches a backend service, which makes it the natural single choke point for validating credentials. Instead of a dozen microservices each independently checking an API key or verifying a bearer token, the gateway does it once, at the edge, and only forwards a request onward if it passes.

Mechanically, the gateway terminates TLS, pulls the caller's token (an API key, an OAuth access token, a JWT) out of the request headers, and either validates it locally — checking a JWT's signature and expiry against a cached public key — or checks with an identity provider, then attaches whatever identity and claims it resolved as internal headers the backend services can trust without re-verifying anything, since those headers came from the gateway rather than an untrusted client. Kong, Amazon API Gateway, Apigee, and Zuul/Spring Cloud Gateway are common implementations of this pattern, and most of them also do coarse-grained authorization here too, rejecting a request outright if the token's scopes don't cover the route being called, before any backend service has to think about it at all.

The tradeoff is that the gateway becomes both a single point of enforcement and a single point of failure for auth — a bug or outage there can lock every request out of the whole system, or, worse, quietly let through requests that should have been denied. It's also a common point of bypass: anything that can reach a service's internal address directly skips the gateway entirely, which is exactly why internal service-to-service calls need their own auth story too — the next two entries.

OAuth2 / JWT for Service-to-Service Auth

The Security page in this section covers OAuth 2.0 and JWTs from the angle of a human user logging into an app. Machine-to-machine calls need the same trust story with no human anywhere in the loop, and OAuth 2.0's client credentials grant is the flow built for exactly that: a calling service authenticates directly to an authorization server using its own client ID and secret (or, more securely, a signed JWT assertion or an mTLS client certificate instead of a shared secret) and gets back a short-lived access token scoped to whatever it's permitted to do — no redirect, no login screen, no resource owner involved at all.

POST /oauth/token
Content-Type: application/x-www-form-urlencoded

grant_type=client_credentials
&client_id=order-service
&client_secret=***
&scope=inventory.read inventory.reserve

The calling service attaches the resulting token as a bearer token on its actual request to the inventory service, which validates the JWT locally — checking the signature against the authorization server's published public key, confirming it hasn't expired, and confirming the required scope is present in the claims — with no callback to the authorization server needed. Because these tokens are machine-issued and machine-consumed, they're deliberately kept short-lived, often just minutes, and rotated constantly rather than cached indefinitely; a compromised token valid for five minutes is a far smaller blast radius than one valid for a day. This is also why secret managers like Vault or AWS Secrets Manager sit underneath so much of this machinery — the credential a service uses to fetch its own tokens needs protecting and rotating at least as carefully as the tokens it produces.

Zero Trust Architecture

Zero trust is a security model built around one blunt principle: never trust, always verify. Traditional network security worked like a castle with a moat — build a strong perimeter (a firewall, a VPN gateway), and once a request or device made it inside, it was implicitly trusted to talk to whatever it wanted. Zero trust rejects that assumption outright: no request is trusted just because it happens to originate from inside the corporate network or the production VPC — every call, whether from the public internet or from one internal service to another sitting two racks away, has to independently prove who it is and that it's allowed to do what it's asking to do.

In a microservices system, this plays out as authenticating and authorizing every service-to-service call, not just the outer edge where a request first enters. That typically combines strong workload identity (each service provably identifiable, often via mTLS certificates — the next entry), fine-grained authorization policy (service A can call service B's /reserve endpoint but not its /admin endpoint, regardless of network reachability), and short-lived credentials rather than long-lived, broadly-scoped ones. Google's internal BeyondCorp model is the reference implementation most often cited — Google removed the assumption of a trusted internal network entirely, requiring every access request, whether from an employee or a service, to be authenticated and authorized per-request regardless of whether it came from Google's own office network or a coffee shop.

This contrasts sharply with the older perimeter model, where a hard, crunchy exterior protected a soft, trusting interior — meaning that once an attacker got past the perimeter, through a phished credential, a misconfigured firewall rule, or a single compromised container, they typically had broad, unchecked lateral movement across everything else inside. Zero trust is largely a direct response to how badly that model has aged: modern systems don't have a clean perimeter anymore — cloud services, remote employees, third-party SaaS integrations, and containers rescheduled onto different hosts constantly all blur where "inside" even is — and a large share of real breaches involve exactly this kind of lateral movement following an initial, contained compromise.

Gotcha
Zero trust is a multi-year program, not a single product to install. NIST SP 800-207 is the commonly cited reference architecture, but implementing it requires simultaneous investment across identity, network policy, and observability — retrofitting it onto a system with years of implicit internal trust already baked in is a significant undertaking, not a sprint.
Perimeter security trusts anything already inside; zero trust verifies every single call, internal or external.

mTLS (mutual TLS)

Regular TLS is one-directional trust: when a browser connects to a website over HTTPS, the server presents a certificate proving its identity, and the browser verifies it against a trusted certificate authority — but the server has no cryptographic proof of who the browser belongs to. Mutual TLS (mTLS) closes that gap by requiring both sides to present and verify a certificate: the client proves its identity to the server, and the server proves its identity to the client, before either trusts the other or exchanges any application data.

In a microservices system, mTLS gives every service a strong cryptographic identity that's independent of network location — exactly the workload identity that zero trust architecture depends on. Instead of relying on network segmentation or an internal firewall rule to imply a caller is "trusted because it's on the internal network," a service checks the caller's certificate on every single connection. That means an attacker who manages to get a container running inside the cluster still can't impersonate another service or quietly eavesdrop on traffic between two legitimate services, since they don't hold the private key matching any valid certificate.

Issuing, distributing, and rotating certificates for every service instance by hand doesn't scale, which is exactly the problem a service mesh like Istio solves. Istio runs a sidecar proxy (Envoy) alongside every service instance, and its control plane (istiod) automatically issues each sidecar a short-lived certificate, handles mutual authentication transparently between sidecars on every call, and rotates those certificates continuously in the background — application code never touches a certificate or even necessarily knows mTLS is happening underneath it. Conceptually, when service A calls service B: A's sidecar and B's sidecar perform a TLS handshake in which A's sidecar presents its certificate (signed by the mesh's internal CA) and B's sidecar presents its own; each side verifies the other's certificate against the shared CA, and only after both checks pass does the connection carry the actual request.

The tradeoff is operational overhead: someone has to run and secure the certificate authority issuing all of this, rotation has to happen reliably and often (a CA outage or a clock-skew bug can quietly break every service-to-service call at once), and debugging connectivity gets an extra layer harder when a "connection refused" might actually be a certificate validation failure two layers down in a sidecar. Most teams adopt mTLS through a service mesh specifically because hand-rolling per-service certificate management at any real scale is its own significant engineering project.

Mutual TLS: both sides present and verify a certificate against a shared CA before any application data flows.

Observability

Distributed Tracing

In a monolith, a single log file (or a debugger) can usually show the entire life of a request. In a microservices system, one user action — "check out my cart" — might fan out across a dozen services: an API gateway, an auth check, an inventory service, a pricing service, a payments service, a notification service, each possibly calling still more services beneath it. When that request is slow or fails, grepping through a dozen separate services' logs, each with its own timestamps and no shared thread connecting them, to figure out where the time went or where it broke is close to impossible at any real scale.

Distributed tracing solves this by assigning a single trace ID to a request the moment it enters the system, and propagating that ID through every downstream call the request causes. Each unit of work along the way — a single service handling its piece of the request — is recorded as a "span," carrying its own start time, duration, and metadata, tagged with the shared trace ID and a reference to its parent span, so the whole thing can be reassembled afterward into one tree showing which services were called, in what order, sequentially or in parallel, and how long each one took.

OpenTelemetry (often shortened to OTel) has become the modern, vendor-neutral standard for generating and propagating this data — instrumenting a service with the OpenTelemetry SDK produces spans and trace propagation without hand-rolling it, and the data can be exported to whichever backend a team has chosen. Jaeger and Zipkin are the two most commonly named open-source backends for storing and visualizing the resulting traces, letting an engineer pull up one slow request and see a waterfall diagram of exactly which downstream service ate the time. The trace context itself typically travels as an HTTP header (the W3C Trace Context standard defines traceparent):

traceparent: 00-4bf92f3577b34da6a3ce929d0e0e4736-00f067aa0ba902b7-01

which packs the trace ID, the current span ID, and a sampling flag into one propagated header. Tracing every single request in a high-traffic system generates a genuinely enormous amount of data, so production systems almost always sample — tracing only some percentage of requests (say 1-10%), or using smarter "tail-based" sampling that captures a trace only after deciding it was slow or errored, so the interesting cases are the ones actually kept.

A trace reassembled from spans: which services were called, in what order, and how long each one took.

Centralized Logging

Every service in a microservices system runs as multiple instances spread across many hosts or containers, each writing its own local log files. Debugging an incident by SSHing into an individual box to tail a log stops being viable almost immediately in this world — the instance that logged the error might already be rescheduled or terminated by the time anyone goes looking, and even if it isn't, checking a dozen instances of a dozen services one at a time to find the one relevant line is not something anyone should be doing at 3am.

Centralized logging solves this by shipping every log line, from every instance of every service, off the box and into one searchable system the moment it's written. The classic open-source stack for this is ELK — Elasticsearch (a search and storage engine), Logstash (an ingestion/transformation pipeline), and Kibana (the visualization/search UI) — though many teams swap Logstash for the lighter-weight Fluentd or Fluent Bit, giving the "EFK" variant. Managed alternatives like Splunk, Datadog Logs, or a cloud provider's own logging service (CloudWatch Logs, Google Cloud Logging) serve the same purpose without the operational burden of running Elasticsearch yourself.

None of this is genuinely useful, though, unless the logs themselves are structured — emitted as JSON with consistent field names (timestamp, service name, log level, trace ID, user ID) rather than free-form human-readable text. A line like "Order 4821 failed for user 55" is fine for a human staring at one box, but a centralized system needs to filter and aggregate across millions of lines: "show me every ERROR-level log from the payments service in the last 15 minutes for this trace ID" is a query only structured logging can answer efficiently. Free-text logs still get indexed, but everyone ends up writing brittle regex to pull fields back out of them — exactly the problem structured logging avoids in the first place.

The main pitfall is cost and noise: shipping every log line from a high-traffic system to a centralized store gets expensive fast, both in storage and in the compute needed to index it all, and a system that logs too verbosely at INFO level everywhere makes finding the signal just as hard as having no centralized logging at all. Most mature setups tune log levels deliberately per environment and use sampling or shorter retention windows for high-volume, low-value log lines.

Metrics & Monitoring

Metrics and logs solve related but genuinely different problems, and conflating them is a common interview stumble. A log is a discrete, timestamped event — "this specific request failed with this specific error." A metric is an aggregated numeric time series — "the error rate across all requests to this service over the last minute was 2%." Logs are what you dig into once you know something's wrong to understand exactly why; metrics are what tell you something's wrong (or about to be) in the first place, cheaply, at a glance, across an entire fleet, without storing the detail of every individual event.

Prometheus has become the de facto standard for collecting and storing these time series in cloud-native systems — it scrapes numeric metrics from each service on a regular interval (rather than services pushing data to it) and stores them efficiently, queryable with its own query language, PromQL. Grafana is the visualization layer almost always paired with it, turning raw time series into the dashboards teams actually watch — request rate, error rate, latency percentiles, CPU/memory usage, queue depth, all graphed side by side.

Google's SRE book popularized a simple framework for deciding which metrics actually matter, known as the four golden signals:

LatencyHow long requests take to completep50/p95/p99 response time for an API endpoint

None of this matters without alerting tied to it — a metric nobody looks at until an outage is already underway isn't doing its job. Effective alerting sets thresholds against these signals (page an on-call engineer if the error rate exceeds 5% for more than two minutes, or if p99 latency crosses a defined SLO) and, critically, alerts on symptoms that matter to users rather than every underlying cause, since alerting on too much noise trains engineers to ignore alerts entirely — a pitfall usually called "alert fatigue."

Correlation IDs

A correlation ID is a single unique identifier generated once, at the very edge of a system — typically at the API gateway or load balancer, the moment a request first arrives — and then propagated, unchanged, through every downstream service call that request triggers, usually carried as an HTTP header:

X-Correlation-ID: 7f3c9a2e-88b1-4d6a-9c2f-1a5e6d0b8f44

Every service in the chain reads that header off the incoming request, includes it in every log line it writes, and forwards the same header on any call it makes to yet another service further downstream. The direct payoff is that when something goes wrong, an engineer can take the one correlation ID surfaced from a user's bug report or an alert and pull every log line, from every service that touched that request, across the entire system, with a single search — instead of separately hunting through a dozen services' logs and cross-referencing timestamps.

This is deliberately similar to distributed tracing above, and the two get confused constantly, but they aren't quite the same thing. A correlation ID is usually just a flat identifier stitched into log lines for searchability; a trace ID does the same grouping job but as part of a much richer structure — spans, parent/child relationships, timing data — that can reconstruct the actual shape and duration of the call graph, not just group log lines together. In practice, many systems use the trace ID itself as the correlation ID, or generate one early and fold it into the trace context, so the two end up carried by the same header in modern OpenTelemetry-instrumented systems — but a system can have correlation IDs for log grouping without full tracing infrastructure at all, which is often exactly how simpler systems start before eventually adopting full distributed tracing.

Gotcha
This only works if every service in the chain is disciplined about propagating the header. If a single service forgets to read the incoming correlation ID and forward it on its own outbound calls — an easy thing to miss when a new service is stood up by a team that didn't get the memo — the chain breaks at exactly that hop, and the trail goes cold right where the incident happened. Framework- or gateway-level middleware that injects and propagates the header automatically removes the need for application code to remember it manually.
A correlation ID assigned at the edge is propagated through every downstream call and logged by each service.

Governance & Operations

Service Versioning

Once a service has real consumers — other teams, other services, external partners — its API can't just change out from under them every time it's redeployed. Service versioning is the discipline of managing how an API's contract evolves over time without breaking whoever's already depending on the version they integrated against. The two dominant approaches are URI versioning, where the version is baked directly into the path (/v1/orders, /v2/orders), and header-based versioning, where the URL stays constant and the caller specifies a desired version through a request header (Accept: application/vnd.company.orders.v2+json, or a custom header like X-API-Version: 2).

URI versioning is blunt but extremely visible and easy to reason about — anyone reading a log, browser history, or API doc immediately knows which version handled a given request, and it's trivial to route entirely different implementations behind a load balancer purely by path prefix. Header-based versioning keeps URLs clean and is arguably more "correct" from a pure REST standpoint (a resource's identity shouldn't change just because its representation evolved), but it's easier for a consumer to get wrong silently — a missing header just falls back to some default version rather than throwing an obvious 404 — and harder to eyeball from raw request logs or a browser address bar.

Either mechanism only actually protects consumers if it's paired with genuine backward-compatibility discipline for the common case: adding an optional new field to a response, or a new optional query parameter, shouldn't require bumping the version at all, since well-behaved consumers should already ignore unknown fields. A true breaking change — removing a field, changing a field's type or meaning, changing required parameters, or altering error semantics — is what actually justifies a new version, and even then, a mature service typically keeps the old version alive and functioning in parallel for a defined deprecation window, with usage monitoring, a sunset date communicated well in advance, and often a deprecation warning header on old-version responses, rather than deleting it out from under consumers the moment the new version ships.

Gotcha
The real failure mode isn't picking the "wrong" versioning scheme — both work fine in production systems everywhere — it's skipping the backward-compatibility discipline entirely, so a "v1" endpoint quietly changes behavior on every deploy because nobody treats it as a real contract. In practice, few teams keep more than two or three live versions of any given endpoint at once; beyond that, the combinatorial cost of testing and maintaining every version in parallel usually forces a harder deprecation push.
URI versioning bakes the version into the path; header versioning keeps one URL and routes internally by request header.

Contract Testing / Consumer-Driven Contracts

Unit tests verify that a single service behaves correctly in isolation, and full integration tests — spinning up a whole cluster of real dependent services to test against — verify the real thing end-to-end. But the first tells you nothing about whether your service still actually works with the other services calling it, and the second is often slow, flaky, and expensive enough that teams don't run it on every single change. The common failure mode this leaves open: service A changes its API in a way that quietly breaks service B, and nobody finds out until B's next deploy fails in staging, or worse, in production.

Contract testing closes that gap without needing a full integration environment. A "contract" is a precise, machine-readable description of what a consumer actually expects from a provider — specific requests it will send, and the specific responses it expects back. In a consumer-driven contract model, the consumer team writes that contract (often by recording their expectations against a mock of the provider during their own tests), publishes it somewhere shared, and the provider team runs that same contract as an automated test against their real service before every deploy — so if the provider's next change would actually break the consumer's expectations, the provider's own CI pipeline fails immediately, long before a live deploy ever reaches production.

Pact is the tool most commonly named for this — it lets a consumer's test suite generate a contract file capturing its expectations, publishes that contract to a shared Pact Broker, and gives the provider a verification step that replays every published consumer contract against its actual codebase, failing the build if any expectation is no longer met. This flips the traditional testing relationship: instead of the provider guessing what consumers need and hoping integration tests catch drift, every consumer's real expectations are captured explicitly and checked automatically, continuously, by the provider that could break them.

The tradeoff is that contract tests are deliberately narrow — they verify the shape and semantics of specific interactions a consumer actually exercises, not the full end-to-end behavior of the system under realistic load or with real data flowing through every service. They aren't a replacement for all end-to-end testing, just a much cheaper, faster, more reliable first line of defense that catches the large majority of "someone changed an API and broke a consumer" incidents without needing a full staging environment spun up for every change.

Configuration Management (externalized config)

A deployed service artifact — a container image, a JAR, a compiled binary — shouldn't need to be rebuilt and redeployed just to change a rate limit, flip a feature flag, or point at a different database connection string. Externalized configuration management pulls exactly that kind of environment- and moment-specific detail out of the artifact itself and into a separate store the running service reads from, at startup or, for values that need to change live, continuously while running.

Spring Cloud Config is a common choice in the Java ecosystem — a dedicated config server that services pull settings from at boot, typically backed by a Git repo so config changes are versioned like code. HashiCorp Consul and etcd serve a similar role more generally (and are also frequently used for service discovery alongside configuration), while AWS Parameter Store (and its sibling, AWS Secrets Manager, for anything sensitive) is a common choice for teams already living in AWS. All of them let an operator change a feature flag, a timeout, or a rate limit and have it take effect across every running instance of a service without a single redeploy.

This entry covers externalized configuration only briefly, as it relates to running microservices day to day. A dedicated page in this site's Software Architecture Patterns section treats the "Configuration Externalization" pattern in much more depth, including how it fits alongside the broader twelve-factor discipline below and how to handle config changes safely across environments.

Service Ownership (you-build-it-you-run-it)

In a traditional operating model, one team writes a service and hands it off to a separate operations team that deploys, monitors, and gets paged when it breaks in production — the team that wrote the code is rarely the team that feels the consequences of a bad 3am incident. "You build it, you run it" inverts that: the team that designs and writes a service also owns running it in production, including carrying the pager for it.

Amazon is usually credited as the originator of this model internally, and Netflix became one of its most visible public champions, building an entire culture (and a lot of internal tooling, including its well-known chaos engineering practices) around small, autonomous teams that own a service's entire lifecycle end to end — design, code, test, deploy, monitor, operate. Each team is deliberately kept small enough to be fed by "two pizzas," as Amazon's internal framing goes, precisely so ownership stays clear and undiluted rather than spreading responsibility so thin that no one actually feels accountable for a given service.

The throw-it-over-the-wall alternative creates a structural misalignment: a separate ops team, with no ability to change the code, can only react to symptoms — restart it, roll it back, escalate to the original developers — rather than fix root causes, while the developers who could actually fix the underlying bug are insulated from ever experiencing the pain their design decisions cause in production. You-build-it-you-run-it removes that insulation deliberately — the same engineers who chose a risky shortcut, skipped a test, or under-provisioned a database are the ones who get paged when it breaks, creating a direct, fast feedback loop between a design decision and its operational consequence.

This isn't free, though — it demands real investment in tooling (good dashboards, alerting, runbooks, self-service deploy pipelines) so developers can actually operate what they've built without becoming full-time infrastructure experts, and it requires organizational buy-in to genuinely reduce scope elsewhere — fewer features shipped per sprint, in exchange for carrying operational load — rather than simply adding "also be on call" on top of an already-full backlog. Done well, though, it consistently produces more resilient, more operable software, because the people best positioned to fix a root cause are exactly the people who feel the pain of not fixing it.

Twelve-Factor App Principles

The twelve-factor app methodology was published by engineers at Heroku in 2011, distilled from their experience building and operating a large-scale platform-as-a-service and observing which patterns made applications easy (or painful) to build, scale, and deploy reliably in the cloud. It predates "microservices" as a widely used term, but it reads today like a foundational checklist for exactly the properties a well-designed, cloud-native microservice should have — which is why it's still cited constantly in system design interviews and real architecture reviews alike.

FactorPrinciple
CodebaseOne codebase tracked in version control, many deploys from it
DependenciesExplicitly declare and isolate dependencies — never rely on what happens to already be installed on the host
ConfigStore configuration in the environment, not in the code (see Configuration Management, above)
Backing servicesTreat databases, queues, and caches as attached resources, swappable without code changes
Build, release, runStrictly separate the build stage, the release stage (build + config), and the run stage
ProcessesExecute the app as one or more stateless processes; persist state only in backing services
Port bindingExport services via port binding — the app is self-contained, not reliant on a runtime-injected web server
DisposabilityMaximize robustness with fast startup and graceful shutdown
Dev/prod parityKeep development, staging, and production as similar as possible
LogsTreat logs as event streams, written to stdout, rather than managed as files by the app itself

Several of these map directly onto ideas covered elsewhere on this page and site: "config" is exactly the externalized configuration principle above, "processes" (stateless, no locally persisted state) is the same statelessness idea covered elsewhere in this section, and "logs as event streams" is precisely what makes centralized logging above actually work — an app that insists on managing its own log files on local disk instead of just writing to stdout makes shipping those logs to a centralized system unnecessarily awkward.

The reason these principles became foundational rather than just one company's internal style guide is that they're what actually makes an application portable and operable at scale: an app that reads its config from the environment can move between dev, staging, and production, or between two different clouds, with zero code changes; a stateless, disposable process can be killed and restarted by an orchestrator like Kubernetes at any moment without anyone noticing; and a codebase with cleanly declared dependencies can be built reproducibly on any CI runner. Violating them tends to surface as exactly the kind of production pain this page is largely about — a service that can't scale horizontally because it secretly holds session state, or a deploy that behaves differently in staging than in production because config was hardcoded rather than externalized.

Common Interview Questions

Structure & Communication

You inherited a system where three services all read and write the same shared database. Why is this a problem, and what's the actual migration path to database-per-service?
A shared database is invisible coupling: any service can come to depend on undocumented schema details (a column, a trigger, a stored procedure) with nothing catching a breaking change, which quietly re-couples every consumer back into one release train and defeats independent deployability. The migration path is to pick one service as the true owner of each table, force every other service to go through that owner's API instead of querying directly, and replace any cross-service joins those other services relied on with either API composition or a CQRS-style read model built from published events — accepting that multi-step business transactions now need a Saga instead of one shared ACID transaction.
What's the practical difference between an API gateway and a service mesh, and would you ever run both?
A gateway manages north-south traffic — the outside world calling into the cluster — handling routing, authentication, rate limiting, and aggregation at one front door. A mesh manages east-west traffic — services calling each other inside the cluster — via a sidecar (commonly Envoy) next to every instance, adding mutual TLS, consistent retries/timeouts, and per-hop tracing without code changes. They solve different traffic directions, so most non-trivial systems run both together: a gateway at the edge and a mesh (Istio or Linkerd) internally.
When would you choose gRPC over REST for a new service, and when would that be the wrong call?
gRPC's protobuf-over-HTTP/2 wire format is smaller and faster than JSON-over-HTTP/1.1 and supports native streaming, making it a strong choice for high-throughput, latency-sensitive internal service-to-service calls you control on both ends. It's the wrong call for a public or partner-facing API, since protobuf isn't human-readable, browsers can't natively call gRPC without a grpc-web proxy in front, and external consumers generally have far better REST/JSON tooling than gRPC tooling.
A publisher emits an "order.placed" event and three services subscribe to it independently. How is that different from putting the same message on a plain queue for three workers, and what problem does the pub/sub version create that the queue version doesn't?
On a plain point-to-point queue, competing workers pull from the same queue and whichever one grabs a message consumes it — the others never see it. In pub/sub, every subscriber gets its own full copy of the event and tracks its own consumption position independently, so all three services (inventory, shipping, notifications) react to the same event without knowing about each other. The trade-off is discoverability: because subscribers are decoupled by design, it becomes hard to know who actually depends on a given event in a large system, unlike an explicit REST contract where you can more easily find every caller.

Resilience & Data Management

Why did Two-Phase Commit fall out of favor for microservices, and what pattern typically replaces it?
2PC requires every participant to hold locks and block, deferring its commit decision to a central coordinator across a network boundary — if the coordinator crashes between the prepare and commit phases, participants are stuck holding locks with no way to independently decide whether to commit or abort. That blocking, tightly-coupled behavior is exactly what microservices architecture tries to avoid, since each service is meant to own its own database and transaction boundary. The Saga pattern replaces it by letting each service commit its own local transaction immediately and handling failure afterward with compensating transactions instead of one blocking distributed transaction.
How does the Outbox pattern solve the dual-write problem, and how does it typically connect to CDC?
The dual-write problem is that updating your own database and separately publishing an event to a broker are two writes to two different systems with no way to make them atomic together — one can succeed while the other fails. The Outbox pattern solves this by writing the event as a row in an outbox table inside the same local database transaction as the business update, making the two writes atomic by construction. A separate relay process then delivers outbox rows onward to the broker — most commonly today, a CDC tool like Debezium tails the database's transaction log and picks up committed outbox rows automatically, so the application never has to publish to the broker directly at all.
What's the difference between a Circuit Breaker and a Bulkhead, and why are they often deployed together?
A circuit breaker reacts to a dependency's failure rate over time, tripping to stop sending it traffic once failures cross a threshold — it's about when to stop calling a struggling dependency. A bulkhead isolates the resources (thread pools, connection pools) used to call different dependencies so that one dependency consuming an entire shared pool can't starve calls to every other dependency — it's about limiting the blast radius while a dependency is failing, before or even without a breaker tripping. They're complementary rather than redundant, which is why resilience libraries like Resilience4j implement both to be used side by side on the same call.
Why are Event Sourcing and CQRS so often paired together?
Event Sourcing produces, as a natural byproduct of how it stores data, an ordered, immutable log of every event that ever happened to an entity — exactly the kind of source-of-truth feed that CQRS's read side needs to build one or more denormalized, query-optimized projections from. Instead of maintaining a single model that has to serve both consistent writes and fast, flexible reads, the event log handles the write side's job of capturing what happened, and CQRS projections consume that log to build whatever read shapes the application's queries actually need — and if a new query pattern comes up later, a new projection can be built by replaying history rather than migrating a live schema.

Deployment & Scalability

Blue-green and canary both enable zero-downtime deployments — when would you pick one over the other?
Blue-green is a fast, all-or-nothing cutover — every user gets the new version at once, and rollback is just as instant since the old environment is left running untouched. That makes it a good fit when you want a clean, fully-tested switch and can afford to run double infrastructure briefly. Canary shifts traffic gradually (5% → 25% → 100%) while watching error rates and latency, which limits the blast radius of a bad release to a small slice of users instead of everyone — better when you want an early warning system before a full rollout, at the cost of running two versions side by side longer and needing solid automated monitoring to catch problems safely.
Why does immutable infrastructure pair so naturally with containers and Infrastructure as Code?
All three reinforce the same idea: replace rather than patch. A container image is already immutable by nature — you rebuild it instead of editing a running one — and an IaC template naturally describes "here is the new definition of this resource" rather than an in-place tweak. Put together, servers become disposable, interchangeable "cattle" instead of hand-maintained "snowflake" pets, which is exactly what makes it safe to kill and replace any instance at any time without fear of losing undocumented manual configuration.
Why does auto-scaling require services to be stateless to work cleanly?
Auto-scaling (like Kubernetes' HPA) works by freely adding and removing instances based on metrics like CPU or queue depth. If an instance held client- or session-specific memory locally, killing it during a scale-down would silently lose that state, and routing would need to pin certain users to certain instances anyway — defeating the elasticity auto-scaling is supposed to provide. Pushing session and cart data into an external store like Redis is what lets any instance handle any request, making it safe to scale the fleet up or down automatically.
Why is the Strangler Fig pattern usually preferred over a big-bang rewrite when modernizing a legacy monolith?
A big-bang rewrite requires betting everything on one long, high-risk effort, while the business still needs bug fixes and new features shipped on the old system the whole time, and undocumented legacy edge cases are nearly impossible to perfectly replicate up front. The Strangler Fig approach routes traffic for one feature at a time to a new service via a facade, delivering value almost immediately, keeping the blast radius of any single migration step small and reversible, and letting the team learn the legacy system's real behavior incrementally rather than all at once.

Security, Observability & Governance

How does mTLS differ from the TLS used by a typical HTTPS website, and why does it matter for service-to-service calls?
Regular TLS only requires the server to present a certificate — the client verifies the server's identity, but the server has no cryptographic proof of who's calling. mTLS requires both sides to present and verify certificates, giving each service a strong, network-independent identity. That matters for service-to-service calls because a workload can't be impersonated or eavesdropped on just by getting a foothold inside the network perimeter — exactly the workload identity zero trust architecture depends on, and why service meshes like Istio automate issuing and rotating these certificates for every service.
Correlation IDs and distributed tracing both help debug a request across many services — how are they actually different?
A correlation ID is typically a flat identifier generated once at the edge and stitched into every log line a request touches, mainly so logs from different services can be searched together. Distributed tracing captures much richer structure — spans with parent/child relationships and timing data for every hop — that can reconstruct the actual shape and duration of the call graph, not just group log lines. In practice many systems use the same trace ID as the correlation ID, but a system can have correlation IDs for log searchability without full tracing infrastructure, which is often how simpler systems start.
What does contract testing (like Pact) catch that a suite of end-to-end integration tests wouldn't, and why not just rely on integration tests?
Full end-to-end tests need every dependent service running together, which is slow, flaky, and expensive enough that most teams don't run the whole suite on every change, so integration breakage between two specific services often isn't caught until deploy time. Contract testing verifies just the specific interactions a consumer actually depends on against the provider's real code, fast enough to run on every provider commit, so a breaking change is caught in the provider's own CI before it ever reaches a shared environment. It's a narrower, faster complement to end-to-end tests, not a replacement for them.
Why does "you build it, you run it" tend to produce more reliable systems than a model with a separate operations team?
When a separate ops team owns production, they can only react to symptoms — restart, roll back, escalate — since they can't change the code, while the developers who made the underlying design decisions never feel the consequences of a bad 3am page. You-build-it-you-run-it, popularized by Amazon and Netflix, removes that insulation — the same engineers who took a risky shortcut are the ones who get paged for it, creating a direct feedback loop between a design decision and its operational cost, which tends to push teams toward more resilient designs over time.
What's the practical difference between zero trust architecture and the older perimeter ("castle-and-moat") security model?
The perimeter model builds a strong outer boundary (firewalls, VPNs) and then implicitly trusts anything already inside it, so a single breach — a phished credential or one compromised container — often gives an attacker broad lateral movement across everything internal. Zero trust assumes no implicit trust from network location at all: every request, whether from the public internet or from one internal service to another, must independently authenticate and be authorized, typically backed by strong workload identity like mTLS. It's a direct response to modern systems no longer having a clean, single perimeter to defend in the first place.

Key Takeaways

Remember
Structure & design decisions — service decomposition, bounded contexts, database-per-service — determine how independently teams can actually move; get them wrong and every other pattern on this page becomes a workaround for a boundary drawn in the wrong place. Communication patterns (synchronous REST/gRPC vs. asynchronous messaging, service discovery, API gateways, service meshes) decide how tightly services are coupled in time and how failures propagate between them. Resilience patterns (circuit breakers, bulkheads, retries with backoff, health checks, chaos engineering) exist because networks and dependencies fail constantly at scale, and the goal is to fail small and contained rather than large and cascading. Data management patterns (sagas, event sourcing, CQRS, CDC, the outbox pattern) all answer the same underlying question — how do you keep data consistent across services that each own their own database — without relying on a distributed transaction. Deployment & infrastructure practices (containers, Kubernetes, blue-green and canary releases, IaC, immutable infrastructure, serverless) turn "ship a change safely" into a repeatable, low-risk operation instead of a risky manual event. Scalability patterns (load balancing, horizontal auto-scaling, the strangler fig migration path) all depend on services being stateless first. Security concepts (zero trust, mTLS, OAuth2/JWT for service-to-service auth) push authentication and authorization to every hop, not just the edge. Observability (distributed tracing, centralized logging, metrics, correlation IDs) is what makes a system with dozens of moving parts debuggable at 3am. And governance practices (contract testing, service versioning, externalized config, you-build-it-you-run-it, the twelve-factor principles) are the organizational discipline that keeps all of the above sustainable as the number of services — and the number of teams owning them — keeps growing.
