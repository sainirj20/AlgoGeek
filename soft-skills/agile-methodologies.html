Agile Methodologies

Overview

Agile is not a single process — it's a family of iterative, feedback-driven approaches to delivering software (Scrum, Kanban, Extreme Programming, SAFe, Scrumban, and others) that all trace back to the 2001 Agile Manifesto: individuals and interactions over processes and tools, working software over comprehensive documentation, customer collaboration over contract negotiation, and responding to change over following a plan. The common thread across every flavor is short feedback loops — build a small increment, get it in front of real users or stakeholders, inspect what happened, and adapt the plan.

Interviewers rarely just want the Scrum Guide recited back to them. At the IC level they're checking that you can operate inside a sprint without friction; at the senior/lead/architect level they're probing for judgment and scar tissue — how you've handled a slipping sprint, a stakeholder who won't stop injecting new asks mid-cycle, a team that's going through the motions of ceremonies without actually improving, or a leadership team that wants "Agile" in name only while still demanding a fixed scope/fixed date waterfall plan underneath. Expect a mix of definitional questions, framework-comparison questions, and scenario/behavioral questions that probe how you'd actually lead a team through dysfunction.

Core Agile & Scrum Concepts

Before framework comparisons and scenarios, interviewers expect fluency with the vocabulary. These terms show up constantly, and getting one wrong (e.g., conflating "velocity" with "story points" or the Product Backlog with the Sprint Backlog) is an easy tell that you haven't actually run in a Scrum team.

TermDefinition
SprintA fixed, time-boxed iteration (commonly 1–4 weeks, most often 2) during which a Scrum team builds a "Done", potentially releasable product increment. Sprints are back-to-back with no gaps, and the length doesn't change mid-project.
Product BacklogThe single, ordered, ever-evolving list of everything that might be needed in the product — features, fixes, non-functional work, tech debt. Owned and prioritized by the Product Owner; refined continuously ("backlog refinement/grooming").
Sprint BacklogThe subset of Product Backlog items the Development Team forecasts it can deliver in the current sprint, plus the plan for delivering them. It belongs to the team, not the Product Owner, and is updated daily.
User StoryA short, user-centric description of a requirement, typically in the form "As a <role>, I want <capability>, so that <benefit>." Paired with acceptance criteria that define when it's provably done.
Story PointsA relative, unitless measure of the effort/complexity/risk/uncertainty to implement a backlog item, usually assigned via a Fibonacci-like scale (1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13…). Points estimate relative size, not hours.
VelocityThe average number of story points a team completes per sprint, measured over several sprints. Used for forecasting release dates — never as a productivity or performance metric for individuals or teams.
Definition of Done (DoD)The team's shared, objective checklist (code reviewed, tests passing, deployed to staging, docs updated, etc.) that a backlog item must satisfy before it counts as complete. Prevents "90% done" ambiguity.
Definition of Ready (DoR)A lightweight checklist (clear acceptance criteria, sized, dependencies identified, no open questions) a story should meet before it's pulled into a sprint, so the team doesn't start work on something half-understood.
Product OwnerOwns the "what" and "why" — maximizes product value, orders the Product Backlog, and is the single point of accountability for backlog content and priority.
Scrum MasterA servant-leader who owns the "how" of the process — coaches the team on Scrum, removes impediments, shields the team from disruption, and facilitates the ceremonies without dictating solutions.
Development TeamThe cross-functional group of people who actually build the increment. Self-organizing — they decide how to turn backlog items into working software, and only they can commit to what fits in a sprint.

The Four Scrum Ceremonies (Events)

  • Sprint Planning — held at the start of the sprint; the team decides the sprint goal and pulls in backlog items it forecasts it can complete (roughly proportional to sprint length — e.g., ~2 hours per week of sprint).
  • Daily Scrum / Standup — a ~15-minute daily sync for the Development Team to inspect progress toward the sprint goal and re-plan the next 24 hours; it is not a status report to management.
  • Sprint Review — held at the end of the sprint; the team demos the increment to stakeholders and collects feedback that feeds back into the Product Backlog.
  • Sprint Retrospective — held after the review, before the next planning; the team inspects itself (people, process, tools) and commits to concrete improvements for the next sprint.
Interviewer signal to watch for
Many candidates forget Backlog Refinement/Grooming is not one of the official four Scrum events, yet it's essential in practice — it's where the team and PO keep upcoming stories estimated, clarified, and "Ready" so Sprint Planning doesn't turn into a discovery session.

Scrum vs Kanban vs XP vs SAFe

"What's the difference between Scrum and Kanban?" is one of the most commonly asked Agile interview questions, and interviewers often extend it to XP and SAFe to see if you understand where each framework actually fits, rather than treating "Agile" as one monolithic thing.

MethodologyCadenceBest FitKey Practice
Scrum Fixed-length sprints (1–4 weeks), events time-boxed within each Product teams building new features with a clear backlog and a need for regular, predictable delivery/inspection cadence Sprint commitment + the four ceremonies; forecast via velocity
Kanban Continuous flow, no fixed iterations Support/ops/maintenance teams, unpredictable or interrupt-driven work, teams that want to reduce WIP and cycle time rather than commit to sprint scope Visual board + explicit WIP limits; pull work only when capacity frees up
XP (Extreme Programming) Very short iterations (often 1–2 weeks), tightly coupled to engineering practice Teams where technical quality/engineering discipline is the bottleneck, high-change environments needing strong safety nets Pair programming, TDD, continuous integration, small releases, collective code ownership
SAFe (Scaled Agile Framework) Program Increments (PI) of ~8–12 weeks, made up of multiple team-level sprints Large enterprises coordinating many Scrum/Kanban teams (Agile Release Trains) against shared roadmaps and dependencies PI Planning, Agile Release Trains, cross-team dependency mapping, Lean-Agile budgeting
Scrumban Hybrid — retains some Scrum cadence (planning, retro) with Kanban's continuous pull and WIP limits Teams transitioning from Scrum to Kanban, or teams with a mix of planned feature work and unplanned support work WIP limits layered onto a lightweight sprint/planning cadence

A useful one-line framing for interviews: Scrum optimizes for a predictable delivery cadence and structured inspect-and-adapt via time-boxed sprints; Kanban optimizes for flow and shortest possible cycle time via continuous delivery and WIP limits. A support/on-call team fielding unpredictable incoming tickets is a textbook case for Kanban; a product team building a new checkout flow with a defined backlog is a textbook case for Scrum.

Common Agile Interview Questions

What's the difference between Scrum and Kanban?
Scrum is a time-boxed, iteration-based framework: work is planned into fixed-length sprints, the team commits to a sprint goal, and progress is inspected through four defined ceremonies. Kanban has no iterations at all — it's a continuous-flow system built around a visual board, explicit work-in-progress (WIP) limits, and pulling the next highest-priority item only when capacity opens up. Scrum's core metric is velocity (points per sprint); Kanban's are cycle time and throughput. I pick Scrum when a team is building planned feature work with a stable-enough backlog to forecast a sprint, and Kanban when work arrives unpredictably (support queues, ops, incident-driven work) where committing to a two-week batch doesn't make sense. In practice, many teams end up in a hybrid ("Scrumban") — keeping planning and retro cadence from Scrum while adopting WIP limits from Kanban for the unplanned-work portion of their load.
How do you estimate story points, and what do you do when the team disagrees?
Story points are a relative measure of effort, complexity, and uncertainty — not hours. I run estimation with Planning Poker: everyone who will do the work reads the story and acceptance criteria, then simultaneously reveals a card from a Fibonacci-like scale (1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13…) so no one anchors on someone else's number. When estimates diverge sharply (say a 2 and a 13), that's the valuable part — we don't average it, we ask the high and low estimator to explain their reasoning, because the gap usually reveals a hidden assumption, an unknown dependency, or someone who's spotted a risk others missed. We re-vote after discussion, and if a story still can't converge or repeatedly lands at 8+, that's a signal the story is too big or too vague and needs to be split. Only the people actually doing the work vote; the PO and Scrum Master facilitate but don't estimate unless they're also coding.
What do you do when it becomes clear the sprint commitment is going to slip?
First, surface it immediately — the worst version of this is discovering it silently at sprint review. In the daily scrum or as soon as a blocker/estimate miss is spotted, I get the team to re-forecast against the sprint goal, not just the story list: can we still deliver the goal by dropping lower-priority stories, or is the goal itself at risk? I loop in the Product Owner early so they can make an informed call — descope non-critical stories back to the backlog, negotiate a reduced sprint goal, or in rare cases pull in help. What I explicitly avoid is quietly extending the sprint or having engineers work overtime to force the original commitment; that erodes the reliability of future estimates and masks the real signal (that something was mis-scoped, under-estimated, or an unknown risk surfaced). The retrospective is where we dig into why it slipped — scope creep, bad estimate, unplanned interruption — and turn that into a concrete process change.
How do you handle a stakeholder who keeps injecting new requirements mid-sprint?
I protect the sprint boundary but not by being rigid for its own sake — I explain the trade-off. The Scrum Master's job (or mine, if I'm leading without that title) is to shield the team from disruption so they can hit the sprint goal, so my default answer to a mid-sprint ask is: "this goes into the Product Backlog and gets prioritized for the next sprint by the PO, unless it's a genuine production emergency." If the request truly can't wait, I make the trade-off explicit and visible: something else in the current sprint has to come out, and it's the Product Owner's call on what, not an unspoken expectation that the team absorbs it for free. I've also learned that the underlying cause is often that the stakeholder doesn't trust the backlog process to get their ask prioritized fast enough — so a longer-term fix is making the PO more available and the backlog refinement cadence tighter so stakeholders feel heard between sprints instead of hijacking the current one.
What's the purpose of a sprint retrospective, and how do you make action items actually stick?
The retrospective is the team's dedicated inspect-and-adapt moment on itself — not the product, the process: what went well, what didn't, and what we'll change. Its value is entirely in follow-through; a retro that produces feel-good discussion but no committed changes is what people call "zombie scrum" — going through the ceremony's motions without the ceremony's function. To make action items stick, I keep the list short (1–2 items, not ten), assign a named owner and a concrete, checkable outcome, and put them at the top of the next retrospective's agenda so the team reviews whether they actually happened before generating a new list. I also track experiments over multiple sprints rather than treating each retro as disconnected — if "reduce PR review turnaround" keeps reappearing unresolved, that's a structural problem (review sla / staffing / process) worth escalating, not just repeating as an action item.
What is technical debt, and how do you advocate for time to address it in an Agile backlog?
Technical debt is the implied cost of future rework created by choosing a quicker, less rigorous solution now instead of a better approach that would take longer — deliberate (a conscious shortcut to hit a date) or accidental (a design that no longer fits as the system grew). It compounds like financial debt: ignored long enough, "interest" shows up as slower delivery, more incidents, and lower morale. To advocate for time against it, I avoid framing it as "engineering wants to gold-plate" and instead translate it into terms the Product Owner and stakeholders act on: this debt is currently costing us N hours per sprint in workarounds, or it's the reason our incident rate on this service is rising, or it's blocking the next three roadmap items from being delivered safely. Concretely, I push for a standing allocation (e.g., a fixed percentage of each sprint, or a dedicated "hardening" sprint every few cycles) rather than hoping debt items win a story-by-story prioritization fight against shiny new features, because on a pure feature-value comparison, debt paydown almost always loses.
How do you handle a disruptive team member or persistent conflict on a Scrum team?
I address it directly and privately first — most disruption (dominating standups, dismissing teammates' estimates, side-channel decisions that bypass the team) comes from a real underlying concern (feeling unheard, distrust of the process, burnout) rather than malice, so a private conversation to understand the "why" comes before any process fix. If it's a recurring pattern rather than a one-off, I bring it into the team's own operating agreement — working agreements the team set for itself (e.g., "one person talks at a time in standup," "estimates are discussed, not overruled") are easier to enforce than a Scrum Master unilaterally policing behavior. Where it's a values or performance issue that working agreements can't fix, that's an escalation to the person's manager — a Scrum Master coaches the team on process, not people's employment, and conflating the two roles usually backfires.
What is Definition of Done, and why does it matter?
Definition of Done is the team's shared, binary checklist — code reviewed, unit and integration tests passing, deployed to a test environment, documentation updated, accessibility/security checks passed, whatever the team agrees is non-negotiable — that a backlog item must satisfy before it's counted as complete. It matters because without it, "done" becomes subjective: one engineer's "done" is code merged, another's is code merged and deployed, and a Product Owner discovers at sprint review that "done" stories still need two more days of QA. A clear DoD keeps velocity numbers meaningful (points only count when work is genuinely shippable) and prevents hidden work from silently rolling into the next sprint disguised as "just cleanup." I distinguish it from Definition of Ready, which is the bar a story must clear before it enters a sprint (clear acceptance criteria, sized, no unresolved dependencies) — DoR guards the front of the pipeline, DoD guards the back.
How do you scale Agile across multiple teams working on the same product?
Below a handful of teams, lightweight coordination (a shared backlog, a Scrum-of-Scrums sync, and clear API/service ownership boundaries) is usually enough. Past that, I've relied on a framework like SAFe or a similar Lean-Agile scaling approach — organizing teams into an Agile Release Train aligned around a shared Program Increment (typically 8–12 weeks made up of several team sprints), with PI Planning as the event where cross-team dependencies get mapped visibly instead of discovered mid-increment. The biggest failure mode at scale isn't picking the wrong framework, it's treating scaling as a process problem when it's usually an architecture problem — teams with tightly coupled services will thrash no matter what ceremony you scale, so I push equally hard on decoupling ownership boundaries (aligned to Conway's Law) as I do on the coordination cadence.
How is Agile testing different from testing in a traditional waterfall project?
In waterfall, testing is a distinct phase that starts after development "completes." In Agile, testing is continuous and embedded within every sprint — test cases are written alongside (or, in XP-style TDD, before) the code, automated regression suites run on every commit via CI, and QA is a collaborator throughout the sprint rather than a gate at the end. This shift-left approach exists because Agile's whole premise is delivering a "Done", potentially shippable increment every sprint — if testing were a separate phase, no increment could ever really be done. Practically, this means acceptance criteria and test scenarios get defined at story-refinement time, not after the code is written, and "Done" in the Definition of Done almost always includes automated test coverage, not just manual sign-off.
Why should velocity never be used as a performance metric?
Velocity is a planning tool — an average of story points completed per sprint, used to forecast how much work a team can realistically take on and when a release might ship. It is not comparable across teams (points are relative to each team's own baseline, not an absolute unit) and it's trivially gameable: if a team knows velocity is being watched by management, point inflation happens almost immediately — stories quietly get bigger numbers, "stretch" work gets logged as done, and the number stops meaning anything. This is a textbook case of Goodhart's Law: once a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure. If leadership asks me to report velocity upward as a productivity indicator, I push back and reframe the conversation around outcomes — cycle time, defect escape rate, customer-facing delivery — rather than an internal estimation number never designed to travel outside the team.
What would make you choose Kanban over Scrum for a particular team?
I'd reach for Kanban when the nature of the incoming work makes a fixed sprint commitment actively counterproductive: a production support team fielding an unpredictable stream of tickets, a platform team handling ad-hoc requests from many internal teams, or any queue where priorities can legitimately change hour-to-hour. Forcing that kind of work into two-week sprint commitments just produces broken commitments and pointless retrospective post-mortems about "why we missed the sprint" when the real answer is "the input pattern doesn't fit iterations." In that setting I'd rather visualize the queue, set WIP limits so people aren't context-switching across ten things at once, and measure cycle time/throughput — and still borrow the parts of Scrum that add value regardless of cadence, like a regular retrospective.

Agile Anti-Patterns to Recognize

Senior and lead-level interviews frequently ask "what dysfunctions have you seen in Agile teams, and how did you address them?" Being able to name these precisely — not just "the team wasn't very Agile" — signals real experience.

Dysfunctions worth naming by name
  • Water-Scrum-Fall — Scrum ceremonies and terminology bolted onto what is still, underneath, a waterfall project: requirements fixed upfront, a big-bang release at the end, sprints used only to track tasks against a plan that was never actually allowed to change. It captures the ceremony overhead of Agile and the rigidity of waterfall, with none of the iterative, adapt-as-you-learn benefit of either.
  • Zombie Scrum / "Agile Theater" — also called Fake Agile, Dark Agile, or "Agile In Name Only": all four ceremonies happen on schedule, but nothing is actually inspected or adapted. Standups become status reports to a manager, retros produce no real action items, and reviews are a formality rather than genuine stakeholder feedback. The team looks Agile from the outside while behaving exactly as it did before adopting it.
  • Sprint scope creep — new work getting pulled into an already-committed sprint without a corresponding trade-off (something else coming out). Left unchecked, it teaches the team that commitments are meaningless and destroys the predictability sprints exist to create.
  • Retrospectives with no follow-through — the same issues get raised sprint after sprint with no owner, no measurable change, and no review of whether last retro's action items actually happened. This is the single fastest way to make a team stop taking retros seriously.
  • Velocity/story points as a performance metric — using a team-relative planning number to rank individuals, compare teams, or set targets. A textbook case of Goodhart's Law: the moment velocity becomes a target instead of a forecasting input, teams inflate points and the metric stops measuring anything real.
  • Standup as a status meeting for management — when the daily scrum turns into each person reporting up to a manager or Scrum Master rather than the team synchronizing with each other on the sprint goal, it loses its purpose and becomes something people dread rather than a useful 15 minutes.
  • Proxy Product Owner / absentee Product Owner — a PO who isn't empowered to make real prioritization calls (everything has to be escalated) or isn't available for the team's questions, forcing developers to guess at intent or block on decisions.

Key Takeaways

  • Agile is a set of values and principles; Scrum, Kanban, XP, and SAFe are specific frameworks that implement them differently — know which fits which situation, not just their definitions in isolation.
  • Fluency with the vocabulary (Sprint, Product/Sprint Backlog, DoD/DoR, velocity vs. story points, the four ceremonies) is table stakes; interviewers use small misuses of these terms as a quick tell.
  • Scrum optimizes for a predictable, time-boxed delivery cadence; Kanban optimizes for continuous flow and shortest cycle time — pick based on the predictability of incoming work, not personal preference.
  • Story points measure relative effort/complexity/uncertainty, never hours, and should only be estimated by the people doing the work; large divergence in estimates is a signal to discuss, not average away.
  • Never let velocity or story points become a performance or cross-team comparison metric — it's a planning input, and treating it as a target invites Goodhart's Law gaming.
  • Retrospectives only have value if action items are few, owned, and reviewed for follow-through next time — ceremonies without genuine inspect-and-adapt are "zombie scrum."
  • Be ready to name real anti-patterns (Water-Scrum-Fall, zombie scrum, scope creep, absentee PO) and describe concretely how you diagnosed and fixed one — this is what separates textbook knowledge from lived experience in a senior/lead interview.
