Body Language Tips

Overview

I've sat on the other side of the table for a lot of interviews, and here's the uncomfortable truth: I'm reading your body language before I've finished reading your resume. Most of that reading happens unconsciously, in the first two or three minutes — how you walk in, how you sit down, what your hands do while you're thinking. None of it overrides a strong technical answer, but it colors how I interpret everything you say afterward. Confident, open body language makes your answers land as confident and open. Closed-off, fidgety body language makes even a good answer sound a little unsure.

This page is deliberately not a list of pop-psychology "power poses" or mind-reading tricks. It's the practical stuff that actually shows up in how interviewers form impressions, grounded in what research on nonverbal communication actually supports — including a myth-bust on one of the most widely repeated (and least supported) claims out there: that looking in a particular direction means someone is lying.

Posture and Positioning

Posture is the first thing I notice, often before you've said a word. It's also the easiest thing to get wrong by overcorrecting — I see candidates sit so rigidly upright that they look like they're bracing for something, which is its own kind of distracting.

  • Sit up straight, but stay relaxed. Good posture signals engagement and self-assurance. Rigid, board-straight posture reads as tense, not confident. Let your shoulders settle rather than pinning them back.
  • Lean in slightly when you're engaged. A small forward lean — especially when the interviewer is talking or you're making an important point — signals interest and attentiveness.
  • Don't slouch, and don't lean back too far. Slumping reads as low energy or disinterest; leaning far back with your arms behind your head reads as overly casual or even a little arrogant for a first interview.
  • Keep both feet grounded. Feet flat on the floor (or one slightly back) is stable and calm. Leg bouncing, foot tapping, or wrapping a leg around the chair leg all read as nervous energy, even if you feel fine.
  • Mirror the interviewer's energy, subtly. If they're leaning forward and animated, match that slightly. If they're calm and measured, don't be the one bouncing off the walls. Subtle mirroring builds rapport; obvious mimicry is just distracting.
  • Walk in like you mean it. Purposeful, unhurried movement when you enter the room (or join the call) sets the tone before the first question is even asked. Shuffling in, fumbling with a bag, or dropping into the chair sets a very different one.
Do Avoid
Sit upright with shoulders relaxed, not pinned back Slouching down in the chair or sitting rigidly stiff
Lean slightly forward when making a key point Leaning far back with arms crossed behind your head
Keep feet flat and still on the floor Bouncing a leg, tapping a foot, or wrapping legs around the chair
Enter the room or join the call with confident, steady movement Rushing in flustered or shuffling awkwardly to your seat
Angle your body toward the interviewer Angling your body toward the door or away from the speaker
Subtly match the interviewer's posture and energy level Sprawling out or taking up visibly more space than the room calls for

Hand Gestures and Arms

Hands are where nerves show up first. I've watched candidates give a perfectly good answer while quietly clicking a pen the entire time, and it's genuinely hard not to focus on the pen.

  • Use open, natural gestures to emphasize points. Talking with your hands — within reason — signals ease and engagement. Pointing to yourself when discussing your own experience, or counting off points on your fingers when listing them, helps the interviewer follow along.
  • Be thoughtful about crossed arms. Crossed arms are commonly read as defensive or closed-off. That said, context matters: some people cross their arms simply because it's physically comfortable, especially in a cold room or a low chair without armrests. One crossed-arm moment isn't a verdict on your engagement — but if it's your default resting position for the whole interview, it's worth practicing sitting a different way, since you don't control how it gets read.
  • Keep hands visible, not in your pockets. Hands tucked away can read as guarded or evasive. Resting them in your lap, on the table, or on the arms of the chair keeps you looking open and at ease.
  • Skip the fidgeting. Pen-clicking, hair-touching, jewelry-fiddling, and face-touching are the classic nervous tells I notice most. None of them mean anything sinister — they just broadcast nerves and pull attention away from what you're saying.
  • Handshake: firm, not crushing. For in-person interviews, one confident pump with eye contact is plenty. A limp handshake undersells you; an overly firm one can come across as trying too hard.
  • Controlled beats excessive. A few purposeful gestures land better than constant hand movement. If your hands are moving the entire time you talk, it can start to look like nervous energy rather than confidence, and it competes with your words for the interviewer's attention.

Eye Contact and Facial Expressions

Eye contact is one of the fastest ways to signal confidence — and one of the easiest to overdo. The commonly cited guideline is roughly 50–70% of the conversation: closer to the higher end while you're listening, and comfortably steady (not a fixed stare) while you're speaking. Staring is just as off-putting as avoiding eye contact altogether.

  • Maintain natural, not constant, eye contact. Hold it for a few seconds at a time, break away naturally, and come back. A relentless, unblinking stare reads as intense rather than confident.
  • It's fine — expected, even — to look away while you think. Breaking eye contact naturally while formulating an answer is completely normal. The problem isn't looking away; it's looking away anxiously, repeatedly, or in a way that avoids re-engaging once you start answering.
  • Smile genuinely. A real smile (sometimes called a "Duchenne smile") engages the muscles around the eyes, not just the mouth — it's what makes a smile look warm rather than performative. Interviewers do pick up on that difference, even if only subconsciously, and it goes a long way toward building rapport.
  • Nod to show you're tracking. Small, occasional nods while the interviewer is talking signal active listening. Constant nodding, on the other hand, can read as eager-to-please rather than engaged.
Myth vs. Reality: Does Looking Left Mean You're Lying?

You've probably heard some version of this: if a candidate looks up and to one side, they're "accessing memory" (telling the truth), and if they look up to the other side, they're "constructing" an answer (lying). This idea comes from Neuro-Linguistic Programming (NLP), specifically its "eye-accessing cues" model developed in the 1970s, which mapped different eye-movement directions to different types of internal cognitive processing.

It's a compelling story, and it's repeated constantly in pop-psychology articles and body-language listicles. But controlled research doesn't back it up. The most direct test is a 2012 study, "The Eyes Don't Have It: Lie Detection and Neuro-Linguistic Programming," led by psychologist Richard Wiseman (University of Hertfordshire) with Caroline Watt (University of Edinburgh) and colleagues from the University of British Columbia, published in the peer-reviewed journal PLOS ONE. Across three separate experiments — coding eye movements of people who were lying versus telling the truth, testing whether teaching people the NLP theory improved their lie-detection accuracy, and analyzing footage from real high-profile press conferences — the researchers found no relationship between eye-movement direction and lying. Knowing the NLP "rule" didn't help anyone spot lies better either. The authors explicitly called on the public and organizations to abandon eye-direction as a lie-detection technique.

What does the broader deception-research literature actually support? Far less than popular culture suggests. Large meta-analyses of nonverbal deception cues (notably the widely cited work led by psychologist Bella DePaulo) find that most proposed "tells" have weak, inconsistent effects at best. General signs of nervousness or discomfort can appear somewhat more often in people who are lying — but they appear just as easily in people who are simply anxious, which describes most interview candidates on their best day. There is no single gesture, glance, or eye movement that reliably indicates dishonesty.

Bottom line: don't try to control which direction you look based on this myth — it isn't grounded in real science, and trying to manage it will just make you look stiffer. And if you're the one interviewing, don't read a candidate's occasional glance away as a red flag; it's normal, it's usually just someone thinking, and over-interpreting isolated gestures is a good way to misjudge a perfectly honest candidate.

Virtual Interview Adjustments

Video interviews change the mechanics of body language even though the underlying goals — looking engaged, confident, and present — stay the same. A few adjustments make a real difference on camera.

  • Look at the camera lens, not the screen. This is the single biggest adjustment. Looking at the interviewer's face on your screen feels natural but reads to them as looking down or away. Looking at the lens simulates real eye contact. You don't need to stare at it constantly — glancing at their video feed occasionally (to read their reaction) is fine, but favor the lens when you're actually speaking.
  • Set the camera at eye level. A laptop propped on a stack of books so the lens is roughly level with your eyes avoids the unflattering "looking down at you" or "looking up your nose" angles.
  • Frame yourself properly. Head and shoulders visible, a reasonable amount of headroom above your head, and centered in frame. Too close feels intense; too far away feels disengaged.
  • Face your light source. Light should be in front of you, not behind you. Sitting with a window behind you silhouettes your face and makes expressions hard to read — face a window or lamp instead.
  • Clean up the background and mute notifications. A cluttered or distracting background pulls focus, and a phone buzzing or a chat notification popping up mid-answer breaks your own concentration as much as the interviewer's.
  • Keep your hands in frame when you gesture. Natural gestures still help on video, but if your hands drop below the frame it looks like you're fidgeting with something offscreen. Sit far enough back that your normal hand movements stay visible.
  • Sit slightly closer than feels default. A little more presence in frame reads as engaged; sitting far back in your chair can read as checked-out, even unintentionally.
  • Test your setup beforehand. Fumbling with a frozen screen, a muted mic, or a "can you hear me?" moment right at the start burns confidence and reads as under-prepared, even though it has nothing to do with your actual qualifications. A five-minute test call removes that risk entirely.

Key Takeaways

Recap
  • Sit upright but relaxed, lean in slightly when engaged, and avoid slouching or leaning too far back.
  • Use open, natural hand gestures; keep hands visible and skip the fidgeting, pen-clicking, and face-touching.
  • A single crossed-arms moment isn't a red flag — context and comfort matter more than any one gesture.
  • Aim for roughly 50–70% eye contact, broken up naturally rather than held in a fixed stare.
  • It's normal to look away while thinking — that's not a "tell," it's how people process questions.
  • The "looking left means lying" claim comes from NLP and has been directly tested (and rejected) by controlled research, notably Wiseman and colleagues' 2012 PLOS ONE study — don't manage your eye direction around it, and don't judge others by it.
  • On video calls, look at the lens rather than the screen, sit at eye level, and test your setup beforehand.
  • None of this works as a rigid checklist to perform — the goal is removing distracting or negative signals so your natural, authentic presence comes through, not replacing it with a stiff routine.
