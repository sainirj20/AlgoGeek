DATABASE TYPES

NewSQL Databases

Overview

NewSQL databases are relational systems engineered to provide full SQL support and ACID transaction guarantees while scaling horizontally across many commodity servers and, often, multiple geographic regions — something traditional RDBMS could not do natively. They emerged to close the gap left by the two prior paradigms: relational databases give you SQL and ACID but scale vertically (or need complex manual sharding), while NoSQL databases scale horizontally from day one but typically trade away strong consistency and rich SQL to get that scale cheaply. NewSQL systems try to give you both sides at once, typically via automatic data sharding, Raft- or Paxos-based consensus for replication, and distributed transaction protocols.

Common characteristics across the category: ACID transactions maintained across a distributed cluster; automatic sharding/rebalancing with no manual partitioning by application developers; consensus algorithms (Raft or Paxos) for synchronous replication and per-shard leader election; a SQL interface, often PostgreSQL- or MySQL-wire-compatible; built-in high availability and geo-distribution; cloud-native, container/Kubernetes-friendly deployment models; and, increasingly, added vector search and HTAP (Hybrid Transactional/Analytical Processing) capabilities for AI-era workloads. See the Relational Databases (RDBMS) page for how traditional relational systems and NoSQL compare on these same dimensions.

Why NewSQL Exists

NewSQL is the right fit for applications needing both strong consistency/SQL and the ability to scale writes horizontally across nodes or regions — global user-account systems, financial ledgers with geographic distribution requirements, systems needing "five nines" availability with automatic failover, and regulated industries needing data residency (pinning data to specific regions) alongside global scale. It's the wrong fit for small-to-mid scale applications where a single well-tuned traditional RDBMS (or its managed cloud variant) is simpler, cheaper, and sufficient; for teams without the operational sophistication to run a distributed system; for workloads dominated by complex, deeply nested joins where distributed transaction coordination adds latency a single-node database wouldn't incur; and for latency-sensitive single-region apps that don't need geo-distribution.

Real-world example
A global fintech company needing account balances and transactions to stay strongly consistent while serving users across North America, Europe, and Asia — with per-region data residency requirements and no tolerance for a single point of failure — is the canonical NewSQL use case, which is why banks and fintechs are among the heaviest adopters of Spanner, CockroachDB, TiDB, and YugabyteDB.

Named Products

Google Cloud Spanner

NewSQL / Distributed SQL, Managed Cloud Service — Spanner is Google's globally distributed, strongly consistent relational database, originally developed for Google's own internal infrastructure (published as a research paper in 2012) and made available as a Google Cloud product in 2017. It's widely considered the database that proved distributed ACID transactions with global consistency were achievable at scale, and it directly inspired the broader NewSQL movement. Full ACID transactions with external (global) consistency across regions rely on TrueTime, Google's globally synchronized clock API (GPS plus atomic clocks) that orders transactions correctly across data centers without a central coordinator; Spanner uses Paxos consensus for replication and dynamic resharding ("splits") to distribute load. In 2025 it expanded into a multi-model database unifying relational, vector, graph, key-value, and full-text search in one engine, with built-in AI integrations (ML.PREDICT, MCP Toolbox for Databases, a Gemini CLI extension). It's recognized as a Leader in Gartner's Magic Quadrant for Cloud DBMS and ranked #1 for Lightweight Transactions in Gartner's 2025 Critical Capabilities report. Best for global applications needing strong consistency across regions without manual sharding; not a fit for single-region, budget-constrained, or multi-cloud projects, since Spanner is Google Cloud-only and generally pricier than open-source alternatives.

Real-world example
Target uses Spanner Graph to blend graph traversals with vector search over proprietary embeddings, delivering real-time product recommendations; Walmart, Goldman Sachs, Shopify, and MercadoLibre run demanding transactional workloads on Spanner. A March 2025 Forrester study found a representative organization achieved 132% ROI and $7.74M in benefits over three years using Spanner.
ProsCons
  • Proven global-scale strong (external) consistency via TrueTime — a genuinely unique architectural achievement
  • Virtually unlimited horizontal scalability with no manual sharding
  • Fully managed by Google — no cluster operations burden
  • 2025 multi-model expansion (graph, vector, full-text) reduces the need for separate specialized databases
  • Google Cloud-only — full vendor lock-in, no on-prem or multi-cloud option
  • Generally more expensive than self-hosted NewSQL alternatives
  • Steeper learning curve; schema/query patterns differ from single-node RDBMS habits
  • Less SQL feature parity with PostgreSQL than some PostgreSQL-wire-compatible NewSQL competitors

CockroachDB

NewSQL / Distributed SQL, Open Core, Multi-Cloud — CockroachDB is a distributed SQL database created by ex-Google engineers (from the Spanner/Bigtable teams) at Cockroach Labs, explicitly designed to survive failures ("if you kill a cockroach, more come back") while providing PostgreSQL wire-protocol compatibility. Unlike Spanner's Google-only model, it's designed to be deployable anywhere — on-prem, any cloud, or multi-cloud/hybrid. Data is split into "ranges," each replicated to at least 3 nodes by default using the Raft consensus protocol; its storage engine, Pebble, is a Go-based, RocksDB-inspired LSM-tree key-value store built in-house by Cockroach Labs. It supports geo-partitioning to pin specific data to specific regions for latency/data-residency compliance, is PostgreSQL wire-compatible for easy migration, supports AI-optimized vector indexing compatible with pgvector's API, and gained an IBM OEM partnership (announced 2025) bringing it into IBM's modernization offerings. Best for resilient, always-on distributed SQL across multiple clouds/regions — user identity systems, payments, order/inventory management, and banks modernizing legacy mainframe applications. Not a fit for small single-node apps or teams needing 100% PostgreSQL feature parity.

Worked example — CockroachDB's Raft replication quorum Default replication factor: 3 copies of each "range" (shard), typically spread across separate nodes/AZs  |  Raft consensus needs a write quorum of ⌈(3+1)/2⌉ = 2 of 3 replicas to acknowledge before a transaction commits.
Because only a majority — not all 3 — must agree, the cluster keeps serving reads and writes after losing any single node or AZ per range; the failed replica simply catches up and rejoins once it's back, with no manual failover step.
Real-world example
Identity and access management providers Ory, Teleport, and AuthZed all built their core datastore on CockroachDB specifically to support always-on, horizontally scalable architectures; Cockroach Labs counts several Fortune 50 banks among customers using it to modernize legacy applications, and IBM has OEM'd CockroachDB to bring distributed SQL into its modernization products for enterprise (including mainframe-adjacent) clients.
ProsCons
  • Multi-cloud/on-prem/hybrid deployment flexibility — no single-vendor cloud lock-in, unlike Spanner
  • PostgreSQL wire-protocol compatibility eases adoption and tooling reuse
  • Strong resilience by design — survives node and datacenter failures with automatic recovery
  • Geo-partitioning elegantly supports regulatory data-residency requirements
  • Distributed transaction latency overhead vs. a single-node PostgreSQL instance
  • Not 100% PostgreSQL feature-compatible — some extensions/edge-case features missing
  • Operational complexity of running and tuning a multi-node distributed cluster
  • Enterprise features (encryption, some multi-region controls) require paid licensing

TiDB

NewSQL / Distributed SQL, Open Source, HTAP — TiDB is an open-source distributed SQL database created by PingCAP (founded 2015), designed to be MySQL wire-protocol compatible while providing horizontal scalability and, distinctively, native HTAP (Hybrid Transactional/Analytical Processing) — real-time analytics directly on live transactional data with no separate ETL pipeline into a warehouse. It separates compute and storage, running two storage engines side by side on the same data: TiKV (row-based, for OLTP) and TiFlash (columnar, for OLAP). In 2025 PingCAP unveiled TiDB X, a new architecture making object storage the backbone of TiDB for further compute/storage decoupling and elastic scaling, plus a unified query engine fusing vectors, knowledge graphs, JSON, and SQL for agentic AI workloads. Best for workloads needing simultaneous OLTP and real-time analytics on the same dataset, MySQL applications outgrowing a single node, and financial services needing high availability and disaster tolerance. Not worth the complexity for simple OLTP-only apps with no analytical component.

Real-world example
Rakuten adopted TiDB to power its loyalty program aggregation layer, handling roughly 25,000 writes per second alongside 5,000 concurrent queries with about 17ms average response times; a fintech-style company replaced MySQL with TiDB to power order creation, billing, and payments across 500+ cities, achieving 3x data compression and 99.99% uptime through 400% growth; Dify.AI consolidated nearly half a million database containers into a single TiDB system.
ProsCons
  • Native HTAP eliminates separate ETL/analytics pipelines for real-time reporting on live data
  • MySQL wire-protocol compatibility eases migration from existing MySQL apps
  • Separates compute and storage for independent, elastic scaling
  • Strong track record scaling to tens of thousands of writes/second with high availability
  • Running two storage engines (TiKV + TiFlash) adds operational and conceptual complexity
  • Not 100% MySQL feature-compatible in every edge case
  • Smaller mainstream adoption/talent pool than MySQL/PostgreSQL
  • Self-managing a TiDB cluster requires meaningfully more expertise than a managed single-node RDBMS

VoltDB (Volt Active Data)

NewSQL / In-Memory Distributed SQL — VoltDB (rebranded "Volt Active Data") is an in-memory, ACID-compliant relational database created by database pioneer Michael Stonebraker (also a co-creator of Postgres) along with Sam Madden and Daniel Abadi, purpose-built for extreme-throughput, low-latency transactional workloads such as telecom billing, real-time fraud detection, and streaming/IoT data ingestion. Storage is fully in-memory; the architecture is shared-nothing across CPU cores/servers; and stored procedures execute serially to completion in a single thread without locking or latching, avoiding lock-manager overhead and enabling extremely high, predictable throughput. Synchronous multi-master replication provides built-in high availability, and lightweight "command logging" gives durability without traditional write-ahead logging. It has strong native ingestion from Kafka/Kinesis. Best for ultra-high-throughput, low-latency workloads with real-time decisioning — telecom billing/charging, fraud detection, gaming leaderboards, IoT ingestion, ad-tech bidding. Not a fit for general-purpose backends or workloads needing rich ad hoc analytics.

Real-world example
Mahindra Comviva, a major telecom software provider, selected VoltDB after a global search specifically for its reputation as "the world's fastest and smartest in-memory database" for telecom-grade real-time charging and billing, where transactions must be processed within milliseconds at very high volume. Peak Games (mobile gaming) also selected VoltDB to power real-time user segmentation and personalization.
ProsCons
  • Extremely high transaction throughput and very low, predictable latency
  • ACID guarantees maintained even at extreme speed via serialized single-threaded stored procedures
  • Purpose-built native streaming ingestion (Kafka, Kinesis) for real-time pipelines
  • Synchronous replication provides strong built-in high availability
  • In-memory architecture means cost scales with RAM — pricier than disk-based scaling for very large datasets
  • Niche use case, not a general-purpose OLTP/analytics database
  • Best performance requires designing logic as stored procedures — a different dev model than typical ORM-driven code
  • Smaller community, talent pool, and market mindshare than Spanner, CockroachDB, or YugabyteDB

YugabyteDB

NewSQL / Distributed SQL, Open Source, PostgreSQL-Compatible — YugabyteDB is an open-source distributed SQL database explicitly designed to take PostgreSQL and give it horizontal, cloud-native scalability, high availability, and geo-distribution; its transaction design is directly modeled on Google Spanner's architecture, aiming to let PostgreSQL users get NewSQL-style scale without leaving the PostgreSQL ecosystem. It uses Raft consensus for replication and hybrid logical clocks for distributed transaction ordering — a lighter-weight software alternative to Spanner's specialized TrueTime hardware. The architecture splits into the YQL (Yugabyte Query Layer, the PostgreSQL-compatible SQL API) and DocDB, a distributed document-storage layer built on a heavily customized RocksDB, with one RocksDB instance per "tablet" (shard). Recent updates add AI-native vector search using HNSW indexing via a pluggable "Vector LSM," plus strong Change Data Capture (CDC) support. Best for teams wanting PostgreSQL compatibility combined with horizontal scale and geo-distribution — financial markets infrastructure, cybersecurity platforms, retail systems needing global resilience. Not worth it for apps that don't need distributed scale, where a single PostgreSQL instance (or Aurora PostgreSQL) is simpler and cheaper.

Real-world example
Paramount+ deployed its streaming platform across multiple cloud regions on YugabyteDB to maintain high availability and resilience during peak viewership events; General Motors, Plume, and Xignite use it for real-time event streaming at scale with geo-distribution; Wells Fargo, Kroger, Hudson River Trading, and Narvar are also cited users, and a major streaming media company used YugabyteDB's CDC capabilities specifically to migrate off MySQL.
ProsCons
  • Strong PostgreSQL compatibility — reuses existing drivers, ORMs, and developer skills
  • Horizontal scalability and multi-region geo-distribution modeled on Spanner's proven architecture
  • Open source with a fully self-hostable option — avoids single-cloud lock-in, unlike Spanner
  • Raft consensus + hybrid logical clocks provide strong consistency without specialized hardware
  • Not 100% PostgreSQL extension/feature-compatible — some extensions don't work in distributed mode
  • Distributed transaction latency overhead vs. a single-node PostgreSQL instance
  • Operational complexity of running, tuning, and monitoring a distributed multi-node cluster
  • Enterprise features and the managed service (Yugabyte Cloud/Anywhere) require commercial licensing

Common Interview Questions

How does NewSQL differ from traditional NoSQL databases like Cassandra or MongoDB?
Both scale horizontally, but NewSQL keeps full ACID transactions and a standard SQL interface across the distributed cluster via consensus protocols (Raft/Paxos) and distributed transaction coordination, whereas classic NoSQL databases typically relax to eventual consistency (the BASE model) and product-specific query languages to get that scale more cheaply.
Why would a bank choose CockroachDB over Google Cloud Spanner?
Deployment flexibility — CockroachDB runs on-prem, in any cloud, or across a hybrid mix, avoiding Spanner's Google Cloud-only lock-in, and it's PostgreSQL wire-compatible, easing migration; Cockroach Labs also has an IBM OEM deal specifically targeting banks modernizing legacy/mainframe applications. Spanner wins on proven global external consistency via TrueTime and being fully managed with no cluster operations burden.
What is HTAP, and how does TiDB implement it without a separate ETL pipeline?
HTAP (Hybrid Transactional/Analytical Processing) means running real-time analytics directly on live transactional data. TiDB does this by running two storage engines side by side on the same data — TiKV (row-based, for OLTP) and TiFlash (columnar, for OLAP) — so a query can hit whichever engine fits, with no batch ETL job copying data into a separate warehouse.
Why is VoltDB a poor fit for a general-purpose e-commerce backend even though it's extremely fast?
VoltDB's speed comes from a specialized model — fully in-memory storage and single-threaded stored procedures executed serially to completion — optimized for simple, repeatable, extreme-throughput transactions like telecom billing or fraud scoring, not for rich ad hoc analytical queries or the ORM-driven development style typical of e-commerce apps; its RAM-based cost scaling also makes it expensive for very large, less latency-critical datasets.
How does YugabyteDB achieve Spanner-like distributed consistency without Spanner's specialized TrueTime hardware?
It uses Raft consensus for replication combined with hybrid logical clocks to order distributed transactions — a software-only alternative to TrueTime's GPS/atomic-clock-synchronized physical clocks — trading a small amount of Spanner's proven precision for a fully self-hostable, open-source system with no dependency on Google's specialized infrastructure.

Key Takeaways

Remember
NewSQL exists to answer one question: what if you didn't have to choose between SQL/ACID and horizontal scale? Spanner is the reference architecture (and the only one with genuinely unique TrueTime-based global consistency) but is Google Cloud-only; CockroachDB and YugabyteDB bring similar distributed-SQL guarantees to any cloud or on-prem, with YugabyteDB leaning on PostgreSQL compatibility and CockroachDB on resilience-by-design; TiDB adds native HTAP for teams wanting live analytics without an ETL pipeline; VoltDB is the outlier — a narrow, in-memory specialist for extreme-throughput transactional workloads rather than a general-purpose distributed SQL database. All of them trade single-node simplicity for meaningfully more operational complexity.
