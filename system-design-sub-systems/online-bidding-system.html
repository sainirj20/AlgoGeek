SYSTEM DESIGN

Online Bidding System (eBay-Style Auctions)

A complete, interview-ready design walkthrough: requirements, capacity estimation, API & data model, the SQL-vs-NoSQL split, serialized per-auction bid acceptance, decoupled real-time fan-out, auto-close and anti-sniping, Saga-style cross-service consistency, sharding & replication, proxy bidding, abuse prevention, and follow-up interview questions.

Problem Statement

Design an online bidding/auction platform in the style of eBay: a seller lists an item for a fixed duration (e.g. 24 hours, 3 days, or 7 days), buyers place competing bids that must strictly exceed the current highest bid by at least a minimum increment, and the listing closes automatically at its scheduled end time. At the instant of close, the system must determine — unambiguously, permanently, and auditably — who the winning bidder is, and notify both the winner and the seller so an order/payment flow can begin. While the auction is live, every bidder and every "watcher" following the item should see the current highest bid update in near real time.

Why does this problem matter in an interview?

Most "design X at scale" interview problems reward eventual consistency almost everywhere — a stale feed, a delayed like-count, a slightly-late notification are all acceptable. This problem is the exception: the single most important requirement is that exactly one bidder is unambiguously the highest bidder at the exact instant the auction closes, and that fact must be provably, auditably correct even when two bids race in the same millisecond on a hot item. A strong candidate identifies this immediately as the load-bearing constraint and designs everything else — real-time fan-out, catalog browsing, cross-service updates — around never compromising it, while also never letting it slow those other paths down.

Why build a dedicated Bidding Service instead of folding bids into the listing record?

  • A hot, viral item can receive a burst of write traffic (bids) concentrated in its final seconds, while the same item is simultaneously being read thousands of times per second by browsers loading its listing page — mixing those two workloads on one service/table means read-heavy browsing traffic and write-heavy bid traffic contend for the same resources.
  • Bid acceptance has a strict-consistency, strict-ordering requirement; listing browsing does not. Separating the services lets each be built, scaled, and replicated according to its own actual requirement instead of forcing one lowest-common-denominator design on both.
  • It isolates the auction-fairness-critical code path (bid validation, ordering, close semantics) from the much larger, much more frequently changing surface area of listing search, recommendations, and catalog browsing.
  • It gives us a single, narrow place to reason about — and prove — correctness under concurrent access, rather than scattering that responsibility across a general-purpose listing CRUD service.

Clarifying Questions to Ask the Interviewer

Spend the first few minutes narrowing scope — this problem has several "it depends" forks that materially change the design.

  1. Do we close on a hard deadline (all bids after close_time are rejected, no exceptions), or do we need anti-sniping auto-extension (a late bid pushes the close time out)?
  2. Is proxy/max-bid auto-bidding in scope, or do bidders always enter an explicit bid amount?
  3. Do we need reserve prices (seller's hidden minimum) and/or a "Buy It Now" instant-purchase option, or is this a pure ascending-bid auction?
  4. Is payment/settlement processing in scope, or does our design stop at "produce a winner and hand off to an order/payment platform"?
  5. What's the minimum bid increment policy — a flat amount, or a tiered schedule that grows with price (e.g. $1 increments under $25, $5 increments over $100)?
  6. Can a bidder retract or cancel a bid, and if so, under what constraints (e.g. only within a grace window, or only if the seller/platform approves)?
  7. Do watchers need just the current price, or also personalized "you've been outbid" alerts?
  8. What scale should we design for — total concurrently live auctions, and specifically the concurrency envelope of a single viral/hot item in its last minute?
  9. Are there legal/dispute-resolution requirements around retaining a full, immutable bid history per auction?
  10. Single region to start, or does the strict-consistency requirement need to be designed for multi-region from day one?
Interview tip
Don't stall waiting for every answer — state a reasonable assumption out loud ("I'll assume a hard deadline plus a short anti-sniping extension window, since that's the pattern real auction platforms converged on, and I'll assume proxy bidding is in scope since it's the feature most likely to generate a meaty follow-up discussion") and keep moving. Naming the fork and picking a defensible side is exactly the judgment interviewers want to see.

Requirements and Goals

Functional Requirements

  1. Sellers can create a time-boxed listing: title, description, images, starting price, minimum bid increment, auction duration/close_time, and optionally a reserve price.
  2. Buyers can place a bid that must strictly exceed the current highest bid by at least the minimum increment; an insufficient bid is rejected with a clear reason.
  3. The system enforces a single, unambiguous highest bidder at the moment of close and notifies the winner (and the seller) as soon as the auction closes.
  4. Everyone watching an auction — bidders and passive watchers alike — sees the current highest bid update live, without refreshing the page.
  5. Buyers can add/remove auctions to a personal watchlist and view their own bid history across auctions.
  6. Support proxy/max-bid auto-bidding: a bidder sets a private ceiling, and the system automatically bids up on their behalf, up to that ceiling, whenever they're outbid.
  7. Auto-close semantics are configurable: a hard deadline, or a hard deadline with an anti-sniping extension policy for bids arriving very close to close_time.
  8. Every bid is retained in a full, immutable, strictly-ordered audit trail per auction for dispute resolution.
  9. Outbid bidders are notified in near real time so they have a fair chance to respond before close.
  10. Buyers can browse and search a very large, constantly changing catalog of live listings by category, keyword, price range, and time-remaining.

Non-Functional Requirements

  1. Strict consistency and strict ordering for bid acceptance: on a hot auction, no bid may be lost, silently dropped, or accepted out of order relative to when it actually arrived — this is the single defining NFR of the whole system, unlike most system-design problems where "eventually consistent is fine."
  2. High read scalability for browsing and watching: catalog search and listing views are read-heavy and can tolerate eventual consistency — a listing's displayed price lagging the true current price by a second or two is a cosmetic issue, not a correctness bug.
  3. Resilience of the close process: the auction-close job must fire exactly once per auction — never zero times (an auction that never resolves) and never more than once (which could re-run winner selection against a mutated state).
  4. Auditability and dispute resolution: every bid must be traceable — who placed it, for how much, at what time, in what order relative to every other bid on that auction — because auction fairness disputes depend entirely on this being provably correct.
  5. Low latency for real-time updates, but never at the cost of the bid-acceptance path — fan-out to watchers must never block or slow down whether a bid is accepted.
  6. Horizontal scalability across a very large number of independent, concurrent auctions, while isolating one hot auction's write load so it can never degrade every other auction sharing the cluster.
  7. Availability of the browsing/catalog path even during partial write-side degradation — a bidding hiccup on one auction should never take down the ability to browse the rest of the catalog.

Extended / Out of Scope

We'll go deep on bid acceptance and ordering, real-time fan-out, auto-close/anti-sniping, cross-service consistency (Saga), data partitioning, proxy bidding, and abuse prevention. We call out but don't build the internals of payment/settlement processing, chargeback/dispute-resolution workflows, image storage/CDN pipelines, seller reputation and reviews, or search-relevance ranking internals — all real subsystems, layered on top of (or alongside) this core.

State assumptions explicitly and let them drive every downstream decision — the numbers below use eBay's own real order of magnitude for live listings as a grounding reference.

System / API Design

Buyers, sellers, and watchers all go through a single, versioned API. Every field needed for ordering, idempotency, and rendering travels with the request so the Bidding Service never needs a side lookup just to validate a bid.

idempotency_key on place-bid is what makes retries safe under network flakiness at exactly the moment a bidder can least afford a duplicate submission (the final seconds of a close race). Data Model and Database Choice

Nature of the Data

  • The bid-acceptance transaction and the resulting order/payment records need ACID guarantees — "who currently has the highest bid" is a strict-consistency requirement, not a best-effort one.
  • Listing/catalog metadata is high-volume (billions of rows), read-heavy, and needs full-text search — a textbook fit for a document/NoSQL store that tolerates eventual consistency in exchange for horizontal read scale.
  • These two datasets have almost opposite requirements, which is exactly why they live in two different kinds of stores rather than one "good enough for everything" database.

Why SQL for the Bid-Acceptance Transaction and Order Records

Accepting a bid is fundamentally a conditional update: "set current_price = X and current_high_bidder = Y, but only if the current_price is still what I last read." That's a compare-and-swap, and a relational database gives us row-level transactions, unique constraints, and (if needed) serializable isolation to make that operation provably correct under concurrent writers. Order and payment records inherit the same requirement: an order must reference exactly one winning bid, and that relationship must never be ambiguous.

Why NoSQL/Document Store for Listing and Catalog Metadata

A listing record (title, description, images, category, denormalized current price for display) is read enormously more often than it's written, needs to scale horizontally to billions of documents, and benefits from a search index (category facets, keyword search, price-range filters) far more than it benefits from relational joins or transactional guarantees. A document store (or a search-optimized store like Elasticsearch/OpenSearch backing a document database) is the standard fit, and it's completely fine if the displayed "current price" on a listing page is a second or two stale — the SQL bid ledger is always the actual source of truth.

Interview tip
If pushed on "why not just use one database everywhere": a single store optimized for catalog-scale eventual-consistency reads would make the bid-acceptance CAS unsafe under concurrency; a single store optimized for the bid ledger's strict transactions would not horizontally scale to a multi-petabyte, billions-of-documents catalog. The split isn't indecision — it's matching each dataset to the consistency/scale trade-off it actually needs.

High-Level Design

Sellers create listings through a Listing/Seller Service; buyers browse and search through a read-optimized Catalog/Search store fed by that service. When a buyer places a bid, the request is routed to a dedicated Bidding Service, which serializes acceptance per auction_id and commits the outcome to a strongly consistent SQL bid ledger. Once committed, an event is published to a broker; a Real-Time/Notification Service consumes it and fans the update out over WebSocket to everyone watching that auction — entirely decoupled from the acceptance path itself, so slow or backed-up fan-out can never delay or block whether a bid is accepted.

Figure 1. High-level architecture — the read-heavy browsing path (Listing/Seller Service, Buyer/Account Service, Catalog/Search) is physically separate from the write-heavy Bidding Service, which is the only path that touches the strongly consistent SQL bid ledger; a broker decouples real-time fan-out and order creation from bid acceptance itself.

Key Design Principles Reflected in the Diagram

  • The Bidding Service is deliberately separate from listing/catalog operations — a hot item's write-heavy bid traffic never contends with the read-heavy browsing traffic hitting the same listing's page.
  • The SQL bid ledger is the single source of truth for "who is currently winning," — the catalog's displayed price is a downstream, eventually consistent copy, never the authority.
  • The broker decouples "bid accepted" from everything that happens next — real-time fan-out and order creation are async consumers that can lag, retry, or fail independently without ever affecting whether the bid itself was accepted.
  • Every arrow into the Bidding Service is routed by auction_id, setting up the serialized, per-auction acceptance path covered next.

Deep Dive — Serialized, Strictly-Ordered Bid Acceptance Per Auction

This is the core correctness problem in the entire system: two bidders submit a bid on the same item within the same millisecond. Whoever "wins" that race must be determined by a strict, auditable, reproducible ordering — not by whichever database replica happened to process it first, and not by wall-clock timestamps alone (clocks skew; two servers can disagree on which request was "really" first).

Approach 1 — Single-Writer-Per-Auction-ID (Actor / Partitioned Queue)

Route every bid for a given auction_id through consistent hashing to exactly one Bidding Service partition/worker (an "actor" in the actor-model sense, or a consumer reading a Kafka partition keyed by auction_id). Because only one worker ever processes bids for that auction, there is no concurrent access to reason about at all — the worker simply processes its inbound queue in strict arrival order and assigns a monotonically increasing sequence_no per auction. This converts a distributed-concurrency problem into an ordinary single-threaded queue-processing problem for any one auction, while still scaling horizontally across the huge number of different auctions (each gets its own worker/partition).

Approach 2 — Optimistic Concurrency (Compare-and-Swap on auction_state)

Keep a single auction_state row per auction with a version column. A bid acceptance is a conditional SQL update: UPDATE auction_state SET current_price=:new, current_high_bidder=:bidder, version=version+1 WHERE auction_id=:id AND version=:expected. Only one concurrent transaction can match the expected version; the loser's update affects zero rows, and that request is retried against the new state (and likely rejected, since the price has already moved past its bid). This works even without a dedicated single-writer worker, at the cost of a retry loop under heavy contention on one row.

Recommended: Combine Both

Partition by auction_id for locality and to minimize contention (Approach 1), and still protect the underlying row with an optimistic version check at the database layer as a defense-in-depth safety net (Approach 2) — so correctness never depends solely on the partitioning layer behaving perfectly. The single-writer partitioning keeps the common case fast (no retries needed, since only one worker is ever touching the row at a time); the CAS at the database layer guarantees correctness is provable directly from the data, which matters enormously for dispute resolution.

Interview tip
If asked "why not just use the database's serializable isolation level for everything and skip the single-writer partitioning": you can, but under heavy contention on one hot row, serializable isolation degenerates into exactly the same retry-storm behavior as naive optimistic concurrency — the single-writer partition is what actually removes the contention, rather than just detecting and retrying around it.

Deep Dive — Real-Time Bid Fan-Out Decoupled From the Acceptance Path

Once a bid is committed to the SQL ledger, everyone watching that auction needs to see the new current price. But the fan-out step must never sit on the critical path of accepting the bid — a bidder's "your bid was accepted" response must come back the instant the SQL transaction commits, regardless of how many (or how few) watchers are currently connected, and regardless of any backlog in the notification pipeline.

Why Decoupling Is Non-Negotiable Here

Fan-out size varies by four to five orders of magnitude across auctions — from zero watchers to a million. If fan-out were synchronous (the Bidding Service itself pushes to every WebSocket connection before acknowledging the bid), a viral item's massive watcher count would directly slow down bid acceptance on that exact item — precisely the item where fast, fair acceptance matters most. Publishing an event and returning immediately, then letting an independent Real-Time/Notification Service handle the fan-out asynchronously, keeps these two very different scaling problems (write-path correctness vs. read-path fan-out) from ever coupling to each other.

Figure 3. The bidder gets an acknowledgment the instant the SQL commit succeeds (fast, synchronous path). Fan-out to watchers is entirely asynchronous and downstream of a broker — a slow or backlogged Real-Time Service can never delay whether a bid is accepted.

Handling the Very Large Fan-Out Case

For an auction with hundreds of thousands of watchers, the WebSocket Gateway itself must shard subscriptions across many stateful nodes (see "Data Partitioning and Replication" below) and may batch/coalesce rapid successive price updates (e.g. at most one push per 250ms per connection) rather than pushing every single accepted bid individually to every watcher during a closing-seconds bidding war — watchers care about "the current price, right now," not a literal blow-by-blow of every increment.

Deep Dive — Auction Close and Anti-Sniping Extension

At close_time, the auction must stop accepting bids and declare a winner — exactly once. Two closely related problems live here: guaranteeing the close fires reliably and exactly once, and deciding how to handle "sniping."

The Sniping Problem

In a hard-deadline auction, a rational bidder's best strategy is often to wait until the very last second to bid, denying every other bidder any chance to respond. This produces a poor experience (a bidder who would have happily paid more never gets the opportunity to) and, at scale, a burst of bid traffic concentrated in literally the final second of every auction — which is also the capacity-planning nightmare referenced in the estimation section above.

Anti-Sniping Extension

The common mitigation (used by many real auction platforms): if a bid arrives within a short window of the scheduled close_time (e.g. the last 2 minutes), extend close_time by a fixed amount (e.g. +2 minutes) from that bid's timestamp. This repeats for as long as bids keep arriving inside the window — the auction only truly closes once a full anti-snipe window passes with no new bid. This preserves the fairness property ("everyone gets a chance to respond to a late bid") without turning every auction into an indefinite, always-open listing, since genuinely uncontested auctions still close right on schedule.

Figure 4. A bid arriving inside the anti-snipe window pushes the close time out; this can repeat, but the auction always closes once a full window passes with no new bid — genuinely uncontested auctions still close exactly on schedule.

Guaranteeing the Close Fires Exactly Once

The close job is a durable, idempotent, per-auction scheduled task — not a naive cron sweep. A common implementation: a scheduler service holds a durable timer per auction_id (backed by a database row or a scheduled-message feature of the broker); when the timer fires, the closing worker acquires a per-auction lock (or relies on the same single-writer-per-auction-id partitioning from the bid-acceptance deep dive), re-checks whether any bid landed inside the anti-snipe window since the timer was set, and either re-arms the timer for the extended close_time or performs the actual close: mark auction_state.status = CLOSED, freeze current_high_bidder as the winner, and publish an auction_closed event. Because this status flip is itself a conditional update guarded by the same version/CAS mechanism as bid acceptance, a duplicate close-timer firing (e.g. after a worker crash and retry) is a safe no-op — the second attempt's CAS simply fails because the status is already CLOSED.

Deep Dive — Saga-Style Cross-Service Consistency

"Bid accepted" is not the end of the story — it also needs to update the auction's displayed price in the catalog (a different store), notify every watcher (a different service), and, once the auction closes, create an order and kick off payment for the winner (yet another service). This is a multi-step operation spanning multiple services and multiple data stores, which rules out a single distributed transaction (two-phase commit across services doesn't scale, and would re-introduce exactly the coupling we deliberately avoided in the fan-out deep dive). The standard pattern here is a Saga: a sequence of local transactions, each in its own service, coordinated either by orchestration (a central coordinator tells each service what to do next) or choreography (each service reacts to events from the previous step, with no central coordinator).

Orchestrated vs. Choreographed

A pragmatic design uses both: choreography (simple event fan-out) for the low-stakes "update catalog price, notify watchers" steps, and a lightweight orchestrator for the higher-stakes "auction closed → create order → hand off to payment" sequence, where we want an explicit, queryable saga state and clear compensating actions if a downstream step fails.

Figure 5. Steps 1–2 are the only strictly-ordered, transactional part of the flow; everything downstream is an independent local transaction reacting to events — a failure at step 6 is handled by retrying or alerting, never by rolling back the already-committed bid.

What Happens When a Downstream Step Fails?

The bid ledger (step 1) is never a candidate for rollback once committed — it's the one piece of state that must be immutable and permanent for audit purposes. If order creation (step 6) fails, the compensating action is to retry with backoff and, after exhausting retries, alert operations for manual intervention — not to "undo" the auction result. This is a deliberate asymmetry: earlier steps in the chain are increasingly expensive/impossible to compensate (you cannot un-notify a watcher, and you must never un-decide a winner), so the design pushes all recoverable failure handling to the last, most compensable step.

Data Partitioning and Replication

Sharding follows the same principle throughout: isolate one hot auction's write traffic from every other auction, and let the much larger catalog scale independently on its own axis.

  • Bids and auction_state are sharded by auction_id (consistent hashing), so a single hot auction's entire write load lands on one shard/partition and can never spill over to affect any other auction's throughput or latency.
  • Replication for the SQL bid ledger favors strong consistency — a leader-follower setup (or a consensus protocol such as Raft) with synchronous or near-synchronous replication to at least one follower before acknowledging a commit, so a leader failure doesn't lose an already-accepted bid.
  • Catalog/listing metadata is sharded separately, at a much larger scale (by listing_id or category, across far more shards than the bid ledger needs), with asynchronous multi-replica replication — eventual consistency is an explicit, accepted trade-off here in exchange for horizontal read scale.
Figure 6. The bid ledger's ring is sharded by auction_id with synchronous replication for strict consistency; the catalog is sharded far more finely by listing_id with asynchronous replication, because the two stores optimize for opposite ends of the consistency/scale trade-off.

Feature-Specific Logic: Proxy/Max-Bid Auto-Bidding

Proxy bidding is the classic eBay mechanic: instead of manually re-bidding every time someone outbids you, a bidder sets a private max_bid_amount once, and the system automatically places the minimum bid necessary to keep them the current highest bidder — one increment above the current price, or one increment above the second-highest active max, whichever mechanic below applies — up to (but never exceeding) their ceiling.

Mechanics

  1. A bidder submits a direct bid amount plus an optional max_bid_amount. If no other bidder is currently active, current_price becomes their direct bid amount (or the listing's starting price, whichever is higher).
  2. When a second bidder places a competing bid (direct or proxy), the Bidding Service compares the two active max ceilings. The bidder with the higher max wins the current round, and current_price is set to one increment above the second-highest max (never their own true max) — the same "second-price-like" mechanic real auction platforms use, so the winning bidder never pays more than the minimum needed to beat the runner-up.
  3. Every automatic increment the system places on a bidder's behalf is still recorded as its own row in the bids ledger (with is_proxy_increment = true) and assigned its own sequence_no — proxy bidding doesn't bypass the strict-ordering/audit requirement, it just originates bids from the system instead of a live click.
  4. If a new bidder's max exceeds the current highest bidder's max, the current highest bidder is automatically outbid, triggering an outbid notification (and, if that bidder has room left under their own max, a further automatic counter-increment).

Interview tip
A bidder's true max_bid_amount is never exposed to any other bidder, the seller, or even shown verbatim in the public bid history — only the resulting current_price is public. If asked "how do you prevent a seller or a colluding account from learning a bidder's true ceiling," the answer is: the max is stored only in the SQL ledger, readable only by the owning bidder and internal services, and every externally visible price is always one increment above the second-highest max, never the true top bid.

Rate Limiting and Abuse Prevention

  • Per-user, per-auction bid-submission rate limits (e.g. no more than N bid attempts per second per user per auction) to blunt bots that hammer the place-bid endpoint in an auction's final seconds trying to win a sniping race purely through submission speed rather than a genuinely higher bid.
  • Bid-sniping bot mitigation is structural, not just rate-limiting: the anti-sniping extension (see the Auto-Close deep dive) already neutralizes most of the incentive to build a submission-speed bot, since a last-instant bid no longer guarantees the win — this is a case where an architectural decision doubles as an abuse defense.
  • Shill bidding detection: an offline/near-real-time analytics job examines the bidder-seller relationship graph (shared payment methods, shared device/IP fingerprints, shared shipping addresses, historical account-linkage signals) combined with bidding-pattern heuristics (an account that repeatedly bids just above the current price on a specific seller's items, then never wins and never bids elsewhere) to flag likely collusion for manual review — this must run asynchronously and never gate real-time bid acceptance.
  • Per-account velocity limits: caps on the number of distinct auctions a single account can actively bid on within a short window, and a cap on total simultaneous proxy-bid (max_bid) commitments outstanding, bounding a single compromised or fraudulent account's blast radius.
  • Step-up verification on suspicious bursts: a CAPTCHA or short auth challenge triggered by an unusual burst of rapid, failed, or rejected bid attempts from one account — a strong signal of automated abuse rather than a real bidder.

Security, Privacy and Permissions

  • Seller and buyer identity verification: account verification (email/phone, and stronger KYC-style checks for high-value listings or sellers with elevated dispute rates) reduces both shill bidding and non-paying-winner risk, without being a hard blocker for casual, low-value listings.
  • Listing-edit permissions: only the owning seller can edit a listing, and only before it has received its first bid — once bidding has started, core terms (starting price, reserve, duration) are locked to prevent a bait-and-switch against bidders who already committed.
  • Bidder identity exposure: the current price and bid count are public (that's the point of a public auction), but individual bidder identities are pseudonymized to other bidders and the seller (e.g. "b***3"); full identity is visible only to platform admins and in dispute-resolution/audit tooling.
  • Payment fraud is explicitly out of scope: this design covers bid acceptance and winner determination only; downstream payment fraud (stolen cards, chargebacks) is owned by a dedicated payments/fraud platform that the Order/Payment Service hands off to — call this boundary out explicitly rather than conflating "auction fairness" with "payment risk."
  • Data-at-rest encryption and immutability: the bid ledger is append-only — no update or delete of a historical bid row, ever — with PII (bidder contact details) encrypted at rest and TLS everywhere in transit.

Load Balancing

Load balancing shows up at several independent layers, each with a different balancing strategy:

  1. Standard L7, health-checked round-robin between clients and the API Gateway for stateless requests (create-listing, get-status, browse/search).
  2. Auction-id-aware routing for place-bid traffic: requests are load-balanced via consistent hashing on auction_id, not plain round-robin, so every bid for a given auction always lands on the same Bidding Service partition — this is a correctness requirement (see the serialized-acceptance deep dive), not just a performance optimization.
  3. Sticky, connection-aware balancing for the WebSocket Gateway — once a watcher's connection is established on a gateway node, it stays there for the connection's lifetime; new connections are balanced across nodes by current connection count / capacity headroom.
  4. Stateless round-robin across catalog/search read replicas, since browsing traffic has no ordering or affinity requirement at all.

Scaling to Multiple Regions

  • Home-region pinning per auction: because bid acceptance must be strictly ordered, all bid traffic for a given auction_id is pinned to a single home region (typically the seller's region) rather than accepted active-active across regions — accepting bids for the same auction in two regions simultaneously would reintroduce exactly the cross-region consensus problem the single-writer partitioning was designed to avoid.
  • Catalog/search runs active-active multi-region with asynchronous cross-region replication — browsing is read-heavy and tolerant of eventual consistency, so there's no correctness reason to centralize it.
  • Watchers connect to their nearest regional WebSocket gateway; the bid-accepted event stream is replicated cross-region (e.g. via a broker mirroring feature) specifically so a watcher outside an auction's home region still gets timely updates, without requiring their connection to be served from the home region itself.
  • A bidder physically far from an auction's home region pays a latency tax on each bid submission — an explicit, accepted trade-off given the strict-consistency requirement; this is a deliberate CAP-theorem choice (consistency over uniform low latency from every region) and should be stated as such if asked.

CAP Theorem Lens

Favor consistency (CP) for the bid-acceptance path and the auction_state row specifically — it is safer to briefly reject or delay a bid (return a retryable error) than to ever accept two different "current highest bidders" for the same auction. Favor availability (AP) for catalog browsing, search, and watcher fan-out — a stale search result or a delayed price update on a listing page is a cosmetic nuisance, never a correctness violation, so those paths should stay up and responsive even during a partial write-side incident elsewhere in the system.

Monitoring, Logging and Analytics

  • Bid-acceptance latency (p50/p99), broken out per auction where possible, with alerting on any regression — a healthy single-writer partition design should show flat, low latency even on the hottest auctions.
  • Close-job reliability / exactly-once-fire monitoring: a dashboard tracking, for every auction, whether its close job fired exactly once — surfacing any auction closed zero times (stuck open past close_time) or more than once (a correctness incident) as a hard-paging alert.
  • Watcher fan-out delivery lag: time from "bid committed in SQL" to "last subscribed watcher's WebSocket push delivered," tracked at p99, with particular attention to the largest (hottest) auctions where fan-out size is greatest.
  • Dispute/audit-trail completeness checks: a periodic job verifying that every auction's sequence_no values are gapless and strictly monotonic in the immutable ledger — any gap or out-of-order sequence is itself a signal of a bug in the single-writer/CAS path and must be investigated immediately, since the entire dispute-resolution promise rests on this invariant holding.
  • Business analytics: distribution of bids per auction, percentage of auctions with a bid inside the anti-snipe window (sniping frequency), proxy-bid adoption rate, close-price-to-starting-price ratio, and the volume of shill-bidding flags raised vs. confirmed after review.

Common Interview Questions

Grouped by theme — expect a real interview to pick several of these as "and then what?" follow-ups on whatever you draw first.

Bid Acceptance and Ordering

How do you fairly resolve two bids submitted within the same millisecond?
Route both bids by consistent hashing of auction_id to the same single-writer Bidding Service partition, which assigns a monotonic sequence_no in true arrival order, then commit via a compare-and-swap against the auction_state row's version column. Whichever bid the single-writer processes first wins; the guarantee is structural (only one worker ever touches that auction), not probabilistic.
Why not just use a distributed lock across all Bidding Service nodes for every auction?
A distributed lock (e.g. via a coordination service) works but adds a network round-trip and a failure mode (lock service outage or lock-expiry races) on every single bid. Consistent-hash partitioning achieves the same single-writer guarantee without any lock acquisition step — it's a routing decision, not a runtime coordination cost, so it's both simpler and faster.
Why is SQL a better fit than NoSQL for the bid-acceptance transaction?
Bid acceptance is fundamentally a conditional compare-and-swap update with a strict, auditable ordering requirement — exactly the guarantee relational transactions, unique constraints, and row versioning are built for. Most NoSQL stores trade away strict, immediate consistency for horizontal scale, which is the wrong trade for a dataset that's small in volume but must be provably correct.

Real-Time Updates

Why must WebSocket fan-out be decoupled from bid acceptance?
Fan-out size varies from zero to roughly a million watchers across different auctions. If the Bidding Service pushed to every watcher synchronously before acknowledging a bid, the busiest (most contested) auctions — exactly the ones where fast, fair acceptance matters most — would have the slowest bid acceptance. Publishing an event and returning immediately keeps the two workloads from ever coupling.
How would you handle one million watchers on a single viral auction?
Shard WebSocket subscriptions for that auction_id across many gateway nodes, and batch/coalesce rapid successive price updates (e.g. at most one push per connection per 250ms) rather than pushing every individual accepted bid — watchers need the current price, not a literal blow-by-blow of a closing-seconds bidding war.

Auction Close and Anti-Sniping

What is sniping, and why does anti-sniping extension mitigate it?
Sniping is bidding at the very last instant of a hard-deadline auction so no other bidder has time to respond. Anti-sniping extension removes the exploit's payoff: any bid arriving inside a short window before close pushes the close time out by that same window, so a last-second bid always leaves room for a counter-bid — it's structurally impossible to snipe-and-instantly-win.
How do you guarantee the close job fires exactly once per auction?
A durable, per-auction timer (not a naive periodic sweep) fires a closing worker that performs the status flip (OPEN/EXTENDED → CLOSED) as a CAS-guarded conditional update, identical in spirit to bid acceptance. A duplicate timer firing (e.g. after a crash-and-retry) simply fails its CAS check because the status is already CLOSED, making the duplicate a safe no-op rather than a double-close.
What are the trade-offs of anti-sniping extension versus a hard deadline?
A hard deadline is perfectly predictable (every auction closes exactly on schedule) but rewards sniping and gives no genuine second chance to respond. Anti-sniping extension is fairer and removes the sniping incentive, at the cost of unpredictable close times on hotly contested items — a trade-off most real auction platforms accept because fairness outweighs schedule predictability for the seller.

Cross-Service Consistency

Why use a Saga instead of a distributed transaction across the bidding, catalog, and order services?
A single distributed transaction (e.g. two-phase commit) spanning multiple services and multiple heterogeneous stores doesn't scale and reintroduces tight coupling between services that should fail and scale independently. A Saga decomposes the flow into a sequence of local transactions with well-defined compensating/retry actions, letting each step succeed, retry, or fail on its own schedule.
What happens if order creation fails after an auction closes?
The already-committed bid ledger and the recorded winner are never rolled back — that outcome is permanent and auditable. Order creation retries with backoff, and if retries are exhausted, the failure is surfaced to operations for manual handling; the compensating action is "retry or alert," never "undo the auction result."

Proxy Bidding

How does proxy/max-bid auto-bidding work mechanically?
A bidder sets a private max_bid_amount. Whenever a competing bid arrives, the system compares the two active maxes and sets current_price to one increment above the second-highest max — never the winner's own true max — so the current highest bidder always pays the minimum needed to stay ahead. Every automatic increment is still recorded as its own row with its own sequence_no.
Why is a bidder's max bid kept private?
Exposing a bidder's true ceiling would let a seller, a colluding shill account, or another bidder game the auction (e.g. bidding just below the known max to extract the maximum price). Keeping it private and only ever surfacing the resulting current_price is what makes the mechanic behave like a fair, sealed-ceiling auction rather than an open-book one.

Data and Partitioning

Why split SQL for bids/orders versus NoSQL for listings, rather than one database for everything?
The two datasets have close to opposite requirements: the bid ledger is small in volume but must be strongly, provably consistent; the catalog is enormous in volume (billions of listings) and can tolerate eventual consistency in exchange for horizontal read scale. Forcing one database to serve both would either make the bid ledger unsafe under concurrency or make the catalog unable to scale.
How do you shard bid traffic without breaking strict ordering?
Shard by auction_id, never by bidder_id or any other key that would split one auction's bids across multiple partitions. Every bid for a given auction always lands on the same shard, so ordering only ever needs to be enforced within a single partition — a solved, single-node problem — never coordinated across shards.

Abuse and Fraud

How do you detect shill bidding?
An asynchronous analytics job cross-references the bidder-seller relationship graph (shared payment methods, device/IP fingerprints, shipping addresses) with bidding-pattern heuristics (an account that repeatedly nudges the price up on one seller's items but never actually wins or bids elsewhere), flagging suspicious pairs for manual review — this must never run synchronously on the bid-acceptance path.
How do you rate-limit bid-sniping bots without blocking legitimate last-second human bidders?
Per-user, per-auction submission-rate limits catch pure speed-based bot abuse without penalizing a human who simply bids late — and because anti-sniping extension already removes the payoff of winning purely by submitting last, the rate limit is a secondary defense rather than the primary mechanism preventing sniping.

Scaling and Operations

How would you scale to multiple regions given the strict-consistency requirement?
Pin each auction's bid-acceptance traffic to a single home region rather than accepting bids for one auction in multiple regions active-active, since that would reintroduce cross-region consensus on every single bid. Catalog browsing and watcher connections can still be fully active-active and geographically distributed, since they don't carry the same consistency requirement.
What's the single biggest bottleneck in this system, and why isn't it aggregate QPS?
A single viral, hotly contested auction — not overall platform traffic — is the practical bottleneck, because that one auction's writes are confined by design to a single serialized partition (a correctness requirement, not a limitation we can shard away). Aggregate system QPS across millions of independent auctions is comparatively easy to scale horizontally; contention concentrated on one row is not.

Key Takeaways

Remember
Strict, auditable ordering of bids on a single auction is the spine of this design, not a detail: single-writer-per-auction-id partitioning plus a compare-and-swap on auction_state is what makes "who's the current highest bidder" a provably correct fact rather than a race-dependent guess — state that explicitly, since it's the one requirement in this problem that can't be relaxed to "eventually consistent." Real-time fan-out and cross-service updates (catalog price, order creation) must be decoupled from bid acceptance via an event broker and a Saga, because coupling them would let a million-watcher fan-out or a slow downstream service degrade the one path that must never slow down. The SQL-versus-NoSQL split isn't indecision — it's matching two datasets with genuinely opposite consistency/scale requirements to two different stores. And the anti-sniping extension is the one piece of "clever" domain logic worth knowing cold: it turns a fairness problem into a simple, provable rule (a late bid always earns a fresh response window) rather than a policy exception bolted on after the fact.
