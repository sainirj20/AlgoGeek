SYSTEM DESIGN

Designing an On-Call Escalation System

A complete, interview-ready design walkthrough for a PagerDuty/Opsgenie-style incident alerting and escalation platform: requirements, capacity estimation, API & data model, the escalation state machine, deduplication, schedule resolution, notification failover, durable timers, multi-region fault tolerance, and follow-up interview questions.

Problem Statement

Design an on-call escalation system like PagerDuty, Opsgenie, or VictorOps. Monitoring tools (Prometheus, Datadog, CloudWatch, custom health checks) fire alerts into the system via webhook or API. The system must correlate related alerts into a single incident, figure out which team owns it and who is currently on-call for that team, notify that person across multiple channels, and — if nobody acknowledges in time — escalate to the next person or level, repeating until someone acknowledges or resolves the incident. It must also manage on-call schedules (rotations, overrides, time-off swaps), suppress alerts during planned maintenance windows, and keep a durable, auditable timeline of everything that happened.

Why does this problem matter in an interview?

It is one of the few "classic" system design questions where the non-functional requirements are more interesting than the functional ones. Every other system on this site can tolerate a brief outage with degraded service; this one cannot — if the on-call system itself goes down, the very outages it exists to surface go unnoticed. That single constraint (this system must be more reliable than everything it monitors) cascades into almost every design decision: multi-region active-active instead of active-passive, multiple independent third-party providers per notification channel, durable server-side timers instead of in-process ones, and a "who pages the pager" fallback mechanism. A strong candidate treats availability and durability as first-class design constraints from minute one, not an afterthought bolted on at the end.

Why do organizations use a system like this?

  • Modern services emit thousands of independent monitoring signals; a human cannot watch dashboards 24/7, so software has to decide who gets woken up and when.
  • A single root cause (e.g. a database running out of connections) can trip dozens of downstream monitors simultaneously — without correlation, the on-call engineer would be paged 30 times for one problem.
  • Teams rotate on-call responsibility across people and time zones; someone has to compute, at any given second, exactly one truthful answer to "who is on call right now."
  • Regulated industries (finance, healthcare, payments) need an immutable audit trail of who was notified, when, and who acknowledged — both for postmortems and for compliance.

Clarifying Questions to Ask the Interviewer

As with any system design interview, spend the first few minutes narrowing scope before drawing boxes.

  1. What scale are we targeting — how many customer organizations, and roughly how many monitored services/alerts per organization?
  2. Do we need to support arbitrary monitoring-tool integrations (Prometheus, Datadog, CloudWatch, Nagios, custom scripts), or a fixed small set?
  3. What escalation policy shapes must we support — a simple linear chain of people, or branching policies (e.g. notify a whole team in parallel at the final level)?
  4. Do we need on-call schedule management (rotations, layers, overrides) as part of this system, or is "who is on-call" supplied by an external system?
  5. What channels must we support for notification — push, SMS, voice call, email, chat-ops (Slack/Teams)?
  6. What is an acceptable end-to-end latency from "alert received" to "first human notified"? Seconds? Tens of seconds?
  7. Do we need maintenance windows / alert suppression for planned work?
  8. Is multi-region / disaster-recovery an explicit requirement, given the nature of the system?
  9. Do we need analytics (MTTA/MTTR dashboards) as part of the core design, or is that out of scope?
  10. Single-tenant internal tool, or multi-tenant SaaS serving many independent customer organizations?
Interview tip
State reasonable defaults out loud and move on: "I'll assume a multi-tenant SaaS product, ~10,000 enterprise customers, a linear escalation policy with up to 5 levels, push/SMS/voice/email as channels, and a hard target of notifying a human within 30 seconds of alert ingestion — I'll flag anywhere that assumption materially changes the design." This shows judgment under time pressure rather than indecision.

Requirements and Goals

Functional Requirements

  1. Ingest alerts from external monitoring systems via webhook/API, normalizing many source formats (Prometheus Alertmanager, Datadog, CloudWatch/SNS, generic custom payloads) into one internal schema.
  2. Route an incoming alert to the correct team and escalation policy, and resolve that policy's target(s) — which may be a specific user or the currently-on-call user(s) of one or more schedules.
  3. Notify the current responder across multiple channels (push, SMS, voice call, email) with escalating urgency.
  4. If unacknowledged within a configurable timeout, escalate to the next person or level, repeating per the policy until acknowledged, resolved, or the policy is exhausted.
  5. Allow a responder to acknowledge, resolve, or snooze (temporarily silence) an incident from any channel.
  6. Manage on-call schedules: rotations (daily/weekly), layers (e.g. primary + secondary), one-off overrides (vacation swaps), and computing "who is on-call right now."
  7. Deduplicate and group related alerts sharing a root cause into a single incident, so responders are not paged repeatedly for the same underlying problem.
  8. Support maintenance windows that suppress notification for alerts matching a declared scope and time range, without silently discarding unrelated incidents.
  9. Maintain a durable, append-only incident timeline / audit log (created, acknowledged, escalated, resolved, notification attempts and outcomes).
  10. (Extended) Provide MTTA/MTTR and escalation-rate analytics per team.

Non-Functional Requirements

  1. Extremely high availability: this system is the alerting safety net for every other system its customers run — it must be more reliable than the systems it monitors, not merely "highly available" in the generic sense.
  2. Very low end-to-end latency: alert-ingested-to-human-notified must be measured in seconds, not minutes — every additional second is time an outage goes unaddressed.
  3. Durability: an ingested alert must never be silently dropped, even under a traffic storm or partial infrastructure failure.
  4. Resilience to widespread infrastructure outages: the system must keep working — and keep reaching humans — even during a major cloud-provider or telecom outage, which argues for multi-region and multi-provider redundancy on the notification path specifically.
  5. Idempotency: a monitoring tool retrying its webhook (because it didn't see our 200 OK) must never create a duplicate incident or fire a duplicate escalation.
  6. Horizontal scalability: ingestion, dedup, routing, and notification fan-out must each scale independently, since a single customer's alert storm should not degrade another customer's routing or notification latency.
  7. Auditability: every state transition and every notification attempt must be durably logged for postmortems and compliance.

Extended / Out-of-Scope

We go deep on ingestion, deduplication, routing, the escalation state machine, notification dispatch/failover, durable timers, and multi-region fault tolerance — the parts of this system that are genuinely hard and differentiate a strong answer. We call out but don't fully design a general-purpose status-page product, a full incident postmortem/retrospective workflow tool, or a runbook-authoring UI, since those are large adjacent products in their own right.

Capacity Estimation and Constraints

This system has an unusual capacity profile compared to most of the other systems on this site: steady-state load is fairly modest, but the system must be provisioned for an "alert storm" burst that can be one to two orders of magnitude above steady state — and that burst is precisely the moment the system's correctness matters most. We size both cases explicitly and never provision only for the average.

Steady-State Traffic

Assumption 10,000 enterprise customer organizations, averaging 50 raw monitoring alerts/customer/day
Total raw alerts/day = 10,000 × 50 = 500,000
Raw ingestion rate (avg) = 500,000 ÷ 86,400 ≈ 5.8/sec → round to ~6 QPS average
Alert volume is bursty across the day (deploys, business hours), so provision baseline ingestion capacity for ~5× average ≈ 30 QPS, well below the storm scenario below
Deduplication Assume a steady-state dedup ratio of 8:1 (a single root cause commonly trips several correlated monitors at once)
Incidents/day = 500,000 ÷ 8 ≈ 62,500
Incidents/sec (avg) = 62,500 ÷ 86,400 ≈ 0.72/sec
Notification amplification Each incident's escalation policy may need to reach 1–5 people across 2–3 channels each = an amplification factor of roughly 2×–15× per incident
Steady-state average: ~1.8 people notified/incident (most incidents ack at level 1) × ~1.5 channels attempted/person (many ack on push before SMS fires) ≈ 2.7× → round to ~3 notification-provider calls/incident
Notification sends/day = 62,500 × 3 ≈ 187,500
Notification QPS (avg) = 187,500 ÷ 86,400 ≈ 2.2/sec

Alert-Storm Burst (Major Cloud-Provider Outage)

The scenario that actually stresses this system: a major cloud region degrades, and every customer with infrastructure in that region alerts nearly simultaneously.

Assumption 15% of the 10,000 customers (1,500 orgs) have workloads in the affected region; each fires ~150 raw alerts within a 5-minute (300-second) window as multiple services cascade (DB timeouts, queue backlogs, HTTP 5xxs, failed health checks)
Raw alerts in window = 1,500 × 150 = 225,000
Peak ingestion QPS = 225,000 ÷ 300 = 750 QPS — ≈125× the steady-state average (~6 QPS), ≈25× the padded steady-state design ceiling (~30 QPS)
Dedup and fan-out under storm Correlated storms dedup harder (shared root cause, tight time window) — assume a 25:1 ratio → incidents in window = 225,000 ÷ 25 = 9,000; incident creation rate = 9,000 ÷ 300 = 30/sec
Fewer quick acks under stress (responders are overwhelmed) → assume ~3 people notified/incident × ~2 channels/person ≈ 6× amplification
Notification calls in window = 9,000 × 6 = 54,000 → peak notification QPS = 54,000 ÷ 300 = 180 QPS

Interview tip
The single most important capacity insight here: this system must be provisioned and load-tested for the storm column, not the average column — under-provisioning for the 125× burst is precisely equivalent to the system failing at the one moment it exists to help. Say this out loud; it's the same instinct that drives the multi-region/multi-provider design later in the interview.

Storage Estimates

Alerts table Raw alert record (normalized fields + source payload) ≈ 2 KB average
Alerts/year ≈ 500,000 × 365 ≈ 182.5 million (steady-state; storms add a small fraction on top)
Raw storage/year ≈ 182.5M × 2 KB ≈ 365 GB; with 3× replication ≈ 1.1 TB/year
5-year audit/compliance retention ≈ 5.5 TB (alerts table alone, replicated)
Incidents and notification_log Incidents/year ≈ 62,500 × 365 ≈ 22.8 million, ≈1 KB/row ≈ 22.8 GB/year raw, ≈68 GB/year replicated
Notification log rows/year ≈ 187,500 × 365 ≈ 68.4 million, ≈500 bytes/row (actor, channel, provider, outcome, latency, timestamp) ≈ 34 GB/year raw, ≈~100 GB/year replicated
Total growth across the three core tables ≈ ~1.3 TB/year replicated — modest; the real engineering challenge here is latency and correctness under burst, not raw volume

Bandwidth Estimates

Bandwidth Ingestion inbound (steady): ~30 QPS × 2 KB ≈ 60 KB/sec — (storm peak): ~750 QPS × 2 KB ≈ 1.5 MB/sec
Notification dispatch outbound to providers (steady): ~2.2/sec × 1 KB ≈ 2.2 KB/sec — (storm peak): ~180/sec × 1 KB ≈ 180 KB/sec

Bandwidth is trivial at this scale in absolute terms — this system is not throughput-bound. The bottlenecks that actually matter are notification fan-out latency under burst, the correctness of the escalation-timer mechanism, and the reachability of third-party channel providers, which is why the rest of this design concentrates there.

System / API Design

A REST API over HTTPS is the natural fit: webhook ingestion maps directly onto POST semantics, and every downstream action (acknowledge, resolve, snooze) is a simple state-changing call against an incident resource.

Interview tip
Notice the ingestion endpoint returns 202 Accepted, not 200/201 with the full incident state. The ingestion path only needs to durably record the alert and hand back an identifier fast — dedup, routing, and escalation kick-off can (and should) continue asynchronously so the monitoring tool's webhook call is never held open waiting on notification fan-out.

Data Model and Database Choice

Nature of the Data

  • Two very different access patterns coexist: a high-volume, append-mostly write path (alerts, notification_log) and a small, latency-critical, strongly-consistent read/write path (the single incident row being escalated right now).
  • Incident and escalation-timer state must never be silently lost — this is the one place in the whole system where a torn write or a lost update is a genuine correctness bug, not a performance blip.
  • On-call schedules and overrides are a comparatively tiny dataset (thousands of schedules, not billions of rows) but are read on nearly every alert, so they benefit heavily from caching.

SQL vs. NoSQL — a Hybrid, Not a Single Choice

The incidents row being actively escalated is a small, low-volume, coordination-heavy record that must support atomic conditional updates (the escalation-claim mechanism below) — a strong-consistency relational database (Postgres/MySQL, or a distributed SQL store like CockroachDB/Spanner for multi-region) is the right fit. The high-volume, append-mostly alerts and notification_log tables are simple, wide, mostly-write, rarely-updated records with no complex joins on the hot path — a natural fit for a horizontally-scalable wide-column/NoSQL store (Cassandra, DynamoDB, Bigtable) or a well-sharded relational store, depending on operational preference. This "SQL for the coordination-heavy control plane, NoSQL (or sharded SQL) for the bulk append-only data plane" split mirrors the same reasoning used for the URL Shortener's key-generation service, applied here to the escalation-timer claim instead of key issuance.

Interview tip
If pushed toward an all-NoSQL answer for uniformity: that's workable for alerts/notification_log, but be explicit that the incidents table's conditional-update claim mechanism needs either genuine ACID transactions or a data store with strong compare-and-swap semantics (e.g. DynamoDB conditional writes) — "eventually consistent" is not an acceptable model for "did exactly one worker claim this escalation."

High-Level Design

Monitoring tools call the Alert Ingestion API, which authenticates the integration and normalizes the payload. The Dedup/Grouping Service fingerprints the alert and either folds it into an existing open incident or creates a new one. The Routing Engine resolves which team owns the incident and which escalation policy and on-call schedule applies, consulting the On-Call Schedules service (rotations + overrides). The Escalation Engine is the core state machine: it notifies the current target via the Notification Dispatch service, starts a durable timer, and — if unacknowledged — advances to the next level. Notification Dispatch fans out across push/SMS/voice/email, each through a primary and backup third-party provider. All state changes land durably in the Incident Store, which a fleet of Scheduler Workers polls to fire due escalations, and which feeds an Analytics pipeline for MTTA/MTTR dashboards.

Figure 1. High-level topology — ingestion and dedup feed routing and escalation; the durable Incident Store sits underneath both the Escalation Engine and the Scheduler Worker Fleet; Notification Dispatch fans out across three independently-failing channel families.

Key Design Principles Reflected in the Diagram

  • Nothing waits on notification fan-out: ingestion returns as soon as the alert is durably recorded; dedup, routing, and escalation continue asynchronously, and notification attempts happen off the ingestion request's critical path.
  • The Incident Store is the single source of truth for escalation state — not application-server memory — which is what makes the Scheduler Worker Fleet able to resume cleanly after any individual worker (or the whole fleet) restarts.
  • Every notification channel has an independent backup provider baked into the topology, not bolted on later — reaching a human is the one job this system cannot fail at.
  • Analytics is fully decoupled from the hot escalation path, so a slow or backlogged analytics pipeline never adds latency to notifying a human.

Deep Dive — The Escalation State Machine

Every incident's lifecycle is governed by a small, explicit state machine. An incident starts TRIGGERED, immediately enters ESCALATING at level 1 as the first notification goes out, and either gets ACKNOWLEDGED by a human (which cancels the pending escalation timer) or, if the per-level timeout elapses with no acknowledgment, advances to the next level and repeats. From ACKNOWLEDGED, a responder eventually marks the incident RESOLVED; alternatively, if the underlying monitoring tool itself sends a resolve event (the source alert cleared on its own), the incident auto-resolves directly from any state.

Figure 2. Escalation state machine — each level has its own ack timeout; an ack at any level jumps straight to ACKNOWLEDGED and cancels the pending timer; the source system clearing the alert auto-resolves directly.

Concretely, the transition table an implementation enforces is small and total (every state + event pair has exactly one defined next state):

Deep Dive — Alert Deduplication and Grouping

Without deduplication, one root cause (say, a shared database losing connectivity) can trip dozens of independent monitors — latency, error rate, queue depth, health checks — each of which would otherwise open its own incident and page the on-call engineer separately. The fix is a fingerprinting + correlation-window algorithm.

  1. Fingerprint computation: fingerprint = hash(integration_id + normalized_key_fields), where the key fields are whichever attributes the source system considers identity-defining (commonly host + check/metric name + service), or simply the source-supplied dedup_key when the integration provides one. URL/label ordering is normalized first so semantically-identical alerts always hash identically.
  2. Open-incident lookup: look up the fingerprint against an index of currently-open incidents (status not RESOLVED) created within a configurable correlation window (e.g. the last 4 hours, or since the incident opened, whichever is shorter).
  3. Match found: append the new alert to that incident's alert list, increment alert_count, and suppress a new notification — the incident is already being escalated.
  4. No match: create a new incident with this fingerprint and kick off routing + escalation.
Figure 3. Deduplication flow — a shared fingerprint within the correlation window folds many raw alerts into one incident instead of one escalation per alert.
Beyond exact-match fingerprints
Exact fingerprint matching handles the common case (the same check firing repeatedly). Some production systems layer a second, fuzzier correlation pass on top — grouping alerts that share a team and an overlapping time window even with different fingerprints (e.g. CPU and memory alerts on the same host within 60 seconds of each other) using content-similarity heuristics or a lightweight ML classifier. That's a valuable extension to mention, but the exact-fingerprint + time-window algorithm above is the load-bearing mechanism and the one to implement first.

Deep Dive — On-Call Schedule Resolution

"Who is on-call right now?" is a small but easy-to-get-wrong interval-overlap problem. A schedule is built from one or more layers (e.g. a primary rotation and a separate secondary/escalation rotation), each layer defined by an ordered list of users, a rotation_start timestamp, and a rotation_length_sec (e.g. one week). On top of the base rotation, overrides let a specific user substitute for whoever the rotation would otherwise pick, for an explicit time range (a vacation swap, a sick day).

Base rotation resolution — O(1) index = floor((now − rotation_start) ÷ rotation_length_sec) mod length(users)
on_call_user = users[index]

Overrides are resolved by an indexed range query — WHERE schedule_id = ? AND start_at ≤ now AND end_at > now, backed by an index on (schedule_id, start_at, end_at) — against a table that is small relative to alert volume (organizations create far fewer overrides than alerts). An override, when present for the current instant, always wins over the base rotation. The final algorithm: for each layer (highest priority first), check for an active override; if none, fall back to the O(1) rotation arithmetic; the result across all layers is "who is on-call right now."

Figure 4. Overrides are checked before falling back to the base rotation, so a short vacation swap for Dana correctly wins over Bob's underlying weekly assignment.

Deep Dive — Notification Dispatch and Channel Failover

Person-level escalation (advancing from responder A to responder B) is the Escalation Engine's job; nested inside each person-level attempt is a separate channel-level escalation, handled by Notification Dispatch: try the fastest, least-intrusive channel first, and escalate through progressively more insistent channels if there's no acknowledgment, with an independent backup provider for every channel in case the primary provider itself fails.

  1. Attempt push via the mobile push provider (FCM/APNs/web push). Wait a short timeout (e.g. 60–90 seconds).
  2. No ack → attempt SMS via the primary SMS provider (e.g. Twilio). If the provider itself errors or times out (not just "no ack" — an actual send failure), immediately fail over to a backup SMS provider (e.g. a second, carrier-diverse vendor) rather than waiting out the ack timeout on a request that never delivered.
  3. No ack on SMS → attempt a voice call via an automated dialer, with the same primary/backup provider failover pattern.
  4. No ack after exhausting channels → control returns to the Escalation Engine, which advances to the next person/level per the policy.
Figure 5. Channel escalation nested inside person escalation — solid arrows are ack-timeout progressions between channels; dashed arrows are provider failover triggered by an actual send failure, not just silence.

Note the important distinction encoded in the diagram: a timeout with no acknowledgment (solid arrows) means the responder saw or could have seen the notification and didn't respond — escalate to a more insistent channel. A provider error (dashed arrows) means the message may never have been delivered at all — failover to a backup provider on the same channel immediately, without waiting out the full ack timeout.

Deep Dive — Durable Escalation Timers and the Scheduler Fleet

The escalation timer is the single most safety-critical mechanism in the whole system, and it must never be an in-process setTimeout/in-memory timer — a server restart, deploy, or crash would silently lose every pending escalation. Instead, the "when does this incident need to escalate next" fact is stored directly on the durable incident row as next_escalation_at and escalation_level. A stateless fleet of Scheduler Workers continuously polls (or subscribes to a change feed on) an index of incidents where status = 'ESCALATING' AND next_escalation_at ≤ now().

Because many workers poll the same due-escalations index concurrently, exactly one of them must win the right to process any given incident — otherwise two workers could both notify the next level for the same incident. This is solved with an atomic conditional update (a compare-and-swap at the database layer):

UPDATE incidents
SET claimed_by = :worker_id, claim_expires_at = now() + interval '30 seconds'
WHERE incident_id = :id
  AND status = 'ESCALATING'
  AND next_escalation_at <= now()
  AND (claimed_by IS NULL OR claim_expires_at < now());
-- exactly one worker's UPDATE affects 1 row; every other worker's identical
-- UPDATE affects 0 rows for that incident_id and moves on to the next candidate

The winning worker then executes the escalation (invokes Notification Dispatch for the next level's target), advances escalation_level, recomputes next_escalation_at from that level's ack_timeout_sec, and clears claimed_by. The short claim lease (claim_expires_at) protects against a worker crashing mid-processing: after the lease expires, the row becomes claimable again, so no incident can get permanently stuck behind a dead worker.

Figure 6. Three scheduler workers race to claim the same due escalation; the database's atomic conditional update guarantees exactly one wins, so restarts or a growing worker fleet can never double-escalate an incident.

An alternative worth naming in an interview: instead of a polled "due escalations" index, use a durable delayed-delivery queue (an SQS delay queue, a Kafka topic paired with a per-partition delay, or a Redis sorted set keyed by due-timestamp acting as a timer wheel, popped via ZRANGEBYSCORE + an atomic ZREM). These give push-based wakeups instead of poll-interval latency, at the cost of an extra moving piece; the claim semantics (exactly one consumer processes a given due item) are conceptually identical either way. The DB-polling approach above is simpler to reason about and to make durable, and is a perfectly good default answer.

Data Partitioning and Sharding

The natural multi-tenant boundary is team_id/organization_id: shard alerts, incidents, and notification_log by (a hash of) this key. This keeps one customer's alert storm from ever affecting another customer's routing or escalation latency — a noisy tenant's shard gets hot, but every other tenant's shard is untouched.

  • Alerts and notification_log are the highest-volume tables; hash-partition by organization_id with consistent hashing so adding shard capacity only reshuffles a small, bounded fraction of tenants.
  • Incidents shard the same way, which is what makes the escalation-timer claim mechanism scale: the "due escalations" index is naturally partitioned per shard, and a fleet of scheduler workers can be assigned (or dynamically balanced) across shards so one organization's storm doesn't starve another organization's due-escalation polling.
  • On-call schedules and overrides are small enough per organization to be cached in full alongside the shard that owns them, keeping schedule resolution effectively a cache hit on the hot path.
  • A single very large enterprise customer can still become a "hot shard" on its own; the standard mitigation is to further split that one tenant's data across multiple sub-shards by team_id within the organization, rather than resharding everyone.

The reason the escalation-timer state specifically needs a reliable distributed scheduler rather than any in-process timer deserves restating here in partitioning terms: a service restart, a rolling deploy, or an autoscaling event must never lose a pending escalation. Because next_escalation_at lives in the sharded, replicated incidents table (not in any one process's memory), any scheduler worker, on any node, in any region reachable to that shard, can pick up the polling work at any time — the durable "next escalation due" index is what makes horizontal scaling of the scheduler fleet safe.

Replication, Multi-Region Fault Tolerance, and the Dead Man's Switch

This is the section to get right
Every other design on this site treats multi-region replication as a scaling and latency optimization. Here it is a correctness requirement: the system that pages you about an outage cannot itself be a casualty of the same outage. If this system and the infrastructure it monitors share a single cloud region, a single cloud provider, or a single notification vendor, that shared dependency is a design flaw, not a footnote.

Multi-Region Active-Active

Run the application tier and the incident data store active-active across at least two, ideally geographically distant, regions. Incident and escalation-timer state — the one place a lost or forked write is catastrophic — uses synchronous or near-synchronous cross-region replication (a Raft/Paxos-based distributed SQL store such as CockroachDB or Spanner-style replication, or synchronous replication to a designated hot-standby region) even though this costs extra write latency (tens to low hundreds of milliseconds). That trade is worth it: losing an incident's escalation state during a region failure is a far worse outcome than a slower write. Everything else — analytics, notification logs, schedule display caches — can be eventually consistent and asynchronously replicated.

No Single Point of Failure in Actually Reaching a Human

This is the part of the design that's easy to get catastrophically wrong: it's not enough for the software to decide correctly who to notify if the notification never physically reaches a phone. Every channel needs at least two independent, non-correlated providers:

SMSTwilioA second, carrier-diverse SMS aggregator (e.g. Bandwidth, Vonage)

Providers are health-checked continuously; a circuit breaker trips to the backup on an elevated error rate or timeout — not only on an outright API failure — so a degrading-but-not-fully-down provider doesn't quietly swallow notifications for minutes before anyone notices. It's also worth deliberately hosting the platform itself across infrastructure not perfectly correlated with the biggest and most common outage causes its customers will page about (e.g., not depending entirely on the exact same cloud region and vendor that a large fraction of the customer base itself runs on).

The Dead Man's Switch

The final layer of defense addresses the question "what if this whole system is down?" The escalation platform emits a periodic heartbeat to a small, independently-hosted watchdog service — ideally on entirely different infrastructure and a different vendor account, so a failure mode that takes down the main platform is unlikely to take down the watchdog too. If the heartbeat goes missing for longer than a defined threshold, the watchdog itself triggers a minimal, dependency-light "break-glass" page: a direct SMS or phone call, through a completely separate provider/account, to a small fixed list of senior engineers. This mirrors the "who pages the pager" instinct behind real production safety nets — the fallback path is deliberately dumb and has as few dependencies as possible, because its entire job is to still work when the sophisticated system has failed.

Security

  • Webhook authentication: every integration is issued its own secret, used either as a bearer token or (preferably) to HMAC-sign the raw webhook payload; the ingestion API verifies the signature before accepting the request and returns 401 otherwise. Scoping secrets per-integration rather than per-organization means a single leaked credential only exposes that one integration's ability to create alerts.
  • Tenant isolation: every query is scoped by organization_id/team_id at the application layer, reinforced by partition-level isolation from the sharding scheme above; a bug in one tenant's escalation policy cannot leak another tenant's incident data or on-call roster.
  • Audit trail for compliance: the incident timeline and notification_log are append-only and immutable — who acknowledged what, when, from which channel, and every notification attempt's outcome is durably recorded, which matters both for blameless postmortems and for regulated-industry compliance requirements.
  • Authorization on responder actions: acknowledge/resolve/snooze actions are scoped to users who belong to the owning team (or an admin), and the actor identity is recorded on every action, not just accepted at face value from the request.
  • Rate limiting per integration: doubles as both an abuse-prevention control and a noisy-neighbor control (see the Bottlenecks section) — a compromised or misconfigured integration credential can't be used to flood shared infrastructure.

Load Balancing

Load balancing sits at three points: between monitoring tools and the Alert Ingestion API, between application services and the incident data store, and between the Escalation Engine/Notification Dispatch and the third-party provider APIs.

  1. Start with health-checked round-robin at the ingestion edge — simple, and automatically drains traffic from an unhealthy instance.
  2. Ingestion instances should scale independently of escalation/dispatch instances, since an alert storm's ingestion burst and its downstream notification fan-out burst have different shapes and different scaling knobs (see the capacity table above — ingestion peaks at ~125× average while notification calls peak at ~80×).
  3. Toward third-party providers, use per-provider connection pools with independent concurrency limits, so one provider throttling under a storm doesn't starve requests destined for a different, healthy provider.

Scaling to Multiple Regions

  • Pin each organization to a home region for normal writes (lowest latency, simplest reasoning about the escalation-timer claim mechanism), but replicate incident/escalation state synchronously enough to a secondary region that a home-region outage can fail over without losing in-flight escalations.
  • Route ingestion via geo-aware DNS/anycast to the nearest healthy region; a health-checked failover redirects an organization's traffic to its secondary region automatically if the home region becomes unreachable.
  • Scheduler worker fleets run per-region against that region's shard of the incidents table, with cross-region failover of the polling responsibility itself if a region's worker fleet goes dark — the durable "next escalation due" state is what makes this handoff safe.
  • Analytics pipelines can run per-region and roll up into a global analytics store on a slower, asynchronous cadence — there's no correctness reason MTTA/MTTR aggregation needs to be synchronous across regions.
  • Notification Dispatch should be able to reach every provider from every region, so a region failover doesn't also have to fail over notification connectivity as a separate step.

Monitoring, Logging, and Analytics

Because this system's job is to surface problems in other systems, its own observability has to be unusually rigorous — a slow degradation here is exactly the kind of failure it's designed to catch elsewhere.

  • MTTA (mean time to acknowledge) and MTTR (mean time to resolve) per team, computed by writing the deltas (acknowledged_at − created_at, resolved_at − acknowledged_at) at transition time and rolling them up into per-team, per-day materialized aggregates rather than recomputing from raw timestamps on every dashboard load.
  • Escalation rate — the fraction of incidents that go unacknowledged past level 1 — is a direct signal of on-call fatigue or under-staffing; a team whose escalation rate creeps upward over weeks is a strong leading indicator worth alerting on in its own right.
  • Notification delivery success rate per channel and per provider, tracked continuously, is what lets a circuit breaker (and a human) notice a degrading SMS or voice provider before it fully fails.
  • Dedup effectiveness — the ratio of raw alerts to incidents created, per integration — flags a noisy or misconfigured monitor before it erodes on-call trust in the whole system ("alert fatigue").
  • The core latency SLO: p50/p95/p99 of alert-ingested-to-first-notification-sent, targeting single-digit-to-low-tens of seconds at p99, not minutes — this is the number that should headline any dashboard for this system.
  • A continuous synthetic canary — a small, harmless test alert sent on a fixed cadence all the way through ingestion, dedup, routing, escalation, and delivery to a real test device — is the closest thing to an always-on, production, end-to-end correctness check for a system whose entire value is "did a human actually get paged."

CAP Theorem Lens

Favor strong consistency (C) for the incidents table's escalation-timer state specifically — the system must never fork or lose the "what happens next" fact for an actively-escalating incident, even at the cost of extra cross-region write latency during a partition. Favor availability and partition tolerance (AP) almost everywhere else: a slightly stale on-call-schedule cache, a delayed analytics rollup, or a notification log that catches up a few seconds late are all vastly preferable to blocking the one thing that matters — getting a human notified.

Common Interview Questions

Interviewers love to pressure-test one mechanism at a time. These are grouped by theme — use them to rehearse, or expect a real interview to pick a handful as "and then what?" follow-ups.

Scaling and Performance

A burst of 50,000 alerts arrives in one minute because a monitoring tool's own bug goes haywire — walk through what happens end to end.
A per-integration token-bucket at the API gateway caps that integration's rate at a documented ceiling well above normal volume but below a level that endangers shared infrastructure; requests over the burst allowance are queued briefly or answered with 429 and Retry-After rather than silently dropped. Genuine cross-customer storm traffic still needs to get through, so rate-limit counters are partitioned by integration_id in a fast, co-located store (e.g. Redis) rather than a single shared global limiter becoming the bottleneck.
How do you keep p99 ingestion-to-first-notification latency low when notification providers themselves start throttling you during a storm?
Queue-and-drain instead of block: ingestion durably records the alert and returns immediately; Notification Dispatch consumes from a durable queue with provider-aware concurrency limits, backing off per-provider (not globally) on 429/5xx, and failing over to a backup provider for that channel rather than stalling the entire queue behind one degraded provider.
How do you compute MTTA and MTTR at scale across millions of incidents without expensive full-table scans?
Write the deltas at transition time — acknowledged_at minus created_at, resolved_at minus acknowledged_at — directly onto the incident row (or a narrow per-incident metrics fact table) the moment each transition happens, then pre-aggregate into per-team, per-day rollup tables, instead of recomputing from raw timestamps across the whole incidents table on every dashboard load.

Correctness and Data

How do you guarantee an escalation timer fires even if the service restarts mid-wait?
Never hold the timer only in process memory. Persist next_escalation_at and escalation_level directly on the durable incident row; a stateless fleet of scheduler workers polls for rows past due. A restart just restarts the polling loop — no in-flight timer state is lost because none ever lived only in memory.
How do you prevent two scheduler workers from double-escalating the same incident?
An atomic conditional UPDATE: claim the row only if it's still due and unclaimed (or its previous claim lease has expired). The database's row-level compare-and-swap guarantees exactly one worker's UPDATE affects a row; every other worker's identical UPDATE affects zero rows. A short claim lease protects against a worker crashing mid-processing.
How do you deduplicate 500 alerts from the same root cause into a single incident?
Compute a fingerprint per alert (a hash of the integration/source plus the normalized key fields the source treats as identity-defining, or a client-supplied dedup_key). Look up an open incident sharing that fingerprint within a correlation window; if found, append and suppress a new notification; if not, open a new incident and escalate.
A monitoring tool retries its webhook because our 200 OK was lost in transit — how do you avoid a duplicate incident or duplicate escalation?
Fingerprint/dedup_key matching already absorbs most of this, since a retried alert with the same key resolves to the same open incident. Layer a short-TTL idempotency cache keyed on an integration-supplied idempotency key (or a hash of the raw payload) in front of that so a byte-identical retry within a few minutes is answered from the original response without even re-running dedup logic.
How do you resolve who is on-call right now given rotations plus overrides, without an expensive query on every alert?
The base rotation resolves with O(1) arithmetic (elapsed time since rotation_start, integer-divided by rotation_length, modulo the user-list length). Overrides are a small, separately-indexed table queried by a cheap (schedule_id, start_at, end_at) range check and always take precedence when active. Cache the resolved on-call user per schedule with a short TTL, invalidated on schedule/override writes.
What consistency model do you use for the incident record across regions, and why?
Strong/synchronous-enough consistency specifically for incident and escalation-timer state, since losing or forking it is a correctness catastrophe, even at the cost of extra cross-region write latency. Nearly everything else — analytics, notification logs, schedule display caches — can be eventually consistent.

Product and API Design

How do you design schedule overrides so a one-off vacation swap doesn't corrupt the underlying rotation definition?
Overrides live in a separate table from the rotation definition and are purely additive/time-boxed rather than mutating schedule_layers. Resolution always checks overrides first and falls back to the base rotation, so an expired override cleanly disappears with no reconciliation step.
How do you support maintenance windows so planned work doesn't page anyone, but a genuinely new, unrelated incident during that window isn't silently swallowed?
Scope the window to a specific team/service/integration and time range, and only suppress notification for alerts matching that scope — the incident is still created and logged for later review, just not escalated while the window is active. Anything outside the declared scope still pages normally, and the window has a hard expiry so it can't be forgotten.
A responder acknowledges by replying to the SMS instead of tapping the push notification — how do you make that consistently update incident state everywhere?
Every channel with a reply path (SMS reply, phone-call keypress, email reply) routes back through the same AcknowledgeIncident action, not a channel-specific side path. As soon as one channel's ack lands, the incident transitions to ACKNOWLEDGED and the escalation timer cancels; any other channel's not-yet-answered notification is marked stale so the responder doesn't get a redundant call minutes later.
Should the per-level escalation timeout be a fixed system default or configurable per team?
Configurable per policy (and often per level) — a payments-critical service may want a 2-minute level-1 timeout while a lower-severity internal tool might tolerate 15 minutes. Keep a platform-wide floor (so a misconfigured 5-second timeout can't page the whole org for noise) and a ceiling (so nothing sits un-escalated for hours by mistake).

Security and Abuse

How do you stop anyone on the internet from injecting fake incidents into a customer's account?
Every integration gets its own secret used to HMAC-sign the webhook payload; the ingestion API verifies the signature before accepting the request and rejects with 401 otherwise. Scoping the secret per-integration, not per-organization, means a single leaked credential only exposes that one integration.
How do you rate-limit a noisy or misconfigured integration without dropping genuine alerts from a real incident?
Rate-limit per integration_id, not globally, so one customer's misconfigured monitor can't consume capacity meant for everyone else. Prefer shedding/queuing over hard-dropping beyond a generous ceiling, so the integration owner sees 429s and delayed delivery rather than the system silently discarding data, and alert the integration owner when their own integration is being throttled.

Reliability and Operations

How would you design this system to survive an outage of its own primary cloud region?
Active-active across at least two regions for both the application tier and the incident store, with organizations pinned to a home region for normal writes but able to fail over to a secondary region via health-checked routing. Deliberately avoid perfectly correlating the platform's own infrastructure with the exact region/vendor a large share of the customer base runs on.
How do you avoid a single point of failure in actually reaching a human, given that SMS gateways and phone carriers can and do go down?
Contract with at least two independent providers per channel and fail over automatically on elevated error rate or timeout, not just outright failure. Diversify across channel types too, and maintain a dead man's switch: an independently-hosted watchdog that triggers a minimal break-glass page through a wholly separate vendor if the platform stops heartbeating.
How would you test the correctness of a distributed escalation engine — timers, state transitions, claim logic — given it isn't a simple single-process state machine?
Unit-test the pure state-transition table independently of timing. Integration-test the claim mechanism under induced concurrency (multiple worker instances against a shared test database, asserting exactly one claims a given due row). Use fault injection in staging (kill a worker mid-escalation, delay clocks, drop a provider call), and run a continuous synthetic canary alert end-to-end to a real test device as an always-on production correctness check.

Extensions

How would you extend this design to support auto-remediation — running a runbook automatically before paging a human?
Attach an optional pre-escalation action to a policy level: on TRIGGERED, invoke a runbook execution service with the incident context and hold the human escalation for a short grace period. If the runbook succeeds and the underlying alert clears, auto-resolve without ever paging anyone; if it fails or times out, fall through to the normal human escalation chain unchanged, with every auto-remediation attempt logged in the same audit timeline as human actions.

Key Takeaways

Remember
This is the rare system design question where the non-functional requirements outrank the functional ones: an on-call escalation platform must be more reliable than everything it monitors, because it IS the safety net. Every major decision flows from that one fact — durable, database-backed escalation timers instead of in-process ones; an atomic claim mechanism so a growing scheduler fleet can never double-escalate; multi-region active-active with synchronous-enough replication for incident state specifically; and, above all, no single point of failure in the notification path itself, backed by a dead man's switch for the day the whole platform is the thing that's down. Layer deduplication and correlation aggressively (fingerprint + time window) so responders trust the pages they get, and keep the ingestion path fast and asynchronous so a monitoring tool's webhook is never held hostage by downstream fan-out. State the CAP trade-off explicitly: strong consistency for escalation-timer state, availability everywhere else.
