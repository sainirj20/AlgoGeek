SYSTEM DESIGN

Distributed Job Scheduler

A complete, interview-ready design walkthrough: requirements, capacity estimation, API design, a data model split across MySQL and MongoDB, leader-follower vs. consensus-based scheduling, sharding the job space, distributed locking for exactly-once-effective firing, DAG dependency resolution, worker heartbeats and retry/backoff, missed-job catch-up semantics, and a large bank of follow-up interview questions.

Problem Statement

Design a distributed job scheduler: a system that lets any internal service say "run this job at 3:00am tomorrow," "run this job every 5 minutes," or "run job C only after jobs A and B have both succeeded," and have it happen reliably, at a scale of millions of active job definitions and tens of thousands of firings per second at peak. The core architectural insight is that scheduling (deciding when a job becomes due) and execution (actually doing the work) are different problems with different scaling profiles, and the design must decouple them: a Scheduler component decides when a job is due, a distributed Queue holds jobs that are ready to run, and a fleet of Workers pulls from the queue and executes — each layer scaling independently. A bursty backfill of ten million catch-up jobs must never stall a time-critical billing run scheduled for 2:00am, and a single slow-running job must never block the scheduler from noticing the next thousand jobs that are also due right now.

Above all, the system must survive its own failures. A scheduler process crashing mid-decision, a network partition between scheduler replicas, or a fleet-wide rolling restart must never silently cause a due job to be skipped — and, symmetrically, must never cause the same job to fire twice with a non-idempotent, user-visible side effect (charging a customer twice, sending a duplicate paycheck).

Why does this problem matter in an interview?

It's a compact vehicle for several classic distributed-systems sub-problems that show up nowhere else quite so cleanly: leader election and consensus, distributed mutual exclusion (locks) under an unreliable network, idempotency in the presence of at-least-once delivery, sharding a huge keyspace across independent workers, and dependency-graph (DAG) scheduling. A strong candidate treats "a due job must fire, and must fire effectively once" as the load-bearing correctness property, and every other decision — locking, heartbeats, sharding, CDC vs. polling — gets justified in terms of how it protects that property under failure.

Where this pattern shows up in production

  • Slack's internal cron rewrite — a leader-follower design: one leader pod makes all scheduling decisions at a time, several standby followers wait on a lease, and a new leader is promoted within seconds of the old one disappearing.
  • Google's distributed cron service — built on Paxos consensus across a small cluster of scheduler replicas, trading a slightly higher per-decision latency for stronger consistency guarantees in an unreliable multi-datacenter environment.
  • Apache Airflow / Temporal / Kubernetes CronJob / AWS EventBridge Scheduler — all variations on the same theme: durable job/DAG definitions, a component that detects "due now," and a decoupled execution fleet.

Clarifying Questions to Ask the Interviewer

Spend the first few minutes narrowing scope before drawing boxes — this problem has a lot of surface area and the "right" answer changes a lot depending on the answers below.

  1. Are one-off delayed jobs, recurring cron-style jobs, and DAGs of dependent jobs all in scope, or should I focus on one and mention the others as extensions?
  2. What execution guarantee do we need: strict exactly-once, or at-least-once with an idempotency contract the caller must honor?
  3. What are the bounds on job payload size and expected execution duration — are these short (seconds), long-running (hours), or a mix?
  4. Is this a single internal team's scheduler, or a multi-tenant platform serving many independent teams/clients with isolated quotas?
  5. What's the acceptable scheduling latency — how many seconds after a job's due time is it acceptable for it to actually fire?
  6. Roughly how many active job definitions do we need to support, and what's the peak fan-out (e.g. many cron jobs all due at the same top-of-hour timestamp)?
  7. If the scheduler fleet is down for an hour, what should happen to the jobs that were due during that window — run all of them, run once, or skip to the next scheduled occurrence?
  8. Do jobs need to be cancellable or reschedulable after creation, and do we need to preserve execution history for audit/compliance?
  9. For DAGs specifically, what happens when an upstream job fails — does the whole DAG halt, or can independent branches continue?
  10. Single region to start, or does the design need to anticipate multi-region deployment and failover from day one?
Interview tip
Don't stall waiting for every answer — state a reasonable assumption out loud ("I'll assume at-least-once execution with a caller-supplied idempotency contract, since true exactly-once execution of arbitrary side effects is impossible in a distributed system without cooperation from the job itself") and keep moving. Naming the CAP-theorem-shaped trade-off explicitly is exactly the signal interviewers are listening for.

Requirements and Goals

Functional Requirements

  1. Schedule a one-off job to run at a specific future timestamp (a delayed job).
  2. Schedule a recurring job on a cron expression or simple rate (every N minutes/hours), in a specified timezone.
  3. Support DAGs of dependent jobs, where a downstream job only becomes eligible to run once all of its declared upstream jobs have completed successfully.
  4. Allow a caller to cancel a pending job, cancel an entire recurring schedule, or reschedule (change the run time or cron expression) without losing the job's identity or history.
  5. Retry a failed job automatically per a configurable policy: maximum attempts and a backoff strategy.
  6. Expose job and execution status (pending, running, succeeded, failed, retrying, cancelled) via a query API.
  7. Support a per-job priority so that operationally urgent jobs (e.g. a security patch rollout) can be drained ahead of routine, low-priority batch work under contention.
  8. Define explicit catch-up ("misfire") semantics for jobs that were due while the scheduler fleet was unavailable — configurable per job as skip, run-once, or run-all-missed-occurrences.
  9. Support job payloads that reference (rather than embed) large inputs, and both short, bounded executions and long-running executions with heartbeats.
  10. Be multi-tenant: isolate job namespaces, quotas, and execution fairness per owning team or client.

Non-Functional Requirements

  1. At-least-once execution with idempotent semantics: a job might be dispatched twice (a redelivered message, a heartbeat timeout that was actually a false positive) — the effect must be safe to apply twice, and the system must make that easy for job authors, not just possible.
  2. Horizontal scalability to millions of active scheduled jobs and tens of thousands of due-job firings per second at peak, by sharding the scheduling decision itself, not just the execution fleet.
  3. Low scheduling latency: a due job should fire within a few seconds of its due time (e.g. p99 < 5s) even at scale — this is the scheduler's core SLA and the reason change-detection latency matters so much.
  4. High availability with fast failover: no single scheduler process is a permanent single point of failure; a crashed leader or a failed consensus node is detected and replaced within a bounded, short window.
  5. No missed jobs, even across a scheduler crash: the durability of a "this job is due" decision must never depend on any single scheduler process staying alive.
  6. Effectively-once firing under normal operation: distributed locking ensures two scheduler replicas practically never both fire the same due job, and idempotency keys provide a second line of defense when they do.
  7. Observability: scheduling latency, missed-job rate, worker failure rate, and queue backlog must be visible in near real time, since a silent regression here (a job quietly never firing) is the worst possible failure mode.

System / API Design

Callers talk to the scheduler through a small, versioned API. A single create-job call supports three shapes of "when": a one-off timestamp, a cron expression, or a reference to a node inside a DAG definition — so the ingestion path doesn't need three different endpoints for what is fundamentally the same lifecycle.

Data Model and Database Choice

Nature of the Data

  • Job and cron metadata is narrow, high-write, and state-machine-shaped: a job moves through SCHEDULED → QUEUED → RUNNING → SUCCEEDED/FAILED, and a distributed lock acquisition must be atomic with that state flip or two replicas can both believe they own the same due job.
  • DAG structure is hierarchical and variable-shaped: one DAG might have 3 nodes, another 300, with arbitrary fan-in/fan-out — an awkward fit for a fixed set of relational join tables at scale, a natural fit for a single nested document per DAG.
  • Execution history is an append-mostly, time-ordered log, read far less often than it's written, and needs cheap time-based retention/archival.

Why MySQL + MongoDB, Not Just One

Job and cron metadata gets a relational store (MySQL) specifically because of the ACID guarantee on state transitions: "acquire the lock for job X and flip it to RUNNING" must be a single atomic operation, and a unique constraint plus a transaction is the simplest, most battle-tested way to get that. DAG structure gets a document store (MongoDB) specifically because a workflow's shape is naturally a tree/graph of arbitrary width, and modeling that as normalized relational tables (a job_dependencies join table with recursive joins to compute "has everything upstream succeeded?") is both slower and more awkward than reading one document and walking its embedded edge list. The pattern mirrors a broader lesson: pick the storage engine to match the access pattern and consistency need of each entity, not one engine for the whole system.

Interview tip
If pushed on "why not just Mongo everywhere, including job/cron state": a document store's default eventual-consistency and lack of true cross-document transactions make "exactly one replica wins the lock for this job" much harder to get right under contention than a relational unique constraint plus a row-level transaction. Reach for the ACID store where correctness under concurrent writers is the requirement, and the document store where flexible shape is the requirement — say that trade-off out loud rather than defending one database as universally better.

See "Database Schema" below for the full table-by-table design; this section covers the storage-technology choice, not every column.

High-Level Design

Job and cron metadata lives in MySQL; DAG structure lives in MongoDB. A CDC pipeline (Kafka Connect reading MySQL's binlog) turns "a row just became due" into a low-latency change-stream event instead of an expensive poll. A fleet of Scheduler shards consumes that stream, each shard responsible for only its own partition of the job space; when a shard sees one of its jobs is due, it acquires a distributed lock, verifies an idempotency key, and publishes the job onto a priority-aware Queue. A Worker fleet pulls from the queue, executes, sends periodic heartbeats, and writes the execution outcome back into the datastore.

Figure 1. High-level architecture — CDC turns due-row changes in MySQL/MongoDB into a low-latency stream; sharded Scheduler replicas lock and dispatch onto a priority queue; a Worker fleet executes and an Execution Tracker writes status back into the datastore.

Key Design Principles Reflected in the Diagram

  • Change detection, not polling, drives the SLA: CDC turns "a row just became due" into an event in tens to hundreds of milliseconds, which is most of the scheduling-latency budget spent before a job is even locked.
  • Sharding happens before locking: each Scheduler shard only ever sees its own partition of the job space, so the distributed lock only has to arbitrate contention among the (small) set of replicas that could plausibly race for the same shard, not the whole fleet.
  • The queue decouples "decided to fire" from "actually executed": the Worker fleet scales on execution load (which can spike independently, e.g. a few jobs that each run for hours), while the Scheduler fleet scales on job-population size.
  • Status flows back through the same datastore that drove the original decision, closing the loop so retries, catch-up, and monitoring all read a single source of truth.

Deep Dive — Leader-Follower vs. Consensus-Based Scheduling

Sharding (next section) parallelizes the scheduler fleet, but within a single shard someone still has to be the one process making scheduling decisions at any given moment, or two replicas will race for the same job. There are two well-proven production patterns for that, and they trade off differently.

Leader-Follower (Slack-style)

One pod holds a time-boxed lease and acts as the leader for a shard; every other replica is a passive standby follower, continuously renewing its watch on the lease but making zero scheduling decisions. If the leader stops renewing its lease (crash, network partition, deploy), the lease expires and a follower acquires it, becoming the new leader within roughly one lease-TTL's worth of time — typically single-digit seconds. This is operationally simple: at any instant, "who is deciding right now" has exactly one, easily-inspectable answer, and there's no multi-node agreement protocol to reason about on the hot path.

Consensus-Based (Google-style, Paxos)

Instead of one process unilaterally deciding, a small cluster of scheduler replicas runs a consensus protocol (Paxos, or a Paxos-derivative like Raft) and a scheduling decision is only considered durable once a majority of replicas have agreed to it. This survives a wider range of failure/partition scenarios without any window where the "wrong" node could believe it's in charge (a stale leader that hasn't yet noticed its lease expired), at the cost of every decision requiring a network round-trip to a quorum rather than a purely local check — higher per-decision latency and materially more implementation complexity.

Figure 2. Leader-follower trades a small failover window for operational simplicity; consensus removes that window at the cost of a quorum round-trip on every scheduling decision.

Deep Dive — Sharding the Job Space Across Scheduler Replicas

Neither leader-follower nor consensus scales to millions of jobs if there's only one shard to lead or agree on — a single process (or a single quorum) scanning tens of millions of rows every few seconds is the real bottleneck long before lock contention is. The fix is to shard the job space itself: partition all active jobs across many independent scheduler shards, each of which runs its own leader-follower (or consensus) group and scans/fires only its own slice.

Two shard-key choices are common, and they optimize for different things. Sharding by hash(job_id) spreads load evenly regardless of when jobs are due, and is simple to reason about, but means every shard must still scan its full slice on every tick looking for due rows. Sharding by next_run_time bucket (e.g. one bucket per minute) means a shard only ever has to look at the (small) set of jobs due in its current bucket, which is far cheaper per tick, at the cost of uneven load across shards if job due-times cluster (the thundering-herd problem again) — the two are often combined: hash(job_id) to pick a shard, next_run_time bucketing within that shard to keep the scan cheap.

Figure 3. The job space is partitioned across scheduler shards by hash(job_id), with each shard further bucketing by next_run_time so it only ever scans jobs that could plausibly be due right now.

Deep Dive — Distributed Locking and Exactly-Once-Effective Firing

Even within one shard, a rolling deploy, a brief network blip, or an overlapping lease-renewal race can leave two scheduler replicas both believing they own the same due job for a moment. A distributed lock (a short-TTL lease in etcd, ZooKeeper, or Redis, keyed by job_id) makes only one of them the actual dispatcher: replica A's SET lock:job_7fa2 A NX PX 5000-style call succeeds; replica B's identical call fails because the key already exists, and B backs off rather than publishing a duplicate. The lock's TTL is deliberately short and renewed while work is in progress, so a crashed lock-holder's lease expires on its own and another replica can safely take over — no permanently stuck lock waiting on a human.

The lock alone isn't a complete guarantee, though: a replica can acquire the lock, crash after publishing to the queue but before renewing or releasing it, and its lease can still expire and be re-acquired by someone else who — not knowing the job was already published — could publish it again. This is exactly why the design layers a second, independent defense: an idempotency key attached to the job at creation time, checked with an insert-if-absent ("claim") write into the execution-history store immediately before publish. If the claim fails because a row for that firing already exists, the second attempt is a no-op. The lock minimizes double-firing in the common case (cheap, fast); the idempotency claim makes double-firing safe in the rare case the lock alone can't prevent.

Figure 4. A short-TTL distributed lock resolves the common case cheaply (first writer wins, second backs off); an idempotency-keyed claim write is the second, independent guard that makes even a rare lock-edge-case double-fire safe.

Idempotency Keys at the Execution Layer

The idempotency key is deterministic and derivable from the job and its scheduled occurrence — typically hash(job_id, scheduled_time) — so that any replica computing it for "job X's 2:00am firing" arrives at the same value, regardless of which replica does the computing or how many times it's retried. This is what turns "at-least-once dispatch" into "effectively-once execution" without needing a perfect lock: the lock is an optimization that avoids most duplicate work, and the idempotency claim is the correctness guarantee that holds even when the optimization fails.

Deep Dive — DAG Execution and Dependency Resolution

A DAG is a set of jobs (nodes) with directed edges expressing "B depends on A." A node becomes eligible to run only once every node it depends on has completed with status SUCCEEDED — the dependency resolver's entire job is answering "are all of this node's upstream dependencies satisfied?" cheaply, at scale, without re-walking the whole graph on every check.

The practical mechanism: each DAG node carries a materialized "pending dependency count." When an upstream node succeeds, the resolver decrements the pending-count of every direct downstream node in a single atomic operation; any node whose count reaches zero is immediately eligible and gets scheduled. This turns "is B ready?" from an O(graph size) traversal into an O(1) check on a counter, at the cost of maintaining that counter correctly as the DAG structure or execution state changes — which is why the counter lives alongside the relational job_runs state (MySQL) even though the DAG's edges live in MongoDB: the state-transition-with-a-counter-decrement needs the same ACID guarantee that the plain job locking does.

Figure 5. Job D's pending-dependency counter starts at 2 (B and C) and is atomically decremented as each upstream job succeeds; D is scheduled the instant it reaches zero, without re-walking the DAG.

Fan-in / Fan-out and Partial Failure in a DAG

Fan-out (A feeding both B and C) is the easy case — both simply become eligible independently. Fan-in (D depending on both B and C) is where partial failure matters: if B fails after retries are exhausted, D's dependency can never be satisfied, and the resolver marks D (and anything downstream of D) as UPSTREAM_FAILED rather than leaving it pending forever. Whether a sibling branch that doesn't depend on the failed node (an unrelated E depending only on A) should keep running is a per-DAG policy choice — the default is "yes, independent branches proceed," with an opt-in "halt entire DAG on any node failure" mode for workflows where partial completion is meaningless (e.g. a financial close process).

Deep Dive — Worker Heartbeats, Timeouts, and Retry-with-Backoff

Once a job is dispatched to a worker, the scheduler's job isn't done — it needs to detect a worker that has silently died (crashed process, OOM-killed container, severed network) rather than waiting indefinitely for a result that will never come. Every worker sends a periodic heartbeat while executing; the scheduler (or a dedicated heartbeat monitor) tracks the last-seen heartbeat per in-flight job and, if it exceeds a timeout threshold (typically 2–3× the heartbeat interval, to tolerate a missed beat or two without overreacting), treats the job as abandoned: the worker's lease on that job is revoked, and the job is returned to RETRYING with its attempt counter incremented, ready to be picked up by a different worker.

Figure 6. Heartbeat-based failure detection: a missing heartbeat past the timeout threshold triggers lease revocation and reassignment; the idempotency claim from the Locking deep dive fences off a stale, late-arriving result from the original worker.

Tuning the Heartbeat Timeout

Timeout multiple of intervalFalse-positive risk (healthy worker reassigned)Detection latency for a truly dead worker
1× (timeout == interval)High — a single slow heartbeat triggers a needless reassignmentFastest
2–3× (recommended)Low — tolerates a missed beat or two under loadBounded, still fast (tens of seconds)
5×+Very lowSlow — a genuinely dead worker's job sits stalled longer than necessary

Exponential backoff on the retry itself (e.g. 2s → 4s → 8s → 16s, capped at a configurable max attempts) avoids hammering a downstream dependency that's the actual root cause of repeated failures (a database the job writes to being temporarily overloaded), and a job that exhausts its retry budget is marked DEAD_LETTERED for alerting and manual or scheduled reprocessing, mirroring the retry/DLQ pattern used throughout distributed messaging systems generally.

The dag_dependencies table is a deliberate, denormalized bridge: the authoritative graph shape lives in MongoDB (easy to author, version, and read as a whole), but the scheduler's hot-path question — "is job X's pending-dependency count now zero?" — needs the same atomic-decrement-and-check guarantee as the distributed lock, which only a relational store with transactions gives cheaply at high concurrency. This is the same storage-choice principle as the top-level Data Model decision, applied one level deeper.

Data Partitioning and Replication

Sharding follows the same principle throughout: keep everything needed to make one decision atomically on the same shard, and let unrelated shards scale independently.

  • jobs / cron_schedules / job_runs are sharded by hash(job_id) across a MySQL fleet (e.g. via a proxy layer like Vitess), so a job's metadata, its lock, and its execution history are always co-located — no cross-shard transaction is ever needed to lock and dispatch a single job.
  • dag_definitions are sharded by dag_id in MongoDB, matching the unit callers actually read and write (the whole graph at once).
  • job_runs is additionally partitioned by time (daily partitions) so that 30-day retention is enforced by dropping old partitions outright, rather than issuing expensive row-by-row deletes against a table with hundreds of millions of rows.
  • The priority job queue is implemented as topics/partitions per priority tier (P0/P1/P2), mirroring the same "physically isolate the urgent lane" pattern used in other high-fan-out systems: a P2 batch flood can only ever back up its own partitions.

Feature-Specific Logic: Missed-Job Catch-Up After Scheduler Downtime

If the scheduler fleet (or a shard of it) is unavailable for a stretch of time, some recurring jobs will have one or more occurrences that were due during the outage and never fired. What should happen when the shard comes back is a genuine product decision, not a purely technical one — different jobs want different answers, which is why catch_up_policy is a per-job field rather than a global setting.

PolicyBehavior on recoveryGood fit for
SKIP (default)Discard every missed occurrence; resume from the next naturally scheduled timeFrequent, "freshness doesn't compound" jobs — a metrics-refresh job that runs every 5 minutes doesn't need 12 backfilled runs after an hour-long outage
RUN_ONCEFire exactly one catch-up run representing "the job needs to happen," then resume normal cadenceJobs where the effect isn't cumulative — a daily cache-warm job just needs to have run once, not once per missed day
RUN_ALL (backfill)Fire once per missed occurrence, in order, as fast as capacity allowsJobs whose effect is cumulative or order-sensitive — an hourly billing-ledger rollup where each hour's numbers must actually be computed

A misfire_grace_period bounds how "missed" is defined in the first place: an occurrence detected within the grace period of its due time is treated as a normal, slightly-late firing, not a misfire requiring catch-up logic at all. Beyond the grace period, the catch-up policy takes over. RUN_ALL deserves special care operationally: backfilling, say, six hours of missed hourly jobs the instant a shard recovers can itself create a mini thundering herd, so a well-built scheduler enqueues RUN_ALL catch-up firings at a lower priority tier and paces them rather than dumping them all onto the queue simultaneously.

Rate Limiting and Abuse Prevention

  • Per-tenant active-job quota (maximum concurrently-scheduled jobs, and a rate limit on job-creation calls per minute), enforced at the API gateway, so one team's misconfigured retry loop creating jobs in a tight loop can't exhaust shared scheduler or storage capacity.
  • Runaway-job circuit breaker: a job that fails N consecutive times in a row (distinct from N attempts within one firing's retry budget) is automatically suspended rather than continuing to re-fire on its normal cadence, since a recurring job stuck in a fail-immediately loop can otherwise burn worker capacity indefinitely while producing zero value.
  • Fair-share worker allocation: the worker fleet enforces a per-tenant concurrency ceiling so one tenant's enormous DAG (thousands of parallel nodes becoming eligible at once) can't starve every other tenant's jobs of execution slots.
  • Priority-aware shedding under genuine overload: if ingestion or execution capacity is truly exceeded, shed P2 (batch) first, then P1, protecting P0 — the priority-tiered queue makes this a natural, already-built-in policy.

Security, Privacy and Permissions

  • Multi-tenant isolation: every table is keyed by tenant_id as the leading key element, so a query or a bug can't cross tenant boundaries by construction; quotas, priorities, and catch-up policies are all configured per tenant.
  • Payload references, not raw payloads: job definitions store a pointer to input data in a separate, access-controlled blob store rather than embedding potentially sensitive payloads directly in job metadata, limiting the blast radius of a metadata-store leak.
  • Least-privilege execution sandboxing: workers execute each job's handler in an isolated container/sandbox scoped to only the credentials that handler needs, so a compromised or buggy job can't reach another tenant's data or another handler's credentials.
  • Per-caller authentication and authorization: API keys or mTLS identify the calling service on every create/cancel/reschedule call, and permission checks ensure a caller can only manage jobs it owns (or that its tenant owns).
  • Audit logging: every create, cancel, reschedule, and manual DLQ-replay action is logged with the acting principal, since "who cancelled the nightly billing job" is exactly the kind of question that comes up during an incident review.
  • Transport security: TLS everywhere; secrets (database credentials, lock-store credentials) stored in a secrets manager, rotated regularly, and never logged.

Load Balancing

  1. Between callers and the ingestion API — a standard L7 load balancer, health-checked, in front of a stateless API tier.
  2. Across scheduler shards — consistent hashing on the job-space partition key, so adding or removing a shard reassigns only a thin slice of the keyspace rather than triggering a full-fleet reshuffle.
  3. Across the worker fleet — inherently self-balancing, since workers pull from the priority queue at their own pace rather than being pushed work; a slow or busy worker simply pulls less often, and auto-scaling adds workers as queue depth per priority tier grows.

The one place that benefits from active load-awareness rather than passive pulling is DAG fan-out bursts: when a wide DAG's root succeeds and dozens of downstream nodes become eligible simultaneously, a rate-aware dispatcher that paces publication to the queue (rather than dumping all of them at once) avoids a self-inflicted mini thundering herd.

Scaling to Multiple Regions

  • Run each region active-active, with each job pinned to a "home" region for scheduling decisions — this avoids the alternative of cross-region consensus on every single due-job decision, which would blow the scheduling-latency SLA.
  • The distributed lock and idempotency store are region-local for a job's home region; cross-region replication of job/cron metadata is asynchronous, used for disaster-recovery failover rather than every-decision coordination.
  • If a whole region fails, jobs homed there fail over to a designated secondary region after a bounded detection window; because the idempotency key is deterministic (derived from job_id and scheduled_time, not from which region computed it), the secondary region can safely resume firing without risking a double-fire for occurrences the primary region already completed and replicated before failing.
  • Workers execute in the same region as the job's data dependencies where possible, to avoid needless cross-region latency on the execution path itself — the scheduler's job is to decide "when," not to force execution somewhere inconvenient for "where."

CAP Theorem Lens

Favor consistency for the lock-and-dispatch decision specifically — a stale or partitioned scheduler replica must not be able to fire a job the current lock-holder already fired, so this path leans on a quorum-backed lock store rather than an eventually-consistent one. Favor availability for status reads and monitoring — a slightly stale "is job X still running?" read is far better than blocking an operator's dashboard, and the idempotency claim (not the read path) is what actually protects correctness.

Leader-Follower vs. Consensus, Revisited

This is the design's single biggest philosophical trade-off, worth restating here: leader-follower buys operational simplicity and near-zero decision latency at the cost of a small, bounded window of potential lateness during failover; consensus buys a stronger guarantee (no window at all) at the cost of a network round-trip on every decision and materially higher implementation complexity. Most teams should default to leader-follower and reserve full consensus for the small subset of job classes where even a few seconds of potential lateness during a rare failover is unacceptable.

Monitoring, Logging and Analytics

  • Scheduling latency (due-time to actual-fire-time), tracked as a distribution (p50/p99), is the single most important SLI — a regression here means the core promise of the system is degrading, often silently.
  • Missed-job rate — jobs whose next_run_time passed without ever transitioning to QUEUED within the misfire grace period; ideally zero, and any non-zero rate deserves an immediate alert, not a dashboard glance.
  • Worker failure rate — heartbeat-timeout-triggered reassignments as a fraction of total dispatches; a rising rate often signals infrastructure trouble (OOM kills, node churn) rather than job-logic bugs.
  • Queue depth / backlog per priority tier — a growing P2 backlog is expected and fine; a growing P0 backlog is an incident.
  • Lock contention rate — how often a replica's lock-acquisition attempt fails because another replica already holds it; a rising rate can indicate shard imbalance or a leader that isn't failing over cleanly.
  • Catch-up backlog size after an incident — how many missed occurrences are queued for RUN_ALL backfill, to gauge how long "back to normal" will actually take.
  • Per-tenant quota utilization — surfaced to tenants themselves so a team approaching its active-job quota gets a warning before it starts seeing 429s.

Common Interview Questions

Grouped by theme — expect a real interview to pick several of these as "and then what?" follow-ups on whatever you draw first.

Coordination and Scheduling

Leader-follower vs. Paxos-based consensus — how do you decide which one to use?
Leader-follower gives near-instant local decisions and a simple mental model, at the cost of a small, bounded failover window (roughly one lease TTL) where the "wrong" node could theoretically still believe it's leading. Consensus removes that window entirely by requiring a quorum to agree on every decision, at the cost of a network round-trip per decision and much higher implementation complexity. Default to leader-follower; reserve consensus for job classes where zero tolerance for a stale-leader misfire is worth the cost.
How do you scale scheduling itself to millions of jobs, not just execution?
Shard the job space across many independent scheduler replicas (by hash(job_id), sub-bucketed by next_run_time), each running its own leader-follower or consensus group and scanning only its own partition. A single process or quorum scanning the entire job population is the real bottleneck long before lock contention becomes one.
Why shard by both hash(job_id) and next_run_time bucket instead of just one?
hash(job_id) alone spreads jobs evenly but still forces every shard to scan its whole slice looking for due rows every tick. next_run_time bucketing alone narrows the scan cheaply but creates hot buckets when due-times cluster (the thundering-herd pattern). Combining them gets even load distribution from the hash and a cheap per-tick scan from the bucket.
How do you mitigate a thundering herd of jobs all due at the same top-of-hour timestamp?
Add a small random jitter to next_run_time at job-creation time so a batch of "top of the hour" cron jobs spreads across, say, a 30–60 second window instead of firing in the same instant; pace dispatch from the priority queue rather than publishing an entire due-minute's jobs simultaneously; and size the priority queue and worker auto-scaling for the peak multiplier anyway, since jitter smooths but doesn't eliminate the spike.

Correctness: Idempotency and Locking

How do you achieve exactly-once execution when the system only guarantees at-least-once delivery?
You don't try to make delivery exactly-once — you make the effect of duplicate delivery a no-op. A deterministic idempotency key (hash of job_id and scheduled_time) backed by a unique constraint and a conditional "claim" insert into the execution-history store means a redelivered or duplicate-dispatched job fails its claim and is treated as already-handled, rather than re-executed.
Isn't the distributed lock enough to prevent double-firing by itself?
Almost, but not quite: a replica can acquire the lock, publish, then crash before releasing or renewing it, and its short-TTL lease can expire and be re-acquired by another replica that doesn't know the job was already published. The lock minimizes duplicate work in the common case; the idempotency claim is the independent guarantee that makes even that edge case safe.
What if the job's side effect (e.g. calling a third-party payment API) isn't naturally idempotent?
Pass the idempotency key through to the downstream call if the third party supports idempotency keys (most modern payment/webhook APIs do); if it doesn't, the job author is responsible for making the effect idempotent internally (e.g. checking "has this charge already been recorded?" before charging) — the scheduler's guarantee is "effectively-once dispatch," and true effect-level idempotency for an arbitrary external system ultimately requires cooperation from that system or the job's own logic.

DAGs and Dependency Resolution

How does the scheduler know when a downstream job's dependencies are satisfied?
Each node maintains a pending-dependency counter, atomically decremented whenever a direct upstream node succeeds. A node becomes eligible the instant its counter reaches zero — an O(1) check rather than re-walking the whole graph on every upstream completion.
What happens to a DAG when one of its jobs fails permanently?
Any node depending (directly or transitively) on the failed node is marked UPSTREAM_FAILED rather than left pending forever. Whether unrelated sibling branches keep running is a per-DAG policy (HALT_DAG vs. CONTINUE_INDEPENDENT_BRANCHES) — the default lets independent branches proceed, since most workflows have partially-useful partial results.
Why store the DAG structure in MongoDB but the dependency-resolution counters in MySQL?
The DAG's shape (which nodes, which edges) is read and authored as a whole document and varies wildly in width/depth per DAG — a natural fit for a flexible document store. The moment-to-moment "is this counter now zero?" check is a high-concurrency atomic read-modify-write that needs the same ACID guarantee as job locking generally, which is why a denormalized, relational dag_dependencies table mirrors the counters even though the graph's source of truth lives elsewhere.

Failure Handling: Heartbeats and Retries

How do you detect a worker that has silently crashed mid-execution?
Workers send periodic heartbeats while running; the scheduler tracks last-seen-heartbeat per in-flight job and treats a job as abandoned once the gap exceeds a timeout (typically 2–3× the heartbeat interval). The job's lease is revoked and it's reassigned with an incremented attempt count and the same idempotency key.
What if the "crashed" worker actually wasn't dead and finishes late, after reassignment?
Its result is fenced off by the same idempotency-claim mechanism used for locking: by the time it tries to record its outcome, the reassigned attempt has already claimed that idempotency key, so the late result is recognized as stale and discarded (though both attempts remain visible in job_runs for audit).
How do you choose the heartbeat timeout threshold?
Too tight (near 1× the heartbeat interval) causes false-positive reassignments of healthy-but-momentarily-slow workers; too loose (5×+) delays detecting genuinely dead workers. 2–3× the interval is the common sweet spot, tunable per job class based on how costly a false-positive reassignment is versus how costly a delayed detection is.

Missed-Job Catch-Up and Multi-Region

The scheduler fleet was down for two hours — what happens to jobs that were due during that window?
Depends on the job's catch_up_policy: SKIP discards the missed occurrences and resumes normal cadence; RUN_ONCE fires a single catch-up run representing "this needs to happen" and resumes; RUN_ALL fires once per missed occurrence, paced at lower priority to avoid creating a self-inflicted thundering herd on recovery. This is a per-job product decision, not a one-size-fits-all technical default.
How do you fail a job over to a secondary region safely without risking a double-fire?
Because the idempotency key is deterministic (derived from job_id and scheduled_time, independent of which region computes it), the secondary region can safely resume firing occurrences the primary hadn't yet completed and replicated — any occurrence the primary already finished and replicated will simply fail its claim in the secondary, exactly as it would against a same-region duplicate.
Why pin each job to a home region instead of a fully active-active global scheduler?
Cross-region consensus on every single due-job decision would add a cross-datacenter round-trip to the scheduling-latency SLA for every job, everywhere, all the time — an expensive tax paid on the common case to protect against the rare case of a full regional outage. Home-region pinning pays that cost only during an actual failover.

Scaling and Multi-Tenancy

How do you prevent one tenant's huge DAG or job-creation loop from degrading the system for everyone else?
A per-tenant active-job quota and job-creation rate limit at the API gateway, plus a fair-share worker concurrency ceiling so one tenant's sudden fan-out of thousands of eligible DAG nodes can't monopolize the worker fleet's execution slots — both are independent of the global priority-tier budget.
Why CDC instead of polling for detecting due jobs?
Polling forces a trade-off between wasted read load (polling frequently against a mostly-not-due table) and detection latency (polling infrequently means a job can sit due-but-undetected for the whole poll interval). CDC via the database's binlog turns "a row just became due" into a push-style event within tens to hundreds of milliseconds, with no wasted scanning of rows that aren't due yet.

Key Takeaways

Remember
Decoupling "when" from "execute" is the design's spine: a Scheduler, a Queue, and a Worker fleet that each scale on their own axis. Sharding the job space is what actually lets scheduling itself scale to millions of jobs — locking and consensus only need to arbitrate within a shard, not across the whole fleet. Treat the distributed lock as an optimization and the idempotency key as the correctness guarantee; state that distinction explicitly, since a lock alone cannot survive every crash timing. Leader-follower and Paxos-based consensus are both legitimate answers to "who decides" — know the failover-window-vs-decision-latency trade-off between them rather than treating one as universally correct. And because missed-job catch-up, DAG partial failure, and multi-region failover all hinge on the same deterministic idempotency key, get that one design decision right first — nearly everything else in the system leans on it.
