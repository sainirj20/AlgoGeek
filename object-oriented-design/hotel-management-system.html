OBJECT ORIENTED DESIGN

Design a Hotel Management System

Where Parking Lot asks "does a physical resource have capacity right now," the Hotel Management System asks the harder version: "is this physical resource free for an entire date range that hasn't happened yet." That shift — from an instant-in-time capacity check to an interval-overlap check — is the single hardest correctness requirement in this problem, and it's the same shape of question tested by seat-hold problems in movie-ticket and airline systems, just keyed by a date range instead of a show or flight instance. Layered on top of that is a modeling decision interviewers love to probe: a Reservation (a promise about the future) and a Stay (what actually happened) are deliberately two different classes, because a no-show reservation and a walk-in stay both need to exist in this model without corrupting each other. Treat this page as the canonical "interval scheduling plus advance-booking-vs-realized-event" template — the same pattern reappears anywhere a physical or schedulable resource is booked ahead of time: equipment rental, meeting rooms, rental cars.

Problem Statement

Design the room booking and stay-management system for a hotel chain. The chain operates one or more properties; each property (Hotel) holds an inventory of Rooms, and each Room belongs to a RoomType — Standard, Deluxe, or Suite — that carries its own nightly rate. A guest searches for availability by date range and room type, places a Reservation that holds one or more rooms for a check-in/check-out window, and eventually shows up to check in (converting that advance booking into a real, physical occupancy) and later to check out (closing the stay and settling the bill). Along the way a room also carries a housekeeping status — it has to be cleaned after every stay before the front desk can hand it to the next guest. Interviewers use this problem to see whether you can design a correct interval-overlap check (the same skill tested by seat-booking problems elsewhere in this section, just with dates instead of seats), whether you separate a future intent (Reservation) from a realized event (Stay) instead of collapsing them into one overloaded class, and whether you proactively raise the concurrency question — two requests racing for the last room of a type — rather than waiting to be asked.

Requirements

  1. A Hotel has multiple Rooms, and each Room has a RoomType (Standard, Deluxe, or Suite) and a nightly rate.
  2. Guests can search room availability for a given date range and room type.
  3. A Reservation books one or more Rooms for a check-in/check-out date range and must never double-book the same Room over overlapping date ranges — the core hard requirement of this problem, analogous to the seat/date overlap problems elsewhere in this section.
  4. Check-in assigns/confirms a specific physical Room for the stay.
  5. Check-out finalizes the Bill (room charges plus any extra services) and marks the Room for housekeeping/cleaning before it can be booked again.
  6. Every Room carries a housekeeping status: Clean, Dirty, Being-Cleaned, Out-of-Service, or Ready.
  7. Guests may extend a stay or check out early, subject to the Room's availability for the changed date range.
  8. Admin manages room inventory and pricing (adding/removing Rooms, editing RoomType rates).

Actors & Use Cases

Four actors touch the system, each initiating at least one use case none of the others can:

  • Guest — searches availability, creates a Reservation, checks in, checks out, and views their bill. The only actor who is actually staying in a room.
  • Front-Desk Staff — performs check-in and check-out on a guest's behalf, and handles walk-ins — guests who show up with no prior Reservation at all.
  • Housekeeping Staff — updates a Room's cleaning status as it moves through the cleaning queue after a Stay closes.
  • Admin — manages the Room inventory and per-RoomType pricing. Never touches a single guest's Reservation or Stay.

Primary use cases: Search Room Availability, Create Reservation, Check In, Check Out, View Bill, Update Room Status, and Manage Room Inventory (admin).

The Reservation-vs-Stay split is the central modeling decision of this page. A Reservation is an advance promise: it exists the moment a guest books, carries a future date range, and can end its life several ways that never touch a physical room at all — it can be Cancelled, or it can simply expire into NoShow if the guest never turns up. A Stay is the realized occupancy: it comes into existence only at the moment of check-in, references the actual Room the guest is physically in, and tracks the actual check-in/check-out timestamps, which can differ from what the Reservation promised (an early checkout, a late checkout, an extended stay). Modeling these as one merged class forces awkward compromises in both directions: a NoShow needs a Stay-shaped object with no real occupancy in it, and a walk-in guest — who checks in with no prior booking — needs a Reservation-shaped object that was never actually reserved. Keeping them separate, with Stay holding an optional (0..1) reference back to the Reservation that produced it, lets both cases fall out naturally: a NoShow Reservation simply never gets a Stay linked to it, and a walk-in Stay simply has reservation = null. This is the same "intent vs. realized event" split that shows up whenever a system has to track both a future booking and what actually happened — an appointment vs. a completed visit, an order vs. a fulfilled shipment.

Design Patterns Applied

Name the pattern (or algorithm) and the forcing function together, not just the label:

  • Interval-overlap check (not a GoF pattern, but the single most important piece of logic on this page) — every "is this Room free?" question reduces to a reusable date-range-overlap test. Model each range as half-open, [checkIn, checkOut), and two ranges overlap exactly when startA < endB && startB < endA. This is the same shape of problem as the seat-hold/seat-lock check in the Movie Ticket Booking and Airline pages elsewhere in this section — there it's "does this seat have a conflicting hold for this showtime/flight instance," here it's "does this room have a conflicting Reservation/Stay for this date range." Same algorithm, different key.
  • State — two separate, easily-confused state machines live here. Reservation status: Confirmed → CheckedIn → CheckedOut, with CheckedIn instead diverting to NoShow (guest never arrives) or Cancelled (guest backs out early) — a small, closed set of terminal outcomes worth encoding as an enum with guarded transitions rather than free-form booleans. Room status: conceptually Available → Reserved/Occupied → Dirty → Clean → back to Available, but implemented as two cooperating pieces of state rather than one — occupancy is derived on demand from overlapping Reservations/Stays (so it's never stale), while HousekeepingStatus is stored directly on Room because cleanliness genuinely doesn't derive from anything else the system already tracks.
  • Observer (Housekeeping notified when a Stay closes) — the moment Stay.close() runs, it doesn't just mutate the Room in place; it publishes a "stay closed" event that a housekeeping-queue subscriber picks up to enqueue the Room for cleaning. Wiring it as a subscription rather than a hardcoded call keeps Stay from needing to know anything about how the housekeeping team's dashboard or queue is implemented — the same decoupling the display boards get in the Parking Lot page.
  • Decorator-style composition (Bill line items) — a Bill doesn't hardcode "room charge + minibar + room service" as fixed fields; it holds a List<LineItem> and exposes addLineItem()/getTotal(). Any new Service/Amenity type becomes one more LineItem appended at charge time, with zero changes to the Bill class itself — the same "don't touch the aggregator when a new leaf type shows up" idea Decorator captures, done here with plain composition since there's no need for LineItems to wrap one another.
Interval overlap Two half-open ranges [startA, endA) and [startB, endB) overlap exactly when startA < endB && startB < endA. Worked example: an existing booking for [Mar 10, Mar 14) and a new request for [Mar 13, Mar 16) — 10 < 16 is true and 13 < 14 is true, so they overlap (both conditions must hold). A request for [Mar 14, Mar 16) against that same existing booking does not overlap: 10 < 16 is true, but 14 < 14 is false, so the second condition fails — the checkout day is free for the next guest's check-in, which is exactly why the range is modeled half-open.

Code Skeleton

Fields and method signatures only — not a runnable program. DateRange.overlaps is worked out in full, since the interval-overlap logic is exactly what interviewers probe on.

public final class DateRange {
    private final LocalDate checkIn;   // inclusive
    private final LocalDate checkOut;  // exclusive

    public DateRange(LocalDate checkIn, LocalDate checkOut) {
        if (!checkIn.isBefore(checkOut)) {
            throw new IllegalArgumentException("checkIn must be before checkOut");
        }
        this.checkIn = checkIn;
        this.checkOut = checkOut;
    }

    // Half-open interval overlap: [checkIn, checkOut) vs [other.checkIn, other.checkOut)
    public boolean overlaps(DateRange other) {
        return this.checkIn.isBefore(other.checkOut) && other.checkIn.isBefore(this.checkOut);
    }

    public LocalDate getCheckIn() { return checkIn; }
    public LocalDate getCheckOut() { return checkOut; }
}

public enum ReservationStatus {
    CONFIRMED, CHECKED_IN, CHECKED_OUT, NO_SHOW, CANCELLED
}

public enum HousekeepingStatus {
    CLEAN, DIRTY, BEING_CLEANED, OUT_OF_SERVICE, READY
}

public enum ChargeKind {
    ROOM, SERVICE
}
public class Room {
    private final String roomId;
    private final String roomNumber;
    private final RoomType roomType;
    private volatile HousekeepingStatus housekeepingStatus;

    public Room(String roomId, String roomNumber, RoomType roomType) { /* ... */ }

    // "Available" is derived, not stored: true only if no CONFIRMED/CHECKED_IN
    // reservation for this room overlaps the requested range.
    public boolean isAvailableFor(DateRange range, List<Reservation> existingReservations) {
        return existingReservations.stream()
                .filter(r -> r.getStatus() == ReservationStatus.CONFIRMED
                          || r.getStatus() == ReservationStatus.CHECKED_IN)
                .noneMatch(r -> r.getDateRange().overlaps(range));
    }

    public RoomType getRoomType();
    public HousekeepingStatus getHousekeepingStatus();
    public synchronized void setHousekeepingStatus(HousekeepingStatus status);
}
public class Reservation {
    private final String reservationId;
    private final String guestId;
    private final List<String> roomIds;   // one or more rooms, all-or-nothing hold
    private final DateRange dateRange;
    private volatile ReservationStatus status;

    public Reservation(String reservationId, String guestId,
                        List<String> roomIds, DateRange dateRange) { /* ... */ }

    public boolean overlaps(DateRange other) {
        return dateRange.overlaps(other);
    }

    public DateRange getDateRange();
    public ReservationStatus getStatus();
    public void markCheckedIn();
    public void markCheckedOut();
    public void markNoShow();
    public void cancel();
}
public class Stay {
    private final String stayId;
    private final Reservation reservation;   // nullable: walk-ins have no prior reservation
    private final Room room;
    private final LocalDateTime actualCheckIn;
    private LocalDateTime actualCheckOut;
    private final Bill bill;

    public Stay(String stayId, Reservation reservation, Room room,
                LocalDateTime actualCheckIn, Bill bill) { /* ... */ }

    public Bill close(LocalDateTime checkOutTime) {
        this.actualCheckOut = checkOutTime;
        if (reservation != null) {
            reservation.markCheckedOut();
        }
        room.setHousekeepingStatus(HousekeepingStatus.DIRTY);   // must be re-cleaned before next booking
        return bill;
    }

    public Optional<Reservation> getReservation();
    public Room getRoom();
}
public class Bill {
    private final String billId;
    private final Stay stay;
    private final List<LineItem> lineItems = new ArrayList<>();

    public Bill(String billId, Stay stay) { /* ... */ }

    public void addLineItem(LineItem item) {
        lineItems.add(item);
    }

    public Money getTotal() {
        return lineItems.stream()
                .map(LineItem::getAmount)
                .reduce(Money.ZERO, Money::add);
    }
}

public class LineItem {
    private final String description;
    private final Money amount;
    private final ChargeKind kind;

    public LineItem(String description, Money amount, ChargeKind kind) { /* ... */ }
    public Money getAmount() { return amount; }
}

Extensibility & Edge Cases

  • Extending a stay by a day. Extending [checkIn, checkOut) to [checkIn, checkOut+1) is not automatically safe just because the guest is already physically in the room today — the same Room still has to pass the overlap check for the additional night against every other Reservation/Stay before the extension is confirmed. A naive implementation that skips the re-check because "the guest is already here" is exactly how a room ends up promised to two different parties on the extended night.
  • Overbooking policy. Hotels commonly overbook a small percentage of rooms deliberately, betting that some fraction of Reservations will resolve to NoShow. This is a conscious business trade-off — the same idea as airline overbooking — not a bug; it has to be modeled as an explicit policy (a configurable overbooking factor Admin sets per RoomType) rather than an accident of a loose availability check.
  • Group bookings. A single Reservation can hold multiple Rooms for the same date range under one guest's name (a family or a conference block). The hold has to be all-or-nothing: if even one of the requested Rooms fails its overlap check, the whole Reservation attempt fails rather than partially succeeding and leaving the guest with three rooms booked and a fourth missing.
  • Loyalty-tier upgrades. A high-tier loyalty guest checking in might be reassigned to a better Room than the one their Reservation named (a Standard reservation upgraded to a Deluxe room at check-in, subject to availability). This is a change to which Room a Stay references at check-in time, not a change to the original Reservation's promised RoomType — the Reservation still recorded what was booked; the Stay records what was actually given.

Follow-up Interview Questions

State the date-range-overlap check precisely, and justify why that formula is correct.
Model each range as half-open: [start, end), where start is included and end is excluded — exactly like checkIn/checkOut, since a guest can check out the same morning another guest checks into that room. Two ranges [startA, endA) and [startB, endB) overlap exactly when startA < endB && startB < endA. Intuitively: the first condition says A starts before B ends, the second says B starts before A ends — both must hold for there to be any shared moment in time. Using closed intervals ([start, end] with <=) would incorrectly flag a checkout on March 14 and a new check-in on March 14 as a conflict, which is wrong and would make every room sit idle for a full day between guests.
Why are Reservation and Stay modeled as two separate classes instead of one?
A Reservation is an advance promise that can end without ever becoming real occupancy — it can be Cancelled, or drift into NoShow if the guest never arrives. A Stay is the realized event: it's created only at check-in, references the actual Room, and tracks actual (not promised) check-in/check-out timestamps. Merging them into one class forces every NoShow to carry Stay-shaped fields that were never populated, and forces every walk-in guest (who checks in with no prior booking) to fabricate a Reservation that was never actually reserved. Keeping them separate, with Stay holding an optional 0..1 reference to the Reservation that produced it, lets both edge cases — NoShow and walk-in — fall out naturally instead of needing special-cased nulls scattered through one overloaded class.
Walk through exactly how the housekeeping-status invariant stops a dirty room from being rebooked.
The moment Stay.close() runs at checkout, it sets the Room's HousekeepingStatus to Dirty as part of the same operation — not as a separate step that could be skipped. The availability check that every new Reservation and check-in relies on isn't just an interval-overlap test against other bookings; it also requires the Room's HousekeepingStatus to be Clean (or Ready) before the room is offered as a candidate at all. Housekeeping Staff explicitly transition the Room from Dirty through Being-Cleaned back to Clean; only that explicit transition re-admits the Room to the bookable pool. Skipping this check — treating "no date overlap" as sufficient for availability — is exactly the bug that would let a guest be handed a room the previous occupant just left.
How would you support extending an existing stay by one more night without double-booking the next guest?
Treat the extension exactly like a brand-new availability request, scoped to the same Room: build the additional-night DateRange (the current checkOut date to checkOut+1) and run the identical overlaps() check against every other CONFIRMED/CHECKED_IN Reservation and Stay for that Room. If it passes, extend the current Stay's expected checkout (and, if a Reservation still backs it, extend or replace the Reservation's date range) and update the Bill's projected room charges. If it fails — because the room is already promised to someone checking in that day — the extension is rejected and the guest is offered a room change or told the hotel can't accommodate the extra night. The key point: being physically present today grants no special claim on tomorrow; tomorrow still has to clear the same overlap check as any other request.
Why model RoomType as a persisted class with a rate field instead of a hardcoded enum, given that Standard/Deluxe/Suite is a fixed, small set of categories?
The category names are stable, but the nightly rate attached to each one is not — seasonal pricing, promotions, and per-property rate differences mean Admin needs to edit RoomType data constantly without a code deploy. A hardcoded enum works fine for something like HousekeepingStatus, which really is a closed, non-priced set of states, but baking a price into an enum constant means every rate change is a code change. This is the mirror image of the Parking Lot page's SpotType decision: there, a class-per-type would have been over-engineering because the types share identical behavior; here, an enum would under-engineer because the types carry genuinely mutable business data.
Two guests hit "confirm" on the last available Standard room for the same dates at nearly the same instant. How do you stop both from succeeding?
The overlap check itself has to run against a consistent, locked view of that room's existing bookings, not a read-then-write race in application code. In practice this means an atomic, conditional insert at the persistence layer — e.g., a database constraint or transaction that only commits a new Reservation row for a room/date-range if no committed row for that same room overlaps it, checked and inserted in one atomic operation (a unique constraint on room+date-range in some schemas, or an explicit row lock on the room during the check-and-insert in others). Whichever request commits first wins; the second either fails the constraint and is told the room is gone, or is routed to search availability again.
What's the trade-off with a hotel's overbooking policy, and how would you model it?
Overbooking trades a small, controlled risk (occasionally having more Confirmed reservations than physical rooms of a type, requiring a "walk" to another property or an upgrade) for higher expected occupancy, since some fraction of Reservations reliably resolve to NoShow. Model it as an explicit, Admin-configurable overbooking factor per RoomType/date — e.g., allow up to 105% of physical capacity to be Confirmed — rather than simply loosening the overlap check. The overlap check itself stays exactly as strict as before (no Room is ever double-assigned to two Stays); overbooking operates one level up, at the "how many Reservations may exist for N physical rooms" capacity-planning layer.
How does a group booking of three rooms stay consistent if one of the three rooms turns out to be unavailable?
The hold has to be all-or-nothing: run the overlap check for every requested Room up front, and only create the single Reservation (with all three room references) if every one of them clears the check. If any one fails, the whole attempt is rejected rather than partially committed — the API shouldn't leave two Rooms held and a third missing, since that produces a Reservation object in an inconsistent state and a guest who thinks they have all three rooms. This is naturally implemented as one transaction spanning all requested rooms' availability checks and the Reservation insert.
A loyalty member's Reservation was for a Standard room, but at check-in the front desk wants to give them a free upgrade to a Deluxe room. What changes, and what stays the same?
The Reservation itself is untouched — it still records that a Standard room was booked and at what rate, which matters for billing and reporting. What changes is which physical Room the resulting Stay references: at check-in, Front-Desk Staff assign a Deluxe Room to the Stay instead of a Standard one (after confirming that Deluxe room clears its own overlap check for the stay's dates). The Bill can then apply the originally-quoted Standard rate rather than the Deluxe rate, since the upgrade is a business decision, not a change to what the guest agreed to pay. This is exactly why Stay carries its own Room reference rather than simply delegating to whatever Room the Reservation named.

Key Takeaways

Key Takeaways
  • The interval-overlap check — startA < endB && startB < endA on half-open [checkIn, checkOut) ranges — is the correctness core of this whole problem, and the same shape reappears anywhere a resource is booked ahead of time.
  • Reservation (future promise) and Stay (realized occupancy) are deliberately two classes, linked by an optional 0..1 reference, so NoShow reservations and walk-in stays don't force awkward nulls into a single overloaded class.
  • A Room's availability is derived from overlapping Reservations/Stays, not stored as a redundant flag — but HousekeepingStatus is stored explicitly, because cleanliness genuinely doesn't derive from anything else.
  • The Dirty-until-cleaned invariant at checkout is what prevents a room from being rebooked before housekeeping actually gets to it — a real requirement, not an incidental detail.
  • RoomType is a class with a mutable rate, not a hardcoded enum, because price — unlike a fixed physical category — is exactly the kind of data Admin needs to change without a deploy.
