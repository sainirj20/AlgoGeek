OBJECT ORIENTED DESIGN

Design a Parking Lot

The parking-lot problem is the "hello world" of object-oriented design interviews — almost every candidate has seen it, which means the bar is really "did you go beyond the obvious class list?" It packs in a physical-capacity constraint (lot full), a small state machine (spot free/occupied), a tiered pricing rule, multiple actors sharing overlapping use cases, and — if you push on it — a genuinely interesting concurrency race and a legitimate debate about whether five parking-spot subclasses are good design or textbook over-engineering. Treat it as a template for any "physical resource with capacity, tiers, and a ticket/session lifecycle" question — the same shape reappears in stadium seating, EV-charging networks, and coworking-desk booking systems.

Problem Statement

Design a multi-floor parking lot that supports several types of parking spots and several types of vehicles, issues a ticket when a vehicle enters, and charges a tiered hourly fee when it leaves. A customer drives in at one of several entry points, takes a ticket from an entrance panel, parks in whatever spot type fits their vehicle, and eventually pays — either at an exit panel (cash or card), through a parking attendant acting on their behalf, or in advance at a per-floor customer info portal so nothing is owed on the way out. The lot has a hard capacity: once every spot of every relevant type is occupied, new vehicles must be turned away at the door rather than let in to search for a spot that doesn't exist. Interviewers use this problem to see whether you separate the *type* of a thing (a spot type, a vehicle type) from its *instance* (this specific spot, this specific ticket), whether you can design a believable multi-tier pricing rule, and whether you'll proactively raise the concurrency and design-critique questions below rather than waiting to be asked.

Requirements

  1. The lot spans multiple floors.
  2. The lot has multiple entry and exit points.
  3. A customer takes a ticket at an entry point and pays at an exit point — either at an automated exit panel or through a parking attendant acting on the customer's behalf.
  4. Payment is accepted as cash or credit card, or in advance at a per-floor Customer Info Portal; a customer who pays there owes nothing further at the exit panel.
  5. Once the lot is at capacity, further entry must be rejected: the entrance panel shows "Lot Full," and the same status is reflected on the ground-floor display board.
  6. Each floor offers several parking-spot types: Compact, Large, Handicapped, Motorcycle, and Electric.
  7. Electric spots have a dedicated charging panel that both authorizes payment and controls charging for the session.
  8. The lot must support several vehicle types: Car, Truck, Van, Motorcycle, and Electric.
  9. Each floor has a display board showing the count of free spots per type; the same information is duplicated on a board near the entrance/ground floor.
  10. Pricing is tiered by hour: $4 for the first hour, $3.50/hour for hours 2–3, and $2.50/hour for every hour after that.

Actors & Use Cases

Four actors touch the system, and — same litmus test as any OOD problem — each one initiates at least one use case none of the others can:

  • Admin — configures the lot itself: floors, spots, panels, attendants, and the pricing table. Never touches a single customer's ticket.
  • Customer — takes a ticket, parks, and pays (cash, card, or in advance at a Customer Info Portal). The only actor who actually owns a vehicle in the scenario.
  • Parking Attendant — a human who can act on a customer's behalf: taking a ticket for them, scanning a ticket at exit, and specifically taking cash payments a fully automated panel can't accept unattended.
  • System — not a person; the background/embedded component that scans tickets at automated panels, drives every display board, and assigns/frees spots as vehicles enter and leave. Modeling it as its own actor keeps "who initiated this" honest: a spot doesn't free itself, the System does it in response to an exit event.

Primary use cases: Add/Remove/Edit Parking Floor, Add/Remove/Edit Parking Spot, Add/Remove Parking Attendant, Take Ticket, Scan Ticket, Pay by Card, Pay by Cash, and Add/Modify Parking Rate.

Class Diagram

Figure 2. ParkingLot (Singleton) —filled diamond→ ParkingFloor —filled diamond→ ParkingSpot (composition: destroy the floor, its spots go with it). ParkingSpot depends on a SpotType enum rather than five subclasses. Vehicle is abstract with five hollow-triangle subclasses. ParkingTicket associates with both ParkingSpot and Vehicle.

The five-subclass debate. Plenty of reference solutions to this exact problem model CompactSpot, LargeSpot, HandicappedSpot, MotorcycleSpot, and ElectricSpot as five subclasses of an abstract ParkingSpot. This diagram deliberately does not do that, and it's worth saying why out loud in an interview rather than silently picking one: subclassing buys you something when subtypes have different behavior (different methods, or the same method with genuinely different logic). Here, all five spot types do exactly the same thing — hold at most one vehicle and answer "am I free?" — and differ only in a piece of data (which category they belong to, which in turn constrains which vehicles fit). That's the textbook signal to favor composition/data over inheritance: a single ParkingSpot class with a spotType: SpotType field. The practical payoff shows up the moment requirements change — adding a sixth spot type ("Oversized") is a one-line enum addition and a config change, not a new class, a new factory branch, and a new file to review. The one place the classic 5-subclass version still has an argument: if a *future* requirement genuinely needs per-type behavior (e.g., only ElectricSpot exposes a startCharging() method), that's the moment inheritance (or a small strategy object attached to the spot) earns its keep — the point isn't "inheritance is always wrong," it's "don't pay for a hierarchy your requirements haven't asked for yet." Vehicle's five subclasses are treated differently in this same diagram precisely because they're not behaviorally identical — each overrides allowedSpotTypes() with a genuinely different answer (a Motorcycle fits in a Motorcycle spot; a Truck typically needs Large).

Design Patterns Applied

Name the pattern and the forcing function together, not just the name:

  • Singleton (ParkingLot) — there is, physically, exactly one lot: one building, one capacity ceiling, one set of floors that every entrance panel, exit panel, and portal must agree on. Unlike the classic Singleton anti-pattern (a logger or config object turned global purely for programmer convenience), this is one of the few defensible real-world uses: the object cardinality in code matches the object cardinality in the physical world. The caveat still worth stating out loud: guard it behind dependency injection (singleton-scoped in your container, or injected in tests as a fake) rather than sprinkling static ParkingLot.getInstance() calls through business logic, so tests can substitute a lot with 3 spots instead of 3,000.
  • Strategy — two independent places this fits: (1) fee calculation — ParkingRate's tiered-hour logic today, a flat monthly-subscriber rate tomorrow, both implementing the same "compute fee for this ticket" contract; and (2) payment method — CashPayment vs. CardPayment implementing a common Payment behavior so ExitPanel never branches on if (method == CASH).
  • Observer (display boards reacting to spot-status changes) — every ParkingDisplayBoard on a floor (and the ground-floor board) subscribes to that floor's spot-status events. When any ParkingSpot flips free↔occupied, ParkingFloor publishes the change and every subscribed board updates — not just "the" board, because a floor and the ground level can each have more than one. This is a genuinely easy requirement to miss: a naive implementation updates a single board reference directly from the assignment code and quietly breaks the moment a second board is added to the same floor.
  • Factory (creating the right Vehicle subtype, and constructing ParkingSpot instances) — a VehicleFactory keeps entry-panel and admin code from being littered with instanceof/switch logic when a new vehicle type shows up. It's also a second, quieter argument for the composition-over-inheritance choice above: ParkingSpot's "factory" is trivially just new ParkingSpot(id, spotType, floor) — one constructor, one enum argument — versus a five-branch factory method the 5-subclass version would need.

public class ParkingRate {
    private static final BigDecimal FIRST_HOUR_RATE      = new BigDecimal("4.00");
    private static final BigDecimal HOURS_TWO_THREE_RATE  = new BigDecimal("3.50");
    private static final BigDecimal ADDITIONAL_HOUR_RATE  = new BigDecimal("2.50");

    // Tiered: $4 for hour 1, $3.50/hr for hours 2-3, $2.50/hr for every hour after.
    public BigDecimal computeFee(Duration parkedDuration) {
        long minutes = parkedDuration.toMinutes();
        long hours = (long) Math.ceil(minutes / 60.0);   // any partial hour rounds up
        if (hours <= 0) {
            hours = 1;
        }

        BigDecimal fee = BigDecimal.ZERO;
        long remaining = hours;

        long firstHour = Math.min(1, remaining);
        fee = fee.add(FIRST_HOUR_RATE.multiply(BigDecimal.valueOf(firstHour)));
        remaining -= firstHour;

        long hoursTwoThree = Math.min(2, remaining);
        fee = fee.add(HOURS_TWO_THREE_RATE.multiply(BigDecimal.valueOf(hoursTwoThree)));
        remaining -= hoursTwoThree;

        if (remaining > 0) {
            fee = fee.add(ADDITIONAL_HOUR_RATE.multiply(BigDecimal.valueOf(remaining)));
        }
        return fee;
    }
}
Worked example A vehicle parked for 5 hours: hour 1 = $4.00, hours 2–3 = 2 × $3.50 = $7.00, hours 4–5 = 2 × $2.50 = $5.00 → total = $16.00. A vehicle parked for 45 minutes rounds up to 1 billed hour → $4.00.

Extensibility & Edge Cases

  • Reserved / pre-booked spots. Introduce a ReservedSpot concept (or a reservedFor: Optional<memberId> field plus a reservation window) that findNearestFreeSpot must exclude for anyone but the reserving customer during that window, and that Admin can configure per spot or per floor.
  • The last-spot race condition. Two entrance panels, at two different physical entrances, both see "1 Compact spot free" and both try to assign the same spot to two different cars at nearly the same instant. A naive "read status, decide, write status" is a textbook lost-update bug. Fix it the same way any last-resource race gets fixed: an atomic, conditional claim at the data layer — e.g. UPDATE parking_spot SET occupied=true, vehicle_id=? WHERE id=? AND occupied=false, checking that exactly one row changed — which is functionally a compare-and-set on ParkingSpot.occupied. Whichever request's update lands first wins; the loser is told the spot is gone and is routed to the next-nearest free spot (or told the type is full) instead of silently double-booking one physical space.
  • Lost tickets. An attendant needs an override path: charge a configurable "lost ticket" maximum-stay fee (protecting against someone claiming a 10-minute stay after 3 days), verify the vehicle's plate against the ParkingSpot it's actually parked in, and issue a replacement/closing ticket rather than leaving the spot permanently marked occupied.
  • A monthly-subscriber pricing tier. Because fee calculation is already a Strategy (ParkingRate), adding SubscriberRate (flat monthly fee, no per-visit charge, maybe a cap on hours/day) means implementing one new class against the same interface — ParkingTicket and ExitPanel don't change at all, they just get handed a different ParkingRate implementation based on the customer's account type.

Follow-up Interview Questions

Why does this design use a single ParkingSpot class with a SpotType enum instead of five subclasses (CompactSpot, LargeSpot, etc.), and isn't that just what everyone else does?
Plenty of reference solutions do use five subclasses, but subclassing only pays for itself when subtypes have genuinely different behavior. Here all five spot types share identical behavior (hold at most one vehicle, answer isFree()) and differ only in a data field. A single class with a spotType enum captures that accurately, makes adding a sixth type a one-line change instead of a new class + factory branch, and avoids five near-empty classes whose only content is a type tag. If a future requirement adds real per-type behavior (e.g. only Electric spots expose startCharging()), that's the moment to introduce inheritance or attach a small strategy object — the point is deferring the hierarchy until behavior actually diverges, not banning inheritance outright.
Two cars pull up to two different entrances at the same moment, and there's exactly one Compact spot left. How do you stop both entrance panels from assigning it?
You cannot safely do "read ParkingSpot.occupied, decide it's free, then write occupied=true" in application code, because both requests can read false before either writes true. The fix is an atomic, conditional update at the persistence layer — a compare-and-set, e.g. an UPDATE with a WHERE occupied=false clause, checking that exactly one row was affected — or equivalently a row-level lock or optimistic version column on that spot. One request's update succeeds; the other's affects zero rows, and that entrance panel falls back to the next-nearest free spot of the same type, or reports "no spots of this type" if none remain.
Walk through exactly how the Observer pattern makes every display board on a floor update when one spot's status changes.
Each ParkingDisplayBoard on a floor (there can be more than one, plus a duplicate at ground level) subscribes to that floor as an observer. When any ParkingSpot on the floor transitions free↔occupied, ParkingSpot notifies ParkingFloor, which recomputes its per-type free counts and calls update(freeCounts) on every subscribed board — not a single hardcoded board reference. This is exactly the requirement naive implementations miss: if the assignment code pushes the new count directly to "the" display board object instead of publishing an event, it silently breaks the day a second board is bolted onto the same floor or a ground-floor summary board is added.
How would you extend this design to a chain of parking lots across a city — e.g., "find me the nearest lot with a free Compact spot"?
Break the single-ParkingLot Singleton assumption at the city level: each physical lot keeps its own ParkingLot instance (still a valid Singleton per-process/per-facility) owning its own floors and spots, while a separate city-level service (a LotLocator or LotRegistry) holds a lightweight summary per lot — location, current free-spot counts by type, published periodically or on change via the same Observer mechanism each lot already uses internally. A "nearest lot with a free spot" query becomes a geo-filter (candidate lots within radius X) intersected with a real-time free-count filter, served from that summary index rather than querying every lot's live database on every request — the same pattern as a store-locator with live inventory.
Why does an Electric vehicle need its own ElectricPanel instead of paying at the same ExitPanel as everyone else?
An Electric spot's "session" isn't just parking time — it's parking time plus a charging session with its own start/stop and its own rate structure (often priced by kWh delivered, not just hours parked). ElectricPanel exists to authorize payment for that combined session and to control the physical charger, which a generic ExitPanel has no reason to know about. It's a good example of a panel that's a specialization by concern, not by vehicle type — a plug-in hybrid parked in a Compact spot still pays at the ordinary ExitPanel.
What happens if a customer loses their ticket?
The attendant flow needs an explicit override: verify the vehicle's plate matches the Vehicle recorded against the ParkingSpot it's actually parked in, charge a configurable maximum-stay "lost ticket" fee (since the real entry time can't be trusted), and issue a closing record so the spot is correctly freed. Without this path, a lost ticket either strands the spot as permanently occupied or lets someone claim an implausibly short stay.
Why does Strategy fit the tiered-fee calculation better than a chain of if/else statements inside ExitPanel?
Tiered pricing is one policy among several ExitPanel might need to apply (hourly, monthly-subscriber, event/flat-rate, promotional). If the tiers are hardcoded as if/else in ExitPanel, every new pricing scheme means editing and re-testing that class. Making ParkingRate an interface with computeFee(duration) as its single contract lets ExitPanel stay unchanged while Admin swaps in a different ParkingRate implementation per lot, per customer tier, or per promotion — new pricing policy, zero changes to the code that calls it.
Why is spot-assignment logic (findNearestFreeSpot) placed on ParkingFloor rather than directly on ParkingLot?
"Nearest" is inherently a per-floor concept — a spot on floor 3 isn't a meaningful candidate for "nearest" if the vehicle just entered on floor 1. Keeping the search scoped to ParkingFloor keeps the lookup cheap (search one floor's spots, not every spot in the building) and keeps ParkingLot's job at the right altitude: decide which floor(s) to even ask, aggregate capacity, and coordinate entry/exit, rather than iterating every spot in a multi-floor structure itself.
How would you add a monthly-subscriber pricing tier without breaking existing hourly customers?
Because ParkingRate is already a Strategy, add a new SubscriberRate implementation (flat monthly fee already paid, computeFee returns zero or a small per-visit surcharge, possibly capped daily hours) and select which ParkingRate a given ParkingTicket uses based on the customer's account type at ticket-issue time. Existing hourly customers keep using the tiered ParkingRate untouched; nothing about ParkingTicket, ExitPanel, or the fee-computation contract changes.
The requirements say a customer can pay in advance at a per-floor Customer Info Portal. What has to change at the exit panel to make that safe?
ParkingTicket needs a paid flag (and ideally the amount/timestamp of the advance payment) set by CustomerInfoPortal at pre-pay time. ExitPanel must check isPaid() before computing or requesting any fee — if true, it skips straight to freeing the spot and opening the gate. The subtlety worth calling out: if the vehicle overstays past what the advance payment covered (e.g., paid for 2 hours but leaves after 4), ExitPanel still needs to compute and collect the difference rather than assuming "paid" means "paid in full forever."

Key Takeaways

Key Takeaways
  • Model ParkingSpot as one class plus a SpotType enum, not five near-identical subclasses — and say why: no behavioral divergence between the five types yet.
  • ParkingLot is a legitimate Singleton because the software's object cardinality matches physical reality (one building), not because Singleton is a reflex.
  • The last-free-spot race condition is the concurrency question this problem is really testing; answer with an atomic conditional update, not "add a lock" hand-waved away.
  • Display boards updating via Observer is a real, easily-missed requirement — a floor (and the ground floor) can have more than one board.
  • Tiered pricing as a Strategy (ParkingRate) is what lets a monthly-subscriber tier, a promotion, or a rate change land without touching ExitPanel or ParkingTicket.
