OBJECT ORIENTED DESIGN

Design a Library Management System

A Library Management System (LMS) is one of the most frequently asked object-oriented design (OOD) interview questions, and for good reason: it is small enough to fully design on a whiteboard in 45 minutes, yet it packs in almost every OOD concept an interviewer wants to probe — search/indexing, a one-to-many relationship between a "title" and its physical copies, state machines (a book item's status), time-bound business rules (checkout limits, due dates, fines), a waiting-list/notification workflow (reservations), and a couple of natural actor hierarchies (Member vs. Librarian). Treat it as a template: the same shape of problem (catalog + inventory + borrowing + notifications) reappears in questions about parking lots, hotel booking, and equipment rental systems.

Problem Statement

Design a Library Management System that lets members search a catalog of books, check out and return physical copies, and reserve titles that are currently unavailable. A library owns many titles (e.g., "Clean Code"), and each title can have several physical copies sitting on different shelves ("racks"). The system has to track each copy independently — one copy of "Clean Code" might be checked out while another sits on the shelf — which is precisely the "one title, many physical instances" wrinkle that makes this problem a good interview signal: candidates who model Book and BookItem as the same class tend to get stuck later when asked "how do you know which specific copy Member X is holding?" The system also has to enforce lending policy (how many books, for how long), charge fines for lateness, let members queue up for a copy that is not currently available, and notify people when something they care about changes (a reservation frees up, a loan is overdue).

Requirements

  1. Members can search the catalog by title, author, subject, or publish date.
  2. Every book (title) has a unique book ID, and every physical copy is assigned a rack location so a librarian can physically find it on the shelf.
  3. A single title can have multiple physical copies; each copy is modeled as a distinct "book item" with its own barcode and its own status (available, loaned, reserved, lost) — two copies of the same title can be in different states at the same time.
  4. The system can answer "who currently holds this book item?" and, conversely, "what does this member currently have checked out?"
  5. There is a cap on how many books a member can have checked out at once (5), and a cap on how long they can keep a book before it is overdue (10 days).
  6. Members are charged an overdue fine for books returned after the due date.
  7. Members can reserve a book that is currently unavailable (all copies loaned out), and are notified automatically when a copy becomes free.
  8. Members are also notified when a book they are holding is overdue (or approaching its due date).
  9. Both book items and library membership cards can be scanned via barcode, since checkout/return typically happen at a physical desk or self-checkout kiosk.

Actors & Use Cases

Three actors interact with the system, each with a clearly different motive — a good check when you're deciding "is this really a separate actor, or just a variation of an existing one?" is to ask whether the actor initiates use cases the others cannot:

  • Librarian — staff member who manages the catalog (add/remove/edit Book and BookItem records) and membership (register/cancel a Member), and who can perform checkout, reservation, and return on behalf of a member at the front desk.
  • Member — the borrower. Searches the catalog, checks out, reserves, renews, and returns books directly (e.g., via self-checkout kiosk or the online portal).
  • System — not a human; represents the scheduled/background component that sends notifications for overdue items and for reservations that have just become available. Modeling it as its own actor makes it explicit in the diagram that some use cases are system-initiated rather than user-initiated, which matters later when you design a cron job / event listener rather than a REST endpoint for it.

Primary use cases: Search Catalog, Add/Remove/Edit Book, Register/Cancel Membership, Check-out Book, Reserve Book, Renew Book, and Return Book, plus two system-initiated use cases, Notify Overdue Books and Notify Reservation Available.

Use-case diagram: the Librarian and Member drive most use cases directly (Librarian can also act "on behalf of" a member at the desk); the System actor owns the two notification use cases.

Class Diagram

UML class diagram: Member and Librarian both inherit from the abstract Account (hollow-triangle arrows). Catalog realizes the Search interface (dashed hollow-triangle). Library aggregates Book and Rack (open-diamond — a Book/Rack row can conceptually be re-parented, e.g. during a branch merge, without being destroyed alongside the Library instance, so aggregation fits better than composition). Book–BookItem, BookItem–BookLending, and BookItem–BookReservation are plain associations with multiplicities shown at each end.

Reading the diagram left to right: Catalog depends on nothing but the Search interface it implements, which is what lets you swap in a different indexing/search backend (e.g., an Elasticsearch-backed implementation) without touching Library. Library is the aggregate root that owns the collection of Book and Rack instances. Each Book fans out into one or more BookItem copies, and each BookItem is the pivot around which the transactional classes — BookLending (0 or 1 active loan) and BookReservation (0 or many queued reservations) — revolve. On the right, Account is deliberately abstract: it holds only what every logged-in identity needs (id, name, status, a LibraryCard), while Member and Librarian each add the state and behavior specific to their role.

Design Patterns Applied

A strong candidate doesn't just name-drop patterns — they explain the forcing function that makes each one the right tool here:

  • Singleton (Library, Catalog) — there should be exactly one in-memory "brain" for the library and one search index per running instance; a private constructor plus a static getInstance() prevents accidentally standing up a second, out-of-sync copy of the catalog index. Caveat worth saying out loud: in a horizontally-scaled deployment, "one instance" really means "one logical instance per process," enforced by your DI container's singleton scope (or, if state must be globally unique, a leader-election/shared-cache layer) rather than a literal static field.
  • Observer (BookReservation notifying waiting members) — BookReservation is effectively the subject and each waiting Member is an observer. When a BookItem transitions LOANED → AVAILABLE on return, the system walks that Book's reservation queue (FIFO) and notifies the next Member. This decouples "a copy became available" from "who cares, and how they're told" — email, SMS, and push can all subscribe to the same event without BookItem or BookLending knowing anything about notification channels.
  • Strategy / polymorphism for Account types — Member and Librarian both extend Account but behave differently at the same conceptual moment (e.g., "what can this account do at the front desk?"). Overriding behavior per subclass avoids if (account instanceof Librarian) branches scattered through LibraryController; adding a third role later (say, a branch Manager) is one new subclass, not a hunt-and-patch across the codebase — the open/closed principle paying for itself.
  • Factory (creating BookItem / Account subtypes) — a factory method centralizes creation of, e.g., a circulating BookItem vs. a reference-only (non-circulating) copy, or a Member vs. Librarian Account, so type-specific defaulting/validation (reference copies never get a due date; Librarians always get elevated permissions) lives in one place instead of being duplicated at every call site that does new.

Code Skeleton

The following is a design skeleton, not a fully executable program — method bodies, persistence, concurrency control, and the actual notification transport (email/SMS/push) are assumed and discussed in prose rather than implemented inline.

Extensibility & Edge Cases

  • Race on the last copy. Two members hit checkout (or checkout-vs-reserve) on the same last-available BookItem at the same instant. A naive "read status, then write status" in application code is a classic lost-update bug. The fix is a concurrency-safe conditional update at the data layer — e.g. UPDATE book_item SET status='LOANED', member_id=? WHERE barcode=? AND status='AVAILABLE' and checking that exactly one row was affected — which is functionally a compare-and-set on BookItem.status. Whoever's update lands first wins; the loser's request fails fast and the system offers them a reservation instead.
  • E-books / digital lending. Introduce a shared LendableItem abstraction (or a DigitalBookItem sibling of BookItem) that swaps the physical concerns (Rack, barcode) for a license/seat count and a DRM token. Checkout becomes "atomically decrement available license count" instead of "assign one physical copy to one member," and there's no physical return step — the loan self-expires when a lease timer runs out, so digital lending likely gets its own lightweight DigitalLending record (with an expiresAt) rather than reusing BookLending's "mark returned" workflow verbatim.
  • Fines that scale with lateness. A flat per-day rate is a fine starting point but rarely matches real policy. Model fine calculation as its own pluggable strategy (e.g., a FineCalculationStrategy) so you can express tiers — a flat rate for the first week, a steeper rate after that, capped at the book's replacement cost for extreme lateness — and so that policy changes (holiday closures excluded from the day count, membership-tier discounts) touch only the strategy, not BookLending or Fine's core structure.

Follow-up Interview Questions

What happens if two members try to reserve or check out the last copy of a book at the exact same time?
This is the classic BookItem race condition. You cannot safely do "read status in application code, decide, then write status" because both requests can read AVAILABLE before either writes. The fix is a concurrency-safe conditional update at the persistence layer — a compare-and-set on BookItem.status (only update if it still equals the expected prior status, and check the affected-row count), or equivalently a row-level lock / optimistic version column. One request wins atomically; the other is told the item is no longer available and is offered a reservation.
Catalog needs to search by four different, independent criteria (title, author, subject, publish date). Why might that call for a Strategy or Composite approach instead of one big method with a switch statement?
Each criterion is really an independent search strategy that implements the same Search contract; Catalog can hold a strategy per criterion and pick (or combine) them at runtime rather than branching internally. A Composite layer on top lets you express compound queries — "title contains X AND publish date after Y" — by combining strategy results (intersection/union) instead of writing a new bespoke method for every combination of filters a product manager asks for next quarter.
How would you shard or scale this design for a multi-branch library system with shared membership?
Partition Catalog/BookItem/Rack data by branch (each branch's inventory and rack layout is local), while keeping Account/Member/LibraryCard in a shared, branch-agnostic identity store so a card scanned at any branch resolves the same member. Route checkout/return/reservation writes to the owning branch's shard using a branch key; replicate the read-heavy search path (Catalog lookups) more broadly, possibly via a cross-branch search index, since "is this title available anywhere nearby" is a common query that shouldn't require fanning out to every branch's database on every request.
What's the difference between aggregation and composition here, using two concrete classes from this design?
Library o-- Book/Rack (open diamond) is aggregation: a Book or Rack row has meaning and an independent lifecycle outside any one Library instance — it could be reassigned during a branch merge or data migration without being destroyed. Contrast that with Account *-- LibraryCard, which is closer to composition: a LibraryCard has no independent identity or lifecycle outside its owning Account — delete the Account and the card should be deleted/invalidated with it. The rule of thumb: aggregation is "has-a, but could exist without the whole"; composition is "has-a, and dies with the whole."
Why model Account as an abstract base class rather than a single Account class with a "role" enum field?
Member and Librarian carry meaningfully different state and behavior (borrowing state and limits vs. catalog-management privileges); a single flat class with a role field tends to accumulate nullable "only applies to one role" fields and if/switch branches scattered through the controller layer. Splitting them via inheritance keeps each role's data and behavior cohesive, at the modest cost of a bit more class ceremony — a trade worth stating explicitly to the interviewer rather than assuming it's obviously correct.
How do you prevent an unbounded or duplicate reservation queue — e.g., one member reserving the same book five times, or 200 people queued for one title?
Enforce a uniqueness constraint on active (Member, Book) reservation pairs so a repeat request is a no-op rather than a new queue entry. For unbounded queue length, either cap the number of outstanding reservations per title or auto-expire a reservation that goes unclaimed for N days after the member is notified, cascading the notification to the next member in line so a slot never sits idle indefinitely.
Where does concurrency control live for the "5 books max per member" rule when two checkout requests race for the same member?
The same compare-and-set idea applies at the Member level: checking "current active-loan count < 5" and incrementing it must be one atomic operation (a conditional update on Member.activeLoanCount, or a serializable transaction), not a separate read-then-write in application code — otherwise two concurrent requests can both read count=4, both decide they're allowed, and both proceed, leaving the member with 6 active loans.
How would you extend this design to support recalling a book early (e.g., a high-demand textbook needed by another member)?
Add a recall workflow: if a BookReservation exists for a Book whose only copies are on active BookLending, LibraryController can shorten the current holder's dueDate and fire a Notification explaining the new due date. This introduces a new interaction between BookReservation and BookLending beyond "passively wait for a natural return," and is a good example of how the class diagram's associations (BookItem–BookLending, BookItem–BookReservation) support behavior the original requirements didn't explicitly call out.
How would renewing a book work, and what should block it?
BookLending.renew() extends dueDate by another standard loan period, but should be rejected if: another member has an active reservation on that title (fairness — recalls/renewals shouldn't starve the queue), the member has already renewed the maximum allowed number of times, or the member has unpaid fines above a configurable threshold.

Key Takeaways

Key Takeaways
  • Separate the "title" (Book) from the "physical copy" (BookItem) early — nearly every requirement (rack location, status, who's holding it) actually belongs on BookItem, not Book.
  • Model Account as an abstract base with Member/Librarian specializations to keep role-specific state and behavior cohesive instead of branching on a role flag.
  • BookLending and BookReservation are the two transactional join classes that make the whole system dynamic; almost every interesting business rule (limits, fines, notifications, recalls) is enforced through them.
  • Concurrency is not a footnote: the last-copy race condition and the checkout-limit race are the two places interviewers most often probe for a compare-and-set / atomic-update answer.
  • Singleton, Observer, Strategy, and Factory each solve one specific forcing function here (one catalog instance, reservation notifications, role-specific behavior, and centralized object creation) — be ready to explain why, not just recite the names.
