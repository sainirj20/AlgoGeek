OBJECT ORIENTED DESIGN

Design LinkedIn

Every professional-networking platform lives or dies on one modeling decision candidates almost never get asked about directly, yet almost always get wrong: a Connection and a Follow look like the same kind of edge between two people, but they are structurally opposite. A Connection is a single, symmetric fact that must be true (or false) identically no matter which of the two Members you ask — if A is connected to B, B is connected to A, full stop, and there is no such thing as a one-sided Connection. A Follow is a directional edge one Member creates unilaterally about another Member or a Company, with no consent and no promise of reciprocity. Modeling Connection the way Follow legitimately should be modeled — as two directional rows — is the single most common mistake on this problem, and it quietly breaks everything downstream: messaging permissions, feed eligibility, and the "degree of connection" badge all key off getting this one relationship shape right. Treat this page as the canonical template for "pick the correct relationship shape for a symmetric fact vs. a directional one," a distinction that resurfaces anywhere two peers can have a mutual-consent bond (friendship, matchmaking, peer review) alongside a one-way subscription (follow, watch, subscribe).

Problem Statement

Design the core object model for a professional-networking platform. Each Member maintains a Profile — a headline, work-experience entries, education entries, and a list of skills. Members build their network in two structurally different ways: a Connection, a symmetric, mutual-consent relationship formed only after one Member sends a request and the other explicitly accepts it, and a Follow, a one-directional subscription to another Member's or a Company's public updates that requires no consent at all. Members post updates (Post) that surface in the Feed of their Connections and followers, and other Members can Like or Comment on those posts. Members exchange direct Messages with their Connections. Companies maintain a page and post Jobs; Members Apply to a Job using their Profile. Interviewers use this problem to see whether a candidate reaches for two different relationship shapes for two relationships that only look alike on the surface, whether a pending connection request's lifecycle is modeled as a real state machine rather than a loose boolean, and whether the candidate proactively draws the line between "this is still an object model" and "this is now a distributed feed-ranking problem," rather than wandering into sharding and fan-out on a question that is meant to test class design.

Requirements

  1. Every Member has a Profile containing a headline, one or more ExperienceEntry records, one or more EducationEntry records, and a list of skills.
  2. A Member can send a ConnectionRequest to another Member; the recipient must explicitly Accept it before the two become mutually Connected. This must be modeled as symmetric — if A is connected to B, B is connected to A — and that fact must be enforceable and queryable efficiently from either side.
  3. Members can also Follow another Member or a Company one-directionally, with no mutual consent required, which is a distinct relationship from a Connection.
  4. Members can post updates (Post), which appear in the Feed of their Connections and followers; other Members can Like or Comment on a Post.
  5. Members can send direct Messages to their Connections.
  6. Companies can post Jobs, and Members can Apply to a Job with their Profile.
  7. A Member's degree of connection to another Member (1st/2nd/3rd) is a commonly-shown concept worth modeling conceptually — even without implementing full graph traversal, a candidate should be able to describe how it maps to a graph problem over the Connection relationship.

Actors & Use Cases

Three actors touch the system, and while Member dominates the use-case count, each actor initiates at least one use case none of the others can:

  • Member — manages their Profile, sends and accepts Connection Requests, follows other Members or Companies, posts updates, likes/comments, messages their Connections, and applies to Jobs. The only actor who can be on either side of a Connection or a Follow.
  • Company — posts Jobs and manages its company page. A Company can be followed (like a Member can), but it never has Connections — it isn't a peer.
  • System — generates each Member's Feed from their Connections' and followed entities' Posts, computes degree-of-connection on demand, and dispatches notifications. The only actor that never acts on behalf of a single human decision.

Primary use cases: Send/Accept Connection Request, Follow Member/Company, Create Post, Like/Comment on Post, Send Message, Apply to Job (Member), Post Job (Company), and Generate Feed (System).

Figure 1. Use-case diagram: Member initiates six of the eight use cases (everything except posting and reviewing a Job). Company's only exclusive use case is Post a Job — a Company can be followed but never initiates Follow or Connection itself. System's only exclusive use case is Generate Feed: no human actor directly "does" feed assembly, it happens on the Member's behalf.

Why Connection and Follow use different relationship shapes is the single most interview-relevant modeling decision on this page. A Connection is a symmetric fact: it is true or false identically for both Members, so the only correct representation is one record that both sides query, not two directional rows that could independently drift out of sync (imagine A's row saying "connected" while B's row was never written — a bug that simply cannot happen if there is only one row to write). That single record is naturally drawn as an association class attached to a reflexive, undirected association on Member — exactly the UML notation for "a many-to-many relationship between instances of the same class, with its own attributes (connectedAt) and its own identity (connectionId)." A Follow, by contrast, is inherently asymmetric: the follower and the followee are different roles that can never be swapped, so it's correctly modeled as a directional edge with its own arrowhead, and there is no consent step and no mirror-image record to keep in sync. Reaching for the wrong shape in either direction causes real bugs: modeling Connection as two directional Follow-like rows risks split-brain state; modeling Follow as a symmetric join (the way Connection is done) would incorrectly imply the followee also follows back.

Design Patterns Applied

Name the pattern (or modeling principle) and the forcing function together, not just the label:

  • Symmetric relationship vs. directional relationship (not a GoF pattern, but the single most important OOD modeling skill on this page) — Connection is one canonical record queried from both sides; Follow is a directional edge with distinct follower/followee roles. Recognizing which of your two "relationship-shaped" requirements is actually symmetric and which is actually directional, and modeling each accordingly, is the core skill this problem is testing.
  • Observer — notifications react to events rather than being hardcoded into the classes that cause them: a new ConnectionRequest notifies its recipient, a new Comment or Like notifies a Post's author, and a JobApplication status change notifies the applicant. Each of these publishes an event that a notification subscriber picks up, so Post/ConnectionRequest/JobApplication never need to know how notifications are actually delivered (push, email, in-app badge).
  • Composite-ish aggregation (Profile over Experience/Education) — Profile doesn't hardcode "first job, second job, ..." as fixed fields; it holds uniform List<ExperienceEntry> and List<EducationEntry> collections and exposes addExperience()/addEducation(). Any number of entries is handled identically without Profile needing special-cased fields per entry.
  • Strategy (extension idea, not designed in full here) — once a Feed or Job-matching ranking algorithm is introduced, it's a natural candidate for a pluggable RankingStrategy interface so different ranking approaches (recency-only, engagement-weighted, ML-scored) can be swapped without changing FeedService's call sites. This page deliberately stops at "candidates for the feed" per the Problem Statement, so ranking strategy is mentioned only as a hook for where the design would grow, not fleshed out.

Code Skeleton

Fields and method signatures only — not a runnable program. Connection's static factory and ConnectionRequest's guarded transitions are worked out in full, since that lifecycle is exactly what interviewers probe on.

public class Member {
    private final String memberId;
    private String name;
    private String email;
    private final Profile profile;

    public Member(String memberId, String name, String email) {
        this.memberId = memberId;
        this.name = name;
        this.email = email;
        this.profile = new Profile(memberId);
    }

    public String getMemberId() { return memberId; }
    public Profile getProfile() { return profile; }
}
public class Profile {
    private final String memberId;
    private String headline;
    private final List<ExperienceEntry> experienceEntries = new ArrayList<>();
    private final List<EducationEntry> educationEntries = new ArrayList<>();
    private final List<String> skills = new ArrayList<>();

    public Profile(String memberId) { this.memberId = memberId; }

    public void addExperience(ExperienceEntry entry) { experienceEntries.add(entry); }
    public void addEducation(EducationEntry entry) { educationEntries.add(entry); }
    public void addSkill(String skill) { skills.add(skill); }

    public List<ExperienceEntry> getExperienceEntries() { return List.copyOf(experienceEntries); }
    public List<EducationEntry> getEducationEntries() { return List.copyOf(educationEntries); }
}
// A single record represents the (undirected) fact that two Members are connected.
// Always queried by "does a Connection exist that involves this member," never
// by asking "does A's row point at B" and separately "does B's row point at A."
public final class Connection {
    private final String connectionId;
    private final String memberIdA;
    private final String memberIdB;
    private final Instant connectedAt;

    private Connection(String connectionId, String memberIdA, String memberIdB, Instant connectedAt) {
        // Canonical ordering guarantees (A, B) and (B, A) never produce two rows.
        if (memberIdA.compareTo(memberIdB) <= 0) {
            this.memberIdA = memberIdA;
            this.memberIdB = memberIdB;
        } else {
            this.memberIdA = memberIdB;
            this.memberIdB = memberIdA;
        }
        this.connectionId = connectionId;
        this.connectedAt = connectedAt;
    }

    // Static factory is the only way to create a Connection, guaranteeing exactly
    // one canonical record represents both directions of the relationship.
    public static Connection of(String connectionId, String memberIdA, String memberIdB, Instant connectedAt) {
        return new Connection(connectionId, memberIdA, memberIdB, connectedAt);
    }

    public boolean involves(Member member) {
        String id = member.getMemberId();
        return memberIdA.equals(id) || memberIdB.equals(id);
    }

    // Given one side of the symmetric relationship, return the other side.
    public String other(String memberId) {
        if (memberId.equals(memberIdA)) return memberIdB;
        if (memberId.equals(memberIdB)) return memberIdA;
        throw new IllegalArgumentException("member is not part of this connection");
    }
}
// Directional: a Member follows a Member OR a Company, with no consent required
// and no mirror-image record created on the followee's side.
public final class FollowRelationship {
    private final String followId;
    private final String followerId;      // always a Member
    private final String followeeId;      // a Member OR a Company
    private final FolloweeType followeeType;
    private final Instant followedAt;

    public FollowRelationship(String followId, String followerId, String followeeId,
                               FolloweeType followeeType, Instant followedAt) {
        this.followId = followId;
        this.followerId = followerId;
        this.followeeId = followeeId;
        this.followeeType = followeeType;
        this.followedAt = followedAt;
    }

    public String getFollowerId() { return followerId; }
    public String getFolloweeId() { return followeeId; }
    public FolloweeType getFolloweeType() { return followeeType; }
}

public enum FolloweeType { MEMBER, COMPANY }
public class Post {
    private final String postId;
    private final String authorId;
    private String content;
    private final Instant createdAt;
    private final List<Comment> comments = new ArrayList<>();
    private final List<Like> likes = new ArrayList<>();

    public Post(String postId, String authorId, String content, Instant createdAt) {
        this.postId = postId;
        this.authorId = authorId;
        this.content = content;
        this.createdAt = createdAt;
    }

    public void addComment(Comment comment) { comments.add(comment); }
    public void like(String memberId, Instant when) { likes.add(new Like(postId, memberId, when)); }

    public String getAuthorId() { return authorId; }
    public Instant getCreatedAt() { return createdAt; }
    public List<Comment> getComments() { return List.copyOf(comments); }
}
// The pending, one-directional precursor to a symmetric Connection.
public class ConnectionRequest {
    private final String requestId;
    private final String fromMemberId;
    private final String toMemberId;
    private ConnectionRequestStatus status;
    private final Instant sentAt;

    public ConnectionRequest(String requestId, String fromMemberId, String toMemberId, Instant sentAt) {
        this.requestId = requestId;
        this.fromMemberId = fromMemberId;
        this.toMemberId = toMemberId;
        this.status = ConnectionRequestStatus.PENDING;
        this.sentAt = sentAt;
    }

    // Guarded transition: only a PENDING request can ever become a Connection.
    public Connection accept(String connectionId, Instant connectedAt) {
        if (status != ConnectionRequestStatus.PENDING) {
            throw new IllegalStateException("Cannot accept a request in status " + status);
        }
        this.status = ConnectionRequestStatus.ACCEPTED;
        return Connection.of(connectionId, fromMemberId, toMemberId, connectedAt);
    }

    public void decline() {
        if (status != ConnectionRequestStatus.PENDING) {
            throw new IllegalStateException("Cannot decline a request in status " + status);
        }
        this.status = ConnectionRequestStatus.DECLINED;
    }

    public void withdraw() {
        if (status != ConnectionRequestStatus.PENDING) {
            throw new IllegalStateException("Cannot withdraw a request in status " + status);
        }
        this.status = ConnectionRequestStatus.WITHDRAWN;
    }

    public ConnectionRequestStatus getStatus() { return status; }
}

public enum ConnectionRequestStatus { PENDING, ACCEPTED, DECLINED, WITHDRAWN }

Extensibility & Edge Cases

Degree of connection Treat every Connection as an undirected edge in a graph whose nodes are Members. degree(A, B) is the length of the shortest path from A to B in that graph: 1 if a direct Connection edge exists, 2 if there's a path through exactly one mutual Connection, 3 if through two hops, and "3rd+" (or unknown) beyond that — a textbook breadth-first search from A, stopping as soon as B is reached or a hop-limit (e.g. 3) is hit. The object model doesn't need to store degree anywhere; it's a derived, on-demand traversal question, not a stored attribute.
  • Computing degree-of-connection at scale. A live BFS over the full Connection graph is fine for a small network, but at LinkedIn's actual scale a per-request traversal is far too slow — this is exactly where the design has to bridge into an HLD conversation about a precomputed or cached graph index (e.g., materialized 2nd-degree neighbor sets refreshed asynchronously) rather than a live graph walk on every profile view. The OOD-level takeaway is just: it's a graph shortest-path problem over the Connection edges, nothing more exotic.
  • Blocking a Member. Blocking must suppress both Connections and Follows bidirectionally and hide content in both directions — a blocked Member's existing Connection (if any) should stop granting messaging rights and feed visibility, any Follow either party had on the other should stop surfacing content, and neither party should be able to re-initiate a ConnectionRequest or Follow while the block is active. A block is not merely "one more relationship type" layered on top; it's a suppression rule that has to be checked ahead of Connection/Follow/feed logic everywhere those are evaluated.
  • Withdrawing a pending request. A ConnectionRequest that's withdrawn before the recipient accepts or declines it moves to a terminal WITHDRAWN status without ever becoming a Connection or a DECLINED request — distinct from Declined (which implies the recipient actively reviewed and rejected it) for accurate history and re-request cooldown logic.
  • Company pages have Follow but not Connection. Because Connection is a symmetric peer-to-peer relationship and a Company is not a Member, a Company can only ever be a Follow followee, never a Connection party — there's no "mutual consent" concept that makes sense between a person and an organization page.

Follow-up Interview Questions

Why do Connection (symmetric) and Follow (directional) need different data models?
A Connection is a single fact that must be identically true for both Members — there is no such thing as "A is connected to B but B is not connected to A." The only representation that can't drift into an inconsistent state is one canonical record queried from either side (an undirected edge with an association class). A Follow is inherently asymmetric: the follower and followee are different, non-interchangeable roles, formed unilaterally with no consent step and no guarantee the followee follows back. Modeling Connection as two directional rows (the way Follow legitimately is) opens the door to split-brain state where one side's row exists and the other's doesn't; modeling Follow as a single symmetric join (the way Connection is done) would incorrectly imply mutual reciprocity that was never granted.
How should a pending ConnectionRequest's lifecycle be modeled?
As an explicit, closed state machine, not a loose boolean: PENDING is the only state from which a transition is allowed, and it can move to exactly one of ACCEPTED (creates a Connection), DECLINED (recipient reviewed and rejected it), or WITHDRAWN (sender pulled it back before any response). Each of these is terminal — there's no path back to PENDING. Guarding accept()/decline()/withdraw() so they throw if the request isn't currently PENDING prevents bugs like double-accepting a request or accepting one that was already withdrawn out from under the recipient.
How would you compute the degree of connection between two Members, and why is it a graph problem?
Model every Connection as an undirected edge between two Member nodes, then run a breadth-first search from Member A: distance 1 means a direct Connection edge to B, distance 2 means a shared mutual Connection, distance 3 means two hops away, and beyond that the UI typically just shows "3rd+" rather than the exact distance. It's a graph problem specifically because "who is close to whom" is a path-length question over a network of edges, not something derivable from either Member's own fields. At real scale, a live BFS per profile view is too slow, so production systems precompute and cache a bounded-hop neighbor index rather than traversing on every request — the object model only needs to describe the graph and the traversal rule, not the caching architecture.
Where's the line between "this is still an OOD-level object model" and "this is now an HLD-level ranking/fan-out problem" for the Feed?
OOD scope stops at defining which Posts are even candidates for a Member's Feed: the query that joins their Connections and followed entities against recent Posts, and the classes involved in expressing that query (FeedService, ConnectionRepository, FollowRepository, PostRepository). The moment the conversation turns to how those candidates get ordered at scale — personalized ranking scores, fan-out-on-write vs. fan-out-on-read, caching layers, sharding the social graph, handling celebrity accounts with millions of followers — that's a distributed-systems / HLD conversation, not a class-design one. A strong candidate names this boundary explicitly rather than drifting into sharding strategy on a question meant to test object modeling.
Why is Connection implemented as a single canonical record instead of two directional rows, and what does the canonical-ordering trick in the static factory actually buy you?
Two directional rows (an "A→B" row and a "B→A" row) require every write and every consistency check to touch two records atomically, and any partial failure leaves the graph in a state where one Member sees a Connection the other doesn't. Storing memberIdA and memberIdB in a fixed canonical order (e.g., lexicographically smaller id first) inside the private constructor, and only ever constructing a Connection through the static factory, guarantees that "A connected to B" and "B connected to A" always produce identical field values — so a uniqueness constraint on (memberIdA, memberIdB) at the persistence layer trivially prevents ever creating two Connection rows for the same pair, without the application needing to remember to check both orderings itself.
How do you prevent Member A from sending Member B five separate ConnectionRequests?
Before creating a new ConnectionRequest, check for an existing PENDING request between the same (fromMemberId, toMemberId) pair (or its reverse, since B could have already requested A) and for an existing Connection between them — exactly the guard shown in the activity diagram's first decision. In practice this is enforced with a uniqueness constraint scoped to "at most one PENDING request per unordered member pair" at the data layer, so a race between two near-simultaneous send attempts still can't produce two live pending requests.
A Member blocks another Member they're currently Connected to and mid-conversation with. Walk through what has to change.
The existing Connection record itself can either be deleted or flagged inactive, but either way it must immediately stop granting messaging rights and stop the blocked party's Posts from appearing in the blocker's Feed, and vice versa. Any FollowRelationship either party held on the other must stop surfacing content in both directions. The Conversation between them should be prevented from accepting new Messages from the blocked party (existing history is a product decision, not an object-model one). Crucially, the block itself needs to be checked as a gate ahead of Connection/Follow/messaging logic everywhere those are evaluated, rather than only at the moment the block is created, since a new ConnectionRequest or Follow attempt from the blocked party must also be rejected going forward.
Why can a Company be followed but never "connected" to?
Connection is specifically a symmetric, mutual-consent relationship between peers — two Members who both agreed to it. A Company isn't a peer in that sense; it's an organization page that publishes content, and there's no meaningful notion of the Company "consenting" back to a Member the way another Member would. Follow captures exactly what a Company relationship actually is: a one-directional subscription to updates, with no reciprocity implied or required — which is why FollowRelationship's followee is typed to accept either a Member or a Company, while Connection's two sides are always Members.
Why is messaging restricted to Connections rather than open to anyone, and how would you enforce that at the object-model level?
Restricting Messages to Connections is both a product decision (reduce unsolicited messaging/spam) and a natural consequence of Connection already representing "these two people have a mutual, established relationship." At the object-model level, before creating a new Conversation (or allowing a Message into an existing one), check that a Connection exists between the participantIds; if it doesn't, reject the send rather than silently creating a Conversation that shouldn't exist. This mirrors the same pattern as the blocking check — a precondition gate evaluated ahead of the actual write, not baked into Conversation's constructor as an afterthought.

Key Takeaways

Key Takeaways
  • Connection (symmetric, mutual-consent) and Follow (directional, no-consent) are structurally opposite relationships and must use different data shapes — a single canonical record as an association class on an undirected edge for Connection, a directional edge with follower/followee roles for Follow. Confusing the two is the single most common mistake on this problem.
  • ConnectionRequest is a real, guarded state machine (Pending → Accepted/Declined/Withdrawn) — not a boolean — and only the Accepted transition ever produces a Connection.
  • Degree-of-connection is a graph shortest-path (BFS) question over the Connection edges, derived on demand rather than stored, though real scale requires a precomputed/cached index rather than a live traversal.
  • The Feed's object model stops at "which Posts are candidates" — joining Connections and Follows against recent Posts. Ranking, fan-out, and sharding the social graph are HLD-scope, deliberately out of bounds for this page.
  • A Company can be followed but never connected to, because Connection presumes peer-to-peer mutual consent that doesn't apply to an organization page.
